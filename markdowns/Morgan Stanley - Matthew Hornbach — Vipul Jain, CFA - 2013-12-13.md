## **US Interest Rate Strategist** Top 10 Surprises for 2014 

**To wind down the year, we propose 10 events for the liquid US interest rate markets that could catch market participants by surprise. These surprises do not reflect our base case views, but are meant to highlight risks to our views.** 

## **1. UST 2-Year Yields End 2014 > 2.00%** 

A much stronger economic growth trajectory, coupled with an aggressive tapering schedule and less focus on rate guidance, brings rate hike expectations to January 2015. 

## **2. UST 10-Year Yields End 2014 < 2.00%** 

A return to muddle-through growth data and continued disinflation prompt additional easing, including a 5.5% unemployment rate threshold and a 1.5% inflation floor. 

## **3. 5s/10s Curve Breaches All-Time Highs** 

A shift-in-mix of accommodation by the Fed allows longer maturity term premiums to maintain their wider levels while lowering rate expectations and better anchoring them. 

## **4. TIPS Implied Deflation Probabilities Rise** 

The loss of deflation-fighting Fed credibility causes the TIPS market to price in a non-zero probability of 5-yearahead cumulative deflation. 

## **5. 10y20y Real Rates Move About 2.5%** 

Accelerating real growth and inflation prompts the Fed to tighten monetary policy. The Fed begins to sell Treasuries and engineers higher long-dated real term premiums. 

## **6. Swap Spread Curve Goes Fully Negative** 

A cut to the IOER rate and a permanent, unlimited RRP Facility drives fed funds and LIBOR rates lower while Treasury yields stay high, driving swap spreads negative. 

## **7. 30-Year Skew Turns Normal** 

As rates rise, the long-end of the curve is increasingly constrained by long-term growth and short rate prospects. Skews turn normal, reflecting balanced back-end risks. 

## **8. Front End Rates Go Negative** 

SEC MMF reform requiring prime funds to switch to Floating NAV or cut in IOR could push already low frontend rates into negative territory. 

## **9. Mortgage CC OAS Approaches Zero** 

The yield curve flattens, leading investors to short volatility to earn carry, which causes spreads to tighten. 

## **10. Up-in-Coupon and 15s/30s Underperform** 

Rallying rates lead Mel Watt (head of FHFA) to ease credit conditions, driving prepayment speeds higher and thus causing losses to 15s/30s and up-in-coupon trades.

## **1. UST 2-Year Yields End 2014 > 2.00%** 

The first surprise we consider, would be if 2-year Treasury yields ended the year 2014 above 2%. Let us step forward in time to the end of 2014, and look back at this scenario… 

_Now, at the end of 2014, it has become obvious that the consensus has drastically under-estimated the strength of the US economy and the actions of the Federal Reserve. The economy has weathered the 2013 storm of higher real rates and higher mortgage rates better than anyone – including Federal Reserve officials – imagined. We have seen a faster decline in the unemployment rate – driven in part by changes to the emergency unemployment compensation benefits – to well below the 6.5% threshold much earlier than expected – e.g., as early as March 2014. And, now at the end of 2014, we are debating whether rate hikes, not tapering, will begin at one of the upcoming two FOMC meetings._ 

_At the December 2013 FOMC meeting, the Fed announced a larger-than-expected taper of $25 billion – consisting of $15 billion US Treasuries and $10 billion agency MBS – without any change in rate guidance. The steepening of the yield curve leading up to the meeting convinced FOMC participants that their message had sunk in – and no further action was needed. In addition, Chairman Bernanke, having heard the unconfirmed news that Former Bank of Israel Governor Stanley Fischer was in line for the Vice Chairmanship harkened back to the days when Fischer was advising his thesis and decided that forward guidance was on its way out[1] . Bernanke reasoned that if eight years of hard work and admirable achievement on rate guidance was about to come to an end, he might as well try to distance himself from the thresholds as soon as possible._ 

_For her part, once comfortably in the Fed Chair seat, Yellen also decided to concede to the newly appointed Vice Chair’s eminent reputation without much debate. And now that the_ 

_’necessary evil’ that was QE was no longer necessary, Yellen decided to end the program as quickly as possible – with unbridled support from the more hawkish members of the FOMC. Because every FOMC meeting allowed a press conference – a change decided upon at the January 2014 FOMC meeting – Fed Chair Yellen was able to explain why the Committee was truncating bond purchases in such a hurry. The markets quickly realized that her reputation as a policy dove was an inaccurate assessment. The much more accurate description was that Yellen was an aggressive user of policy. And since she reasoned that policy no longer needed to add accommodation, she decided to stop adding accommodation as soon as was practically possible._ 

_With all bond purchases having ended by the April 29-30 FOMC meeting, the market brought forward the timing of the first rate hike from late 2015 to early 2015 –meaning that 2- year yields have begun to move much higher._ _**Exhibit 1** shows how 2-year Treasury yields traded versus the fed funds target rate going into the 2004-06 rate hiking cycle. Given (1) the historical precedent of where the front end of the yield curve was trading in 2003-04, (2) the ‘shell-shock’ of Yellen’s seeming policy bias about-face, (3) the dramatically increased uncertainty over future policy under the new ‘no rate guidance’ regime, and (4) the possibility that rates will have to be hiked faster than in 2004-06, 2-year yields now have risen above 2.0%._

## **2. UST 10-Year Yields End 2014 < 2.00%** 

The second surprise of 2014 would be if 10-year Treasury yields ended the year below 2.0%. This would be quite a surprise indeed. In fact, if 10-year yields ended the year below 3% even, almost every forecaster who contributed to the Bloomberg survey would be surprised to a certain degree (see **Exhibit 2** ). Relative to our bull case for Treasuries, published in our 2014 Global Rates Outlook[2] – in which we suggested 10-year yields could fall to 2.25% – a 10-year yield below 2.0% would surprise even our most bullish aspirations. How realistic is such a scenario? Consider this: the most bearish case for the economy that our US economists could conceive is near the growth rate that the US economy has experienced for the past several years – around 2%Y.[3] While this might otherwise throw cold water on the notion that 10year yields could ever return to their depths below 2.0%, it highlights the optimistic skew embedded into expectations for the economy and opens the door to an aggressive repricing of markets should the economy disappoint. Again, we step forward in time to the end of 2014, and look back, to illustrate how this scenario might play out… 

_Of course, looking back on 2014, we noticed that the move to sub-2.0% 10-year yields did not begin right away. Rather, it began when 10-year yields were not able to move above 3.0% after the Fed decided to delay tapering at the December 2013 FOMC meeting. Unfortunately, that proved to be its last chance to taper.  Instead, the Fed lowered the unemployment rate threshold to 5.5% at its January meeting in an effort to defend the credibility of the threshold. Given the continued decline in the labor force participation rate, due to both underlying trends and the changes to the emergency unemployment compensation benefits, the Fed did not want to be seen as moving the goal posts at the last second._ 

_As the year progressed, realized inflation data remained subdued and the growth data stopped accelerating. While the Fed achieved some success in supporting intermediate breakeven inflation expectations through both lowering the unemployment rate threshold and introducing an inflation floor, the decline in real rates dominated. Net, nominal rates moved substantially lower._

## **3. 5s/10s Curve Breaches All-Time Highs** 

The third surprise of 2014 would be if the 5s/10s Treasury curve breached its all-time high level of 155bp. Two conditions would have been met:(1) the Fed would have shifted the mix of monetary policy accommodation away from LSAPs and toward rate guidance, and (2) the market would have assigned a high degree of credibility to the Fed’s forward rate guidance. 

_The shift-in-mix:_ The 5s/10s curve has traditionally been a reflection of the monetary policy as defined by the fed funds target rate. **Exhibit 4** shows the strong negative relationship between the 5s/10s curve and the fed funds target rate over the past twenty years. In our base case forecasts, we expect the Fed to lower the unemployment threshold to 6.0% along with a $10 billion tapering of Treasuries. For the 5s/10s curve to reach all-time highs, the Fed would need to announce either a lower unemployment rate threshold (5.5%), or an inflation floor of 1.5% / 1.75%, in addition to a cut in the unemployment threshold to 6%. These conditions would lay the groundwork for the 5s/10s curve to steepen to its all-time highs. ## **4. TIPS Implied Deflation Probabilities Rise** 

The fourth surprise of 2014 would be that TIPS implied deflation probabilities rise. Currently, the TIPS market is pricing a very low (zero) probability of cumulative deflation over a 5-year time horizon (see **Exhibit 6** ). 

Plotting a time series of TIPS-implied probabilities of deflation versus headline CPI inflation suggests the correlation between realized CPI and 5-year-ahead deflation probabilities fell after the announcement of the MEP extension and has remained low ever since. The plot also suggests that each FOMC large-scale asset purchase announcement (LSAP) was followed by a notable decline in the probability of deflation on this measure. Based on these observations, the obvious conclusion is that Federal Reserve official actions have had a meaningful impact on the market’s perception of the probability of deflation. Markets likely interpreted the Fed’s policies as a credible tool against deflationary outcomes, while aggressive post-crisis policy actions likely ensured the Fed’s willingness pull all the levers in the event that prolonged deflation fears do rise.  This view was further supported by Bernanke’s spring assertion that the Fed will “defend the inflation target from below” despite increased talk of tapering.

## **7. 30-Year Skew Turns Normal** 

For the past two years, OTM payors on 30-year rates have been rich to OTM receivers (see **Exhibit 8** ). Based on our Dynamic Term Structure Model[11] , we estimate that long-dated term premiums should average around 2.0%, leaving a 50bp buffer between the Fed’s long-term short rate and current market expectations. The tighter buffer increases the need for lower-rate protection and shifts skews to a more symmetric pricing of risk. 

Additionally, the funded status for pensions could improve dramatically, as rates rise to our forecasts, and equities also rise to our US equity strategy team’s expectations[12] , helping both the asset and liability sides of pension balance sheets. In this scenario, reaching fully funded status for the first time in years, pension accounts may decide to lock in their gains by hedging their exposure to lower rates and buy receiver swaptions on the back end of the curve. The pension community flows could bid receivers up, driving implied skews closer to normal. 

Finally, a steeper curve and higher vol environment may increase the attractiveness of callable bonds to Asian investors. In this event, callable issuance could increase – causing dealers to get long vega at higher rates, and vice versa. The asymmetric vega profile would contribute to a greater need for low-strike volatility – again pushing skews to a normal distribution. 

## **8. Front-End Rates Go Negative** 

The eighth surprise of 2014 would be if already low front-end rates decline further and potentially move into negative territory. 

Front-end bill and repo rates have stayed in the single-digits through most of 2013, owing to the dramatic decline in bill supply. Bill supply has declined by 15% over the six months leading up to fiscal year-end, causing bill and other money market rates to remain low. We envision two scenarios where front-end rates could potentially decline further and even dip into negative territory: 

First, if the SEC goes with “alternative one” of its money market fund (MMF) reform proposal and requires all institutional prime MMFs to switch to floating NAV. In a recent op-ed piece Boston Fed Governor Eric Rosengren reiterated

his case for requiring prime funds (both institutional and retail funds) to switch to floating NAV and for the SEC to abandon “alternative two”, which will impose “liquidity fees” and “redemption gates” on MMF investors during times of stress. This could potentially trigger outflows from prime money funds and inflows into government-only money funds and bank deposits. Currently, institutional prime funds hold 37.2% of MMF AUM and that increases to 56.3% if all prime fund holdings are included (see **Exhibit 9** ). With the dearth of frontend supply, there will only be limited capacity for inflows into government money funds. The overwhelming demand for liquid front-end government securities could push front-end rates lower and potentially into negative territory. 

Second, if the Fed cuts interest on reserves (IOR) without introducing unlimited fixed-rate reverse repo operations (RRP) to set an implicit floor on front-end rates. In the October minutes the Fed discussed the possibility of reducing IOR as a way to strengthen forward rate guidance and as a way to communicate its intension of keeping front-end rates “lower for longer”. Since then an intense debate has taken place in the markets as to whether the Fed will in fact reduce IOR “to signal its intention to keep short-term rates low or reinforce the forward guidance on the federal funds rate”. In the meantime the Fed continues its operational readiness exercise on its new RRP facility, which is scheduled to last until the end of January. Although some market participants expect the Fed to make RRPs permanent just like IOR, it is unclear when the Fed will undertake such a measure. If the Fed reduces IOR in isolation with introducing RRP, then fed funds effective rate and other front-end rates could decline further. We think it is unlikely that the Fed will cut IOR since we believe the costs far outweigh the benefits, and hence assign a low probability to such an outcome, but it is not entirely outside the realm of possibility.

## **9. Mortgage CC OAS Approaches Zero** 

The final surprise of 2014 would be that mortgage spreads tighten from here and OAS approaches zero. After the Fed starts tapering, the yield curve flattens as the market starts pricing in future rate hikes, and investors get very comfortable with the longer-term rate outlook – and because of very high Fed holdings, rolls trade very special.  Investors increasingly look to short volatility to earn the extra carry instead of going out the duration curve. Spreads tighten (instead of widening), which takes investors by surprise and forces them to cover their underweight positions. Historically, there have been a number of instances when mortgages have traded at zero to negative OAS (see **Exhibit 10** ) even when the Fed was not involved. The most recent experience was in 2005-2006, when the Fed was hiking rates in a very gradual manner and volatility declined significantly.
