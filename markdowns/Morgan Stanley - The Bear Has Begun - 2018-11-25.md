## **2019 US Credit Outlook | North America** 

## The Bear Has Begun 

We believe the credit bear market, which likely began when IG spreads hit cycle tights in Feb 2018, will continue in 2019, with HY and then eventually loans underperforming, as headwinds shift from technicals to fundamentals. 

**A more challenging macro backdrop:** In 2018, weakening flows and tighter liquidity conditions served as the key headwinds in credit, but as an important offset, the US economy remained solid. In 2019, we think it gets tougher on both fronts – monetary policy will likely near restrictive territory for the first time this cycle, while the tailwind from a booming economy fades as growth decelerates and earnings growth potentially slows to a standstill. As that happens, late cycle risks may morph into end-of-cycle fears, continuing to break the weak links along the way, especially the more levered parts of corporate credit markets. 

**Late cycle and beyond:** A turn in the credit cycle is not a specific point in time, but instead occurs in stages, over multiple years, beginning when growth is strong. With credit flows turning, financial conditions tightening, and idiosyncratic risks rising, we think that process has already started, slowly for now. And remember, the vulnerabilities in a cycle are always ignored on the way up. As this process continues to unfold and credit conditions tighten, the bull market excesses - this time centered around non-financial corporate balance sheets - should become increasingly clear. 

**A few silver linings:** While we certainly do not think the consensus has embraced the idea that end-of-cycle risks are rising fairly quickly, at the least, sentiment is much less uniformly bullish than it was at the beginning of 2018. Additionally, while spreads are nowhere near where they will likely peak when the cycle fully turns, after the recent sell-off, valuations are not as extreme in places. Both of these factors help at the margin. That said, we very much stick to our bigger picture view that the credit bear market is under way, and until valuations have truly priced in long-term fundamental risks, investors should use rallies to move up-in-quality. 

**2019 forecasts:** In our base case we forecast a -0.8% IG excess return, a 0.5% HY total return and a 1.3% loan total return. We expect $1.24tr, $183bn, and $436bn in IG, USD HY, and institutional loan gross issuance, respectively. Lastly, we project a 2.9% HY default rate. 

**Recommended positioning:** In IG we prefer As over BBBs, Fins over non-Fins, the front-end of the curve, low $- priced bonds, US over European banks, and European over US BBBs. In HY and loans we remain up-in-quality, and prefer short-duration HY bonds. We have a modest preference for loans over HY, but think that view may change later in the year. In derivatives we prefer long CDX risk to cash, owning long-dated vol, positioning for decompression, and buying BBB CDS protection vs index.

## Introduction 

**We believe the credit bear market, which likely began when IG spreads hit cycle tights in Feb 2018, will continue in 2019, with HY and then eventually loans underperforming as headwinds shift from technicals to fundamentals.** Looking back over the past few years, in hindsight, 2017 was the ‘Goldilocks’ year. Growth expectations were rising on the back of a synchronized global expansion, investors could look forward to the possibility of tax cuts, and monetary policy remained very accommodative, aided by low inflation. In 2018, challenges began to mount, but remained mostly technical in nature. In other words, the economy and earnings both boomed, in part due to fiscal stimulus, but the Fed began stepping harder on the brakes, weakening the flows into US credit. IG spreads widened as a result, driving negative excess returns YTD. High yield was more resilient to weakening demand, until recently, in part on the back of low supply, and the loan market bucked the outflow trend as investors searched for protection from higher yields. 

**In 2019, we think it gets tougher on both fronts – monetary policy should near restrictive territory for the first time this cycle, while the tailwind from a booming economy fades as growth decelerates and earnings growth potentially slows to a standstill.** Hence, we believe the challenges will shift from just technicals (tighter liquidity conditions/ weaker flows) to also fundamentals (concerns around credit quality/ weakening growth), and as that happens, late cycle risks may turn into end-ofcycle fears, with the higher beta/ more levered parts of credit markets underperforming. 

**The good news is that complacency is lower and valuations are cheaper (but not** 

**cheap).** For example, while we certainly do not think the consensus has embraced the idea that end-of-cycle risks are rising notably, at the least, sentiment is much less uniformly bullish than it was at the beginning of 2018. Additionally, while spreads are nowhere near where they will likely peak when the cycle fully turns, after the recent sell-off, valuations are not as extreme in places. Both of these factors help at the margin. **That said, we very much stick to our bigger picture view that the credit bear market has started, and until valuations have truly priced in long-term fundamental risks, investors should use rallies to move up-in-quality.**

## **As we have frequently argued, fundamental issues are easier to hide when liquidity is** 

**flooding into markets, and it is not anymore.** As liquidity conditions get squeezed, it is natural for dispersion in performance across asset classes, regions, sectors, and single names to pick up, with the weak links breaking first. US high yield was more resilient for most of the year than other markets, in part due to very low supply, and in part given its close ties to the strength in the US economy. But even HY, the "resilient" credit market, has only managed a roughly flat total return YTD, despite very strong earnings growth, a solid US economy, and supply down ~30%, which we think speaks to the importance of this tightening in liquidity conditions. 

**Looking to 2019 – two points are key to remember: 1) The liquidity withdrawal is going to accelerate, at first, and 2) unlike in 2018, it will happen as growth is decelerating. We think this will create an even more challenging backdrop, with the outperformance of higher beta credit fading as a result** . On the first theme, as we alluded to above, our economists expect two more rate hikes in 2019 (after one more hike in December 2018).

## From Late Cycle to End-of-Cycle 

**A turn in the credit cycle is a long multi-year process, in our view, not a specific point in time, and we think that process has begun** . While credit cycles are always different from one to the next, in our view the cycle turn often plays out in the following way: 

**1.** Inflation pressures build, the Fed progresses in its rate hike cycle, and credit flows begin to weaken, in part as higher rates mitigate the need to reach for yield. 

**2.** Financial conditions begin tightening, volatility rises, and spreads drift wider after hitting cycle tights, all while the economy remains strong. 

**3.** Idiosyncratic problems pop up more frequently as tighter liquidity conditions start to break the weak links. The excesses that built up in the bull market slowly start to expose themselves. 

**4.** Economic data begin to decelerate and growth expectations turn lower, slowly at first and then eventually much faster. 

**5.** Market sentiment turns more sharply as end-of-cycle fears rise, credit conditions tighten quickly, spreads gap wider. 

**6.** Companies retrench as earnings decline and the most levered credits lose access to capital – the credit cycle feeds into the business cycle. 

**7.** Defaults and downgrades follow, peaking often after the point when spreads have already peaked.

**Either way, the vulnerabilities, which are always tough to spot on the way up, will**

In short, as we have argued for a while, we think the excesses/imbalances are very much present, driven in part by such a long period of extremely low rates, which drove investors to reach aggressively for yield, and non-Financial corporates, in particular, to issue significant volumes of debt. Note, in many cases these risks look even worse today than when we ran this analysis at the end of 2017. To provide a few examples: 

In the leverage loan market, covenant quality is weaker than in 2007, the cushion beneath the average loan is lower and 1st lien leverage levels are higher. 48% of LBO transactions are levered over 6x vs 51% in 2007, but as a part of those leverage numbers, 27% of deals have EBITDA adjusted for prospective cost savings/ synergies vs only 15% in 2007. 22% of loan issuance was B- or lower in 2018 vs 15% in 2017 and 13% in 2007, driven in part by record CLO demand/issuance.

**Of course, the problems last time are not the same as the problems this time** . In the 2007-’09 cycle, the leverage built up in the financial system, in housing and on consumer balance sheets in the bull market, and those were the key sources of stress when the cycle turned. Most of those areas are much healthier today, as our table shows. In the current cycle, the leverage has found its way on non-Financial corporate balance sheets, among other places, which we think will be the main source of problems this time around. 

**When it comes to timing a more material turn, we need to pay attention to the market signals.** And we think most of these signals are suggesting that late-cycle risks are rising fairly quickly, again even versus this time last year. To provide just a few examples 

The 2s/10s yield curve hit just 18bp this year (currently at 26bp). 

- Labor shortages are rising, margin pressures are building, EPS has likely peaked, and P/E multiples are contracting. 

- Consumer and business confidence hit extreme levels this year and may have peaked. 

10 

- Equity breadth narrowed, even as markets were rallying this fall, with very defensive leadership. 

- Dispersion in markets has risen, with much more variable performance across the globe, as is often the case when liquidity conditions are tightening. 

- Volatility is trending higher (even in up markets), financial conditions have likely troughed, and we are probably quite late in a rate-hike cycle. 

IG credit diverged from equities for most of 2018 (through September). 

- The ‘tail’ in both IG and HY is growing with one ‘idiosyncratic’ problem after the next. Cyclical sectors in particular are showing an increasing number of cracks. Loan defaults have been creeping higher. 

- Consumer attitudes are deteriorating towards home buying and auto purchases, two key rates sensitive/ cyclical sectors. Consumer delinquencies are off of trough levels, especially for subprime borrowers. 

## **Again, ultimately, we need to look out for a more material tightening in credit** 

## **conditions – where levered companies start losing access to cheap and easy credit –** 

**and declining growth expectations.** While we are not fully there yet, given (1) these signals noted above; (2) our macro/earnings forecasts; and (3) some of the weakness in credit markets across the globe this year as well as in key cyclical sectors, we may be closer to this point than many believe. 

## **Finally, in the last stage in the credit cycle, which often takes place as the bear market** 

**has run its course, downgrades tend to spike and defaults peak.** See Corporate Credit Research: The Nature of the BBBeast, 2018.10.05, for our latest work on long-term downgrade risks. In short, given the size of the BBB part of the IG market and leverage levels today, we think long-term downgrade potential is high in the next credit cycle. In our view, this factor, among others (i.e., the size of credit markets and the changing ownership structure over time) will contribute to liquidity challenges on the way down. Similarly, we expect defaults to remain generally low next year, as defaults lag spreads. However, we think default expectations will soon start rising, with the default cycle potentially beginning in 2020. For our latest on defaults, see (Corporate Credit Research: Defaults Through the Cycle, 2018.10.26) and the leveraged finance section below.

**Qualitatively, in our base case, the credit bear market, which began when IG spreads hit cycle tights this past February, continues in 2019.** As we detailed in the first section above, in 2018, the technical headwinds for IG picked up as liquidity conditions tightened, but the US economy remained strong, helping to support higher beta credit. 

14 

In 2019, according to our forecasts, the Fed continues tightening at first, maintaining pressure on the flows into the asset class. However, unlike this past year, growth decelerates significantly, with GDP growth averaging only 1.4% in the 2nd and 3rd quarters as fiscal stimulus starts to fade, the interest rate sensitive parts of the economy (i.e., autos/housing) continue to soften, financial conditions tighten, and tariffs weigh on business investment. This combination of tighter central bank policy (globally) and considerably slower growth, drives spreads wider. Specifically, in 2019, with much slower economic growth and near-zero earnings growth for several quarters, weak fundamentals start to become more noticeable, leading to more material underperformance for the more levered/ economically sensitive parts of credit markets. And while leverage could tick lower for another quarter or two, we expect it to start trending higher again from already elevated levels by the end of 2019, as EBITDA growth again slows below debt growth. 

In our base case, we see a few modest positives. Investor complacency is lower today than it was at the beginning of 2018. Additionally, supply will decline in 2019, per our forecasts, as M&A activity slows, given moderating animal spirits, increased scrutiny of global transactions, and tighter financial conditions. And we expect the Fed to pause their rate hike cycle in 2H19, while ending quantitative tightening later in the year. A less aggressive Fed may mean currency hedging costs stop increasing (though as an offset, a weaker dollar may pressure unhedged foreign bond holdings, and we expect modestly softer LDI flows next year as well). Particularly when the Fed begins hinting at this pause, we could see a tradable rally in credit. But we see any rally next year as tactical, with the overall trend wider, as weakening growth drives increasing end-of-cycle risks. Remember, the biggest bout of spread widening in a credit bear market typically comes when the Fed is done hiking rates. 

**In the bull case** , the global economy remains robust as trade tensions fade, business investment remains strong, and productivity continues improving. The Fed does not pause their hiking cycle as a result, and rates rise more rapidly (consistent with our economists' bull case scenario), but spreads manage to tighten in the process (BBBs outperform), with investors encouraged by the likelihood of an even longer cycle. We forecast a year-end 2019 bull case spread target of 97bp and an excess return of 3.4%. 

**In our bear case** , we assume the economy is headed towards a recession in late 2019 or in early 2020. Flows get pulled from risk assets at a faster pace, investors are forced to sell into an illiquid market, and credit trades back to recession-like valuations by the end of the year, with high beta credit underperforming in a significant way. We forecast a year-end 2019 IG spread of 205bp in our bear case, and an excess return of -4.3%.

## Fallen Angels 

Overall, we expect ~$50bn of fallen angels in 2019, and ~$15bn of rising stars. We look at upgrade/downgrade potential in a few different ways. First, in terms of what the market is pricing in, ~$96bn of IG debt today trades wider than the BB HY index. Narrowing it down and controlling for maturity, we find that $188bn in bonds are from companies where all their 5-10Y debt trades wider than the BB+ index. On the other hand, just $22bn of debt in the HY index trades at or tighter than BBB index spreads. Second, we note that roughly $116bn of BBB rated debt by Moody’s is currently on 

20 

watch negative (though most of this debt is within the higher notches of the Baa cohort). Finally, using the 1Y ratings transition matrix from Moody’s, applied to the US IG index outstanding today, we get to ~$75bn in fallen angels and roughly $44bn in rising stars in 2019. 

Our forecast of $50bn in fallen angel volumes is skewed towards the lower end of most of the ballpark estimates we outlined above. In a nutshell, as we have noted in the past, downgrades typically lag market moves. And while IG spreads have widened a fair bit this year, 2018 is also likely going to mark a record low for fallen angel volumes (~$10bn through 3Q based on Moody's). Our forecast does incorporate more downgrades in 2019 compared to this year, but we think credit markets need to see further sustained weakness before fallen angel volumes pick up in a material way. 

The long-run story, of course, is different. As we have argued in many recent reports, given the size of credit markets today, and the deterioration in quality of the indices, downgrades over the course of this cycle could be meaningful. For example, over the past 3 cycles, fallen angels have added up to 7-15% of the IG market. As a % of the size of the BBB market, fallen angels have ranged from 23-45% over these cycles. Simply assuming that we see a repeat of these levels going forward, we get to a range of ~$550bn - $1.1tr for potential fallen angels long term, over the course of the next multiyear credit cycle (Exhibit 34). Further, as we show in Exhibit 35, the IG index is as levered as it has ever been. If companies were rated only based on leverage, 38% of the IG nonFinancial index would already carry a high yield rating. ## Bank deregulation enters 2019 

**We see a continued move toward a lighter regulatory touch for US banks, but not enough to materially weaken bank credit quality in the current cycle. We contend 2018 has proven that the worst case scenario of deregulation (for bank credit) will likely not play out.** 

We discussed regulatory developments for US banks at length in (Corporate Credit Research: Fins Are Fine, 2018.05.11), but in short, the US administration and their regulatory appointees continue to advance incremental tweaks to the post-crisis regulatory regime. While these changes surely unlock capital return (a key theme of bank stock bulls), we don't believe they imperil the capital strength of US banks, one of the key drivers of our constructive view on US Fins. 

**Thinking through the lessons of CCAR 2018** , the severe economic assumptions 

embedded in the test meant that three large US banks fell short of required regulatory minimums after capital distributions. However, the Fed's issuance of a "conditional nonobjection" to these banks is another reminder that banking regulators have a fair bit of leeway in supervisory behavior, even in the absence of a change in regulation or legislation, a theme we’ve previously discussed. Additionally, it marks a Fed willingness to consider bank arguments against ratcheting capital requirements yet higher from current standards and a desire for more predictability and transparency. 

**CCAR will continue to evolve in 2019 and 2020.** Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Quarles commented on remaining elements of the bank regulatory agenda in a November 9 speech at Brookings, looking beyond the Fed's October 31 proposal on banks less than $250bn in size, with a particular focus on changes to CCAR. For CCAR 2019, Quarles proposed exempting banks with less than $250bn of assets and tweaking certain assumptions, for example, eliminating the assumption that balance sheets continue to grow in stressed periods. Looking forward to 2020 CCAR and SCB, Quarles outlined a number of proposals and principles that the Fed will work on in 2019, including eliminating the qualitative test in CCAR, removing the leverage ratio from stress testing, allowing banks to see test results before requesting distributions, and looking at tweaks to the markets stress test. See Large Cap Banks: Quarles Comments Support our View that Deregulation Coming for GSIBs Too (9 Nov 2018) from our banks team in equity research for more details. 

So, the trend for capital requirements is lower, as it has been for the past year, but we 

23 

think it is only modestly lower. As we've said before, even if these regulatory changes were to lead capital or liquidity to concerning levels (not our base case), we think it will be an issue for the next cycle, and that Financials will outperform through the coming credit cycle.

## Overweight the front-end (1-5Y), equal-weight 30Y, underweight 5-10Y 

We expect the front-end of the market to outperform longer maturities in absolute and risk-adjusted terms (see table in Returns Forecast for a full summary of our return projections across the curve). While credit curves are still steep relative to history, in our view, a gradual bleed wider in spreads throughout 2019 should see the front-end outperform for a few reasons. First, after the spread widening in 2018, the front-end is a decent defensive carry trade, given its low volatility and attractive roll-down, especially as the full market drifts wider and overall excess returns remain weak (similar to 2015). Second, some of the technical headwinds that have impacted short-duration IG this year, should fade at the margin in 2019. For example, our view is that the impact of lower demand from corporate Treasuries due to repatriation is largely in the price. 

On the other hand, while the long-end has been supported by the LDI bid this year, the magnitude of these flows was likely outsized due to one-off factors such as the Sep 15th deadline for corporate pensions to make contributions and take advantage of higher tax rates. Relative to 2018, the LDI bid could slow down next year, especially if Treasury yields start declining again and the curve keeps flattening. ## **Qualitatively, in our base case, the credit bear market, which likely began when IG spreads hit cycle tights this past February, continues in 2019, with HY and, eventually,** 

**loans beginning to underperform.** Similar to what we outlined in the IG section above, in 2018, the technical headwinds for credit picked up as liquidity conditions tightened. HY was more insulated from weaker demand, until very recently, in part due to minimal supply, and in part given the strength in the US economy. The loan market was the exception to the outflow trend, as investors searched for protection from rising yields. In 

32 

2019, according to our forecasts, the Fed continues tightening at first. However, unlike this past year, growth decelerates significantly, with GDP averaging only 1.4% in the 2nd and 3rd quarters as fiscal stimulus starts to fade, the interest rates-sensitive parts of the economy (i.e., autos/housing) continue to soften, financial conditions tighten, and tariffs weigh on business investment. Tighter central bank policy (globally) starts to bite leveraged finance markets in 2019, especially as it is happening in the face of considerably slower growth and minimal earnings growth. Specifically, in this environment, weak fundamentals start to become more noticeable, leading to more material underperformance for the more levered/ economically sensitive parts of credit markets. 

In our base case, we see a few modest positives. Anecdotally, we observe lower investor complacency today than at the start of 2018. Additionally, supply will remain muted for HY and decline in the loan market, per our forecasts, as moderating animal spirits, higher yields, and tighter financial conditions impact M&A activity. And we expect the Fed to pause their rate hike cycle in the 2H19, while ending quantitative tightening later in the year. Particularly when the Fed begins hinting at this pause, we could see a tradable rally in credit. But we see any rally next year as tactical, with the overall trend wider, as weakening growth drives increasing end-of-cycles risks. Remember, the biggest bout of spread widening in a credit bear market typically comes when the Fed is done hiking rates. Lastly, we expect loans to remain resilient early in the year as the Fed keeps hiking, but start to underperform (beta-adjusted) later on in 2019 as end-of-cycle concerns rise, and weak fundamentals in the asset class start to become more noticeable as a result. Note, in addition to softer retail flows, we also expect lower demand from CLOs for loans in 2019 as well. 

**In the bull case** , the global economy remains robust as trade tensions fade, business investment remains strong, and productivity continues improving. The Fed does not pause their hiking cycle as a result, and rates rise more rapidly (consistent with our economists' bull case scenario), but spreads manage to tighten in the process (CCCs outperform), with investors encouraged by the likelihood of an even longer cycle. 

**In our bear case** , we assume the economy is headed towards a recession either in late 2019 or in early 2020. Flows get pulled from risk assets at a faster pace, investors are forced to sell into an illiquid market, and credit trades back toward recession-like valuations by the end of the year, with high-beta credit underperforming in a significant way.

## Recommended Positioning for 2019 

## **Up in quality – BBs over Bs, Bs over CCCs** 

We continue to recommend low beta high yield. Part of this view simply comes down to our expectation for credit spreads to continue widening and our belief that a credit bear market has begun. In short, we think reaching for yield at this point in the cycle is poor risk/reward. If all goes well, CCCs may outperform modestly, as they did in 2018. When the cycle does turn more clearly, CCCs have significant potential downside – having bottomed at $43 or lower (vs $84 today) in each of the last three bear markets (Exhibit 66). Also remember, CCCs do not only underperform around recessions, but in fact lag the market regularly in the 2nd half of cycles. For example, in the 2nd half of the last three cycles, CCCs underperformed HY in 73% of all years (Exhibit 67). 

Some investors argue that growth is strong, earnings growth is solid, and defaults are low - all good for high-beta credit. Our answer is that credit markets are forward looking. For example, think about a few other strong years from a macro perspective in this cycle. In early 2011, the ISM index surpassed 59, earnings were growing at 28% y/y and the HY default rate was ~2%. In Aug 2014, the ISM index surpassed 58, and HY defaults were sub 2%. Those were both ideal times to sell CCCs. Similarly, we expect economic growth and earnings growth to slow meaningfully in 2019, with monetary policy continuing to tighten, and as that happens, the most levered parts of credit markets will underperform, in our view. 

The risk to this trade is that growth remains much stronger than we expect and rates continue to rise, driving underperformance in low beta credit.

## **Loans over HY in the 1st half of the year** 

We continue to prefer loans over HY, at least in 1H19. Fundamentally, we think the loan market looks weaker than HY, especially after the growth in loan par in just the last two years, and the deterioration in credit quality, covenants, and structures in the loan market in this cycle. But ultimately, loans are the lower-beta asset class, and we are forecasting wider spreads next year. It is difficult to find too many environments where loans underperform HY when spreads are widening. Additionally, while HY has cheapened relative to loans in this recent sell-off (Exhibit 68), HY still looks modestly richer according to our fair value framework (see Exhibit 20 in the first section above). Lastly, we expect the loan market to remain more resilient from a demand perspective early in the year as the Fed continues to hike rates. However, as the year progresses and growth weakens, while the Fed stops hiking, we think loan flows may start to turn. Additionally our CLO strategists are expecting lower CLO supply in 2019 as well. Especially if Treasury yields start falling at that point, HY may outperform, at least betaadjusted. Hence, later in the year we think it may make sense to reduce loan vs HY exposure, but for now, we maintain our preference for loans. 

The risk to this trade is that spreads move sideways or tighten, especially if that happens as Treasury yields decline, and hence HY outperforms in total return terms.

## Credit Derivatives 

**In credit derivatives, we forecast a base case CDX IG spread of 91bp and CDX HY spread of 485bp for year-end 2019 (see Exhibit 77).** Our index spread forecast is based on two key factors: First, our overall forecast for spread widening in the cash market laid out earlier and second, we expect a widening liquidity premium in cash as bond technicals further weaken, which should drive outperformance of synthetic products. Our spread forecasts translate into a full-year return of 0.7% for rolling on-the-run CDX IG, boosted by the steepness of the curve and roll-down. In CDX HY, assuming a 3% projection for defaults, we estimate a rolling 1Y return of -1.1%. 

Bigger picture, as we think about how the credit cycle plays out, it is important to remember that ultra-easy central bank liquidity in this cycle has been an outsized technical tailwind, specifically for the corporate bond market. In comparison, the synthetic credit market has not seen the same kind of positioning excesses/reach for yield in this cycle. For example, structured credit flows drove large demand for synthetic credit risk in the previous cycle. In contrast, there hasn’t been a comparable buyer of CDS risk in recent years. In fact, volumes in the single-name CDS market have shrunk dramatically on the back of regulatory and capital headwinds. Considering the overall technical backdrop, as central banks continue to pull back in 2019, we think the technical unwind in cash markets will be more meaningful than in synthetic credit.

## 9 **) Tranche Trades – Dispersion and Convexity** 

In the tranche space, we recommend two trades. First, we like going short the IG equity tranche vs. index (delta-adjusted). While idiosyncratic risks have risen across cyclical pockets of the CDX IG market, equity tranches have held in reasonably well, in part because specific names that comprise the portfolio tail have rallied (e.g., the large retail names). In 2019, we expect elevated single-name dispersion and accordingly remain cautious on equity tranches. The risk to this trade is that dispersion remains low and equity tranches outperform. 

The second trade we like is going short 7-15% in IG31 vs. the index (equal-notional). This is our top pick for getting convex downside exposure. While implied vol could rise further, monetizing convexity in options outright can be hard given the short-dated expiry and wide forwards (that make puts expensive on a relative basis). In comparison, the senior mezz tranche has a 5Y duration, trades just slightly wider than the index, with a delta of 1.7x. The delta of this tranche increases as spreads widen, making it an attractive convexity play. The risk to this trade is that risk allocation to the 7-15% tranche keeps going down.
