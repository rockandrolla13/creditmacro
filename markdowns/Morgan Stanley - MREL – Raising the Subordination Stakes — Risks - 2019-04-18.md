## MREL – Raising the Subordination Stakes 

Strictly an RWA-linked measure previously, the BRRD2 has introduced a backstop for MREL linked to 8% of Total Liabilities and Own Funds (TLOF). The SRB has separately increased its own subordination minima. Re-running eurozone bank issuance needs, we get to goalposts of €243bn-€363bn. 

**Looking again at potential eurozone issuance following the final BRRD2,** which parliament finally signed off on Tuesday. Our calculations when we last looked at MREL, working with our equity research colleagues, started from an average eurozone bank MREL level of ~26.5% of RWAs, whereas our estimated go-to MREL level was closer to 27.7% (25.7% + a 200bp buffer). However, within that 26.5%, we had been counting 660bp of senior preferred debt. We ultimately arrived at an estimate of ~€345bn of NPS issuance needs over the next three years for the eurozone banks (~€500bn for European banks). 

**We now have a non-risk-weighted measure in place for MREL:** In previous proposals for the BRRD2, there had been disagreements on whether to have a TLOF-related MREL measure, in addition to a risk-weighted asset measure. 8% of TLOF made it into the final BRRD2, although this may be reduced to as low as ~6.6% by resolution authorities (RAs). We already factored this into our original MREL estimates (the full 8%), as we expected it to make it into the final. 

**Separately, the SRB is increasing its minimum subordination targets for GSIIs** 

**and OSIIs to 16% and 14% plus the combined buffer ratio** (CBR), from 13.5% and 12% plus CBR, and we expect these targets to be reset higher and higher over time. While under the BRRD2, the RA may allow banks to hold up to 3.5% of RWAs in non-subordinated format (i.e., preferred senior), counting towards MREL. However, the SRB has made it clear in policy and in speeches that subordination gives credibility to any resolution plan. 

**Our most conservative scenario suggests issuance needs of ~€363bn** using our equity colleagues' model and least conservative ~€243bn, for the eurozone banks, including refinancing needs. We continue to expect the bulk of MREL instruments to be issued over the next three years, despite the BRRD2 deadline for compliance being January 2024. Guidance from the SRB suggest that banks have 1-3 years to meet end-state MREL, although there will clearly be flexibility. 

**We include a note here on set-off rights,** considering the questions we've had from investors. In our view, the confusing message in the CRR2 ultimately means that having no waiver of set-off rights on bond documentation will not disqualify it from being either capital or counting for MREL. Going forward, though, we expect all bonds to be issued with such terms in the Ts & Cs, as we are already seeing.

## BRRD2 finalised and SRB policy toughened 

We've included a detailed explanation below for die-hard Directives fans, but in summary, we believe that the BRRD2 and the SRB's latest policy update bring in more onerous standards for size and subordination requirements for instruments that can be used to meet a bank's MREL (Minimum Requirement for Own Funds and Eligible Liabilities). 

Currently, there is a formula for calculating an RWA-equivalent figure for a bank's MREL. In the eurozone, this is two times a bank's Pillar 1 plus Pillar 2 requirements, then adding on Buffers and also a Market Confidence Charge (MCC), plus a bank-specific requirement (which is not disclosed and we do not estimate). This gets us to around a 26% RWA MREL requirement, on average (again, without the bank-specific extra). 

## Adding a non-risk-weighted backstop measure 

**The BRRD2 introduces a backstop measure of 8% fully subordinated TLOF** (although this can be reduced to as low as 6.6%, at the resolution authority (RA)'s discretion). **A bank's MREL is set at the higher of the TLOF measure and the RWA formula above.** We had already included a soft version of this 8% TLOF in our original MREL calculations, but the publication of the final BRRD2 formalises this. Note, for non-systemically important banks with greater than €100bn of assets, if the TLOF measure equates to a higher equivalent figure than 27% of RWAs – and this is higher than their formulaic RWA requirement for MREL – then their MREL is capped at 27% of RWAs. For GSIIs and for systemically important banks with less than €100bn of assets, there is no such cap. Banks deemed not systemic and with less than €100bn of assets will not have a TLOF backstop in any case. 

## MREL – the case for subordination 

Whether instruments that can meet MREL have to be subordinated to senior liabilities or not is a key question in our calculation of issuance needs and costs. Before turning to the SRB's policy and views here, it's worth a recap of why subordination is necessary for instruments which the RA expects to write down, convert to equity or bail in, to recapitalise a bank in resolution. Note, the correct BRRD2 term for wiping out capital in a resolution is write-down, for senior debt of any level, it is bail-in. We tend to use writedown in the following to cover all possibilities for the fate of bonds – write-down, bailin (meaning the same thing – going to zero) or conversion (converted to equity, to create equity capital). 

Banks issue equity, AT1 and Tier 2 as regulatory capital, and also non-preferred senior (NPS) debt and preferred senior debt. In a resolution, NPS is junior to preferred senior debt, and will be bailed in (thus increasing the failing bank's capital) in a resolution, before preferred senior debt. If a bank has a holding company, such as most UK banks, Swiss, ING and KBC, then all regulatory capital is issued out of the holdco, plus senior debt. These banks also issue senior debt from the operating companies. This senior debt from the holdco (holdco senior) can be viewed as the equivalent of NPS issued by a 

2 

bank without a holding company, as both get bailed in before the opco preferred senior debt. In what follows, we write about NPS for ease, but readers considering banks with holdcos can equate this to holdco senior. 

## NPS bailed in before preferred senior, in a resolution 

In a resolution, all regulatory capital must be written down or converted into equity, before the RA decides what to do with NPS. It can choose to write down or convert all, or part, or none of the NPS, depending on the recapitalisation needs of the institution in resolution. Only once all NPS is written down or converted would an RA look at preferred senior debt. 

## **Writing down preferred senior debt/converting it to equity is problematic, to say the** 

**least:** In most countries it ranks pari passu with a number of other liabilities that are either exempt from write-down (e.g., secured liabilities) or those the RA may not wish to write down (e.g., derivatives liabilities), due to systemic concerns. Furthermore, aside from Italy and Portugal, preferred senior debt is pari passu with certain deposits, such a corporate deposits, which again, theoretically the RA may wish to avoid touching. Also, still in some countries – the main example being Italy – preferred senior debt is held to still a significant extent by retail clients, which again may cause (not least political) problems with an attempted write-down/conversion. Finally, writing down preferred senior debt could destabilise bank funding more broadly – we believe that it would be in the regulator's and the EU's interests generally to preserve a layer of bank funding which truly cannot be written down, if only for the smaller, weaker banks to keep funding costs low. 

## Using preferred senior to meet MREL creates a host of risks 

The principles of 'No Creditor Worse Off' and respecting the creditor hierarchy argue against certain classes of liabilities getting bailed in at the same time as pari passu instruments remaining untouched. This could apply if, say, preferred senior debt was written down, while large corporate deposits, with which they are pari passu, are not. However, as we've heard from more than one RA, they "will get sued anyway" no matter what path resolution follows, but still, minimising litigation is a priority, at least when setting resolution plans. Indeed, the requirement for allowing non-subordinated instruments to count for MREL includes exactly that – "the inclusion of these liabilities in eligible liabilities items **would not give rise to a material risk of successful legal** 

**challenge or of valid compensation claims** as assessed by the resolution authority". Clearly, regulatory capital and NPS (or holdco senior) are all straightforward to write down or convert and recapitalise a failing bank – they are all contractually subordinated to the class of preferred senior which contains instruments the RA cannot or will not bail in, in a resolution. All these instruments would be swiftly written down/converted to equity/bailed in over the resolution weekend, recapitalising the failing bank to a level where it has the confidence of market counterparties, opening on the Monday. This cannot be said of preferred senior, which ranks pari passu with a number of instruments exempt from bail-in or that the RA would not wish to bail in. In our view, no RA would wish to have to rely on preferred senior to try to recapitalise a failing bank. Thus the EL in MREL – the Minimum Requirement for Own Funds and Eligible Liabilities – is crucial. Own funds (regulatory capital) can 'easily' be written down; should EL include preferred senior as well as NPS, this poses problems.

## Will the SRB allow the preferred senior carve-out? 

Despite this need for clearly bail-inable instruments, **the CRR2 does allow a carve-out for preferred senior to meet MREL.** In line with the FSB's term sheet for TLAC for G- SIFIs, this is set at the equivalent of 3.5% of RWAs after December 31, 2021 (2.5% before). Hence if an MREL was 28.5% of RWAs for a bank, and the RA allowed it to use this preferred senior carve-out, it would only have to fill 25% with regulatory capital and NPS, and the rest with preferred senior. 3.5% of RWAs is a sizeable amount of debt – equating to around €11bn for Credit Agricole, for example. Banks might be tempted to use this carve-out to meet their MREL as preferred senior debt is cheaper to issue than NPS and regulatory capital (for Agricole, 2025 maturity € NPS trades around z+80bp, preferred senior around half that level). 

As argued in our last MREL note, as the SRB sets MREL for banks on an individual basis with what we believe are annual targets, and with different ultimate timelines, it's been difficult to come up with supply estimates for non-preferred senior, the cheapest form of debt that banks can issue in 'subordinated' format. However, the SRB's second wave of broad resolution plans give a clear indication that it wants subordination levels to rise – GSIIs will need a minimum of 16% plus combined buffer requirements in subordinated format, while OSIIs must have 14% plus buffers. This is an increase from the 13.5% and 12.0%, respectively required under the first wave of resolution plans. Bank-specific subordination add-ons will be considered in addition to the general level based on systemic importance, which the SRB states it will use to address NCWO (no creditor worse off) issues, which we have highlighted above. 

The preferred senior carve-out is in CRR2 Art 72b(3), which notes that "the resolution authority **may** permit liabilities to qualify as eligible liabilities instruments **up to** an aggregate amount that does not exceed 3.5% of the total risk exposure amount" (our bold). So this carve-out needs RA approval, and it can be up to 3.5% – so could be allowed at a lower level, or not at all. Again, while the subordination requirement is not needed, "the inclusion of these liabilities in eligible liabilities items **would not give rise to** 

**material risk of successful legal challenge or of valid compensation claims** as assessed by the resolution authority". As discussed above, we are sceptical that bailing in preferred senior would be straightforward. This point (avoiding risk of successful legal challenge) is repeated in CRR2 Art72b(4), whereby it notes that if banks do not receive the 3.5% carve-out approval in 72b3, then they can still include such liabilities (preferred senior) if the amount of instruments excluded from EL (Art 72a()2) which rank pari passu to preferred seniors does not exceed 5% of a bank's own funds (regulatory capital) and EL. Such instruments excluded from resolution include certain deposits including <1 year, covered deposits, any retail and SME deposits exceeding the coverage level, secured liabilities, <7 day liabilities, liabilities arising from derivatives or debt instruments with embedded derivatives. **We think this is a high bar.** However, as noted above, Portugal and Italy have seniorised their whole deposit stacks, which would certainly help. Page 16 of the SRB's latest policy statement notes that "holdings of subordinated or senior instruments by retail investors could prove to be an impediment to resolution", which again could be particularly problematic in Italy, as efforts to prevent the sale of senior bank debt instruments to retail are relatively recent.

## Subordination is instrumental in making resolution strategies credible and feasible – SRB 

**Subordination remains critical for the SRB.** In her latest speech on January 17, Elke Koenig, Chair of the SRB, noted that the 2018 policy added the element of subordination, as "Subordination is instrumental for making resolution strategies more credible and feasible." In the January 16, 2018 SRB policy, it notes "Subordination improves resolvability and reduces the risk of breaching the NCWO principle. Subordination can be considered a tool to improve resolvability by making the implementation of the bail-in tool feasible and credible. In particular, subordination addresses risks stemming from (i) having bail-in-able instruments ranking pari passu with operational liabilities and any other excluded liabilities prescribed by Article 44(2) of the BRRD and (ii) the exercise of the discretionary power of the resolution authority to exclude some liabilities from bail-in under exceptional circumstances (Article 44(3) of the BRRD)." The current policy, while noting the 2.5% senior allowance (will move to 3.5% in 2022) is at the discretion of the SRB and not a given, states it cannot have a material adverse impact on resolvability, and that the proportion of mandatory excluded liabilities that rank pari passu with senior liabilities is less than 10%. Furthermore, the SRB is able to set a NCWO add-on related to mandatory exclusions from bail-in. Hence, right now, the SRB sets minimum subordination of 16% plus buffers for GSIIs, 14% plus buffers for OSIIs, but then can allow 2.5% (moving to 3.5%) for a senior allowance. The SRB is slowly creeping up the subordination stakes year by year, in our view, and has stated that it will continue to update its policy. 

## What's the timeline? 

The BRRD2 sets a deadline for MREL compliance by January 1, 2024, with some flexibility, including for those banks which are expected to get there in a reasonable timeframe thereafter. The BRRD2 also states that by January 1, 2022 non-GSIIs must meet subordinated MREL of at least 13.5% of RWAs and 5% of leverage exposure (undemanding, we believe). Separately (and noting the 13.5%/5% are minima), RAs will set an intermediate target level for the same date, on a bank-by-bank basis, which (according to the BRRD2) as a rule, shall ensure a linear build-up. 

We understand that the SRB is setting timelines for compliance bank-by-bank, running from one to four years, hence the longest timeline is currently to 2023, a year ahead of official BRRD2 compliance. **With each SRB policy refresh, we expect subordination requirements for MREL go up, and in our view, full subordination of MREL will ultimately be the norm, with any carve-out allowed for preferred senior the exception.** 

## Our scenarios 

We already had a 'soft' 8% TLOF in our previous MREL model, as this had been included in one of the draft versions of the BRRD2. Clearly, it's now set in stone. In terms of the NPS versus preferred senior angle, we had assumed that banks would take full advantage of the 3.5% preferred senior carve-out and also allowed for banks to fill just 75% of their 'MREL gap' with NPS, over the next few years. **We update these** 

**assumptions now and add more conservative approaches,** considering views expressed by the SRB linked to subordination, plus the timelines we detail above, with the SRB setting compliance for banks currently at the latest for 2023 (although clearly, there 

5 

will be some flexibility). Our scenarios are as follows, from most conservative (largest amount of subordinated MREL required) to least. In every scenario though, we are ignoring any institution-specific MREL add-on that the SRB can include, and hence these are less conservative than otherwise. These institution-specific numbers are not disclosed. 

- Scenario 1 – the most conservative – takes the higher of the RWA-based MREL requirement per bank and 8% of TLOF, then assumes the whole will be required to be subordinated. It assumes that no preferred senior is allowed to count as MREL, and that a bank has another 100bp of subordinated debt (NPS) on top of its MREL, as an excess. 

- Scenario 2 – takes the higher of the RWA-based MREL requirement per bank and 8% of TLOF, then assumes the whole will be required to be subordinated. It assumes that if any preferred senior is allowed, this is purely used as an excess, sitting on top of its fully subordinated MREL requirement. We generally assume a bank would want to maintain a 200bp excess; should it get the full 3.5% preferred senior allowance from the RA, then it would use this for its excess (i.e., its excess would be 350bp, not 200bp). 

- Scenario 3 – same as Scenario 2, but allows a reduction in the 8% TLOF according to the BRRD2 formula, on a bank-by-bank basis. If the binding constraint in Scenario 1 was the RWA measure, then this scenario would have no impact on Scenario 1 numbers. 

- Scenario 4 – same as Scenario 3, but assumes the 3.5% preferred senior carve-out is allowed and that banks use this to fill their 200bp excess and part of their actual MREL requirement (150bp of it). 

- Scenario 5 – same as Scenario 4, but assumes a bank only maintains a 100bp excess; hence can use preferred senior to meet 250bp of the MREL requirement. 

- Scenario 6 – same as Scenario 5, but reverting to our original, generous assumption that the banks meet their current MREL 'gap' with just 75% of subordinated instruments and 25% preferred senior – take whichever scenario gives you the lower NPS need. We do not believe this will be allowed in any end-state. 

From Exhibit 2, Scenario 1 clearly has the highest issuance needs as it's the most conservative, at a total of €362.5bn. That suggests that 25% of the 'gap' is a lower number than the 3.5% of RWAs carve-out. ## An aside on set-off rights 

We get a lot of investor questions on set-off rights for MREL. CRR2 Article 72b (f) requires that "the liabilities are not subject to set off or netting arrangements that would undermine their capacity to absorb losses in resolution"; this applies to capital instruments as well as EL. This makes sense from a resolution perspective, wanting to retain the full amount of the liability (not a netted-off amount) for write-down/bailin/conversion. However, Recital 21d notes that "This **should not mean** that the contractual provisions governing the liabilities should contain a clause explicitly stating that the instrument is not subject to set-off or netting rights" (our bold). We understand the background to the somewhat confusing flexibility stated in the recital (is no set-off rights a requirement? is it not?) is that a number of Member States demanded this flexibility. This stems from concerns linked to contractually removing consumer rights, potentially enshrined elsewhere in EU legislation as a legal principle, rather than specifically linked to bank debt instruments. **Ultimately, there is no legal requirement to have a clause (waiver of set-off) and the absence of a clause should not disqualify an instrument as otherwise counting as capital or EL.** 

Banks we have spoken to agree with this conclusion – a contractual clause is not required, hence the bonds remain compliant if they meet all other requirements. The conversation tends to end there. Our take from the backdrop to what's in the CRR2 is that it is not meant to disqualify liabilities and that we should not expect any regulatory par calls for capital, or early calls if it's otherwise uneconomic, or LME on EL, due to the lack of a waiver of set-off in documentation alone. At best, it could theoretically be a supporting factor only, to any of these. In addition, the CRR2 indefinite grandfathering arrangements for EL do not require a waiver of set-off rights (Article 72b(2)f) for instruments to qualify, hence it is clearly not seen as a critical factor. AT1 and Tier 2 without the waiver would still quality for the CRR2 6-year grandfathering for capital instruments – however, we stick with the view it does not need to be contractual, in any case. 

We would expect all capital and EL instruments going forward though to include such a waiver, in a 'belt and braces' approach (and for the ones we have checked, they do). Separately, we'd point out (and these are thoughts – we are not lawyers): 

Compliance here appears to be left to the appreciation of the supervisor/resolution entity, and it seems highly unlikely that they would opine on individual instruments. Most likely would be that the EBA covers this topic in terms of providing guidance, either in its annual AT1 report, or linked to grandfathering, or MREL definitions, later this year. 

It's difficult to get answers here, but some national jurisdictions (we know of Denmark and Sweden) do not grant automatic set-off rights, so theoretically there is nothing to waive, for bonds issued by those banks. 

- If netting rights do indeed exist, we wonder what level of liability you would need to set off against an AT1-level asset, as an investor? Again, we are not lawyers and don't know how set-off works in insolvency or if level of subordination matters, but

If you are a Spanish investor, owning a French bank NPS, which is written under English law, what law do you need to refer to when thinking about set-off rights? And would it matter what law your offsetting liability was under? We assume it would all come under French law but, again, we simply don’t know how this might work. 

Finally, we had been thinking about insolvency laws in various countries, and setoff rights enshrined there. However, banks will not face insolvency in the future – they will face resolution. Resolution rules give rights to the resolution authority to write-down bonds. We could appreciate that set-off rights in insolvency could be relevant when thinking about NCWO, and comparing bondholder losses in resolution versus the alternative, but that might be as far as it goes. Should a bank be in resolution and has failed in such a manner that any estimated valuations of liabilities under an insolvency procedure would almost certainly be zero, set-off rights are likely irrelevant then in any case. 

We wrote about set-off rights initially in our Outlook last November, pointing out a number of the problems mentioned above. Considering this to be one of the leastimportant CRR2 changes at the time, we noted that the cross-section of AT1s with no waiver of set-off rights but being otherwise CRR2 compliant was just nine bonds. All the rest with no waivers had underlying law problems, such as English law with no contractual recognition of EU write-down powers. We published those nine in our Outlook, and in our most recent published presentation include all those AT1s without waiver of set-off, for completeness. Again, we do not believe that this is a real issue for compliance with capital and EL rules and do not expect any accelerated calls.

## Appendix: BRRD2 update 

The final version of the BRRD2 was voted through by the European Parliament on April 16 and adds a TLOF-measure to MREL. The source for the key information on this is BRRD2 Art 45 ‘Eligible Liabilities for Resolution Entities’. Again, note MREL stands for Minimum Requirement for Own Funds and Eligible Liabilities', essentially instruments that are formally subordinated (CET1, AT1 and Tier 2, all Own Funds, or bank capital) and Eligible Liabilities, namely other instruments which can absorb losses/be bailed in. As NPS is subordinated to preferred senior in a resolution, NPS should be viewed very clearly as EL. 

## **45b states that EL generally must satisfy all EL characteristics but not the** 

- **subordination one** (CRR2 72b2d); this clearly opens the door to preferred senior counting as EL. 45b stipulates though that G-SII EL must meet all the characteristics – i.e., including subordination. 

**45b(3) goes on to state that the resolution authority (RA)** _**shall**_ **ensure that part of the minimum MREL (calculated versus RWAs) is fully subordinated** – i.e., meets all the characteristics of EL (and does not carve out the subordination requirement). However, while it could be more clearly worded, it does still appear to allow the 3.5% RWA preferred senior allowance within that – this carve-out is in CRR2 Art 72b3). This requirement applies to GSIIs, non-G-SIIs >€100bn in assets and non-G-SIIs<€100bn which are deemed systemic. 

**The RAs** _**may**_ **permit this subordination level to be lower than 8% of Total Liabilities and Own Funds (TLOF):** Hence the 8% of TLOF subordination 

requirement can be reduced, but not lower than the result of a specific formula, which is 1-(X1/X2), multiplied by 8%. X1 is 3.5% of RWAs, X2 is 18% of RWAs plus the combined buffer (defined in CRD4 Art 128 (6)). If we take a large bank's combined buffer as ~4%, we get the minimum to 6.73% TLOF. As the combined buffer includes the Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) of 2.5% for all banks, **the lowest this can go to is 6.63%.** Theoretically, reading 128(6) a bit more loosely (we don't) could lead you to arguably ignore the CCB and just take into account the CCyB, plus GSII/OSII and systemic risk buffer, as appropriate. If these are zero, then the ultimate lowest this subordination requirement can go is 6.4%. 

If the above results in non-GSIIs with >€100bn of assets having to hold subordinated MREL of above 27% of RWAs, then **the RA** _**shall**_ **limit the requirement to maximise subordination to 27% RWAs** and no more (if the resolution plan for the non-GSII does not consider access to the resolution financing arrangements – i.e., resolution would not require a common fund to step in). However, **if you are a non-GSII with less than €100bn and having systemic importance, this 27% maximum does not apply** (i.e., it could go above 27% RWAs). 

**45b(6) states that RAs** _**may**_ **decide that the requirement for sub debt will be set at the higher of 8% TLOF or** ((2x the total capital requirement of 8%) plus (2x Pillar 2 Requirements) plus the combined buffer). 45b7 states that 45b6 (i.e., a likely punitive requirement) can be used for the same banks subject to the TLOF backstop (but no more than 30% of them) which meet any of a set of conditions, 

10 

including having impediments to resolvability and not taking remedial action, having a resolution plan that lacks credibility and having Pillar 2 Requirements (as per CRD5 Article (104A) linked to risks not covered by other capital requirements, including due to repeatingly failing to maintain Pillar 2 Guidance and that put them in the top 20% riskiest institutions. 

Article 45c relates to ‘Determination of the minimum requirement for own funds and eligible liabilities'. 45c3a states that notwithstanding 45b, non-GSIIs must meet a subordinated part of MREL of at least 13.5% of RWA and 5% of leverage exposure. This would suggest that 45b could result in a lower requirement, which seems somewhat odd, although we'd point out the 8% referred to in 45b is TLOF and in 45c the 5% links to leverage exposure, which includes some off-balance sheet items (defined in CRR2, Articles 429 and 429a). 

Article 45m 'Transitional and post-resolution arrangements' notes that the RA will decide a transitional period for banks to reach requirements set according to Article 45b(3) or 45b(6), with an intermediate deadline set at January 1, 2022 and **final compliance required January 1, 2024** . However, a longer transitional period is allowed under a number of conditions, including that the bank is likely to ensure compliance in a reasonable timeframe. Furthermore, **the deadline to comply with 45c(3a) shall be January 1, 2022,** giving us a better rationale as to why those set requirements (which are far lower than the TLOF-related requirements) exist.
