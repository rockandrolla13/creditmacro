## Are You Sure That's 'Low Beta'? 

Asset class betas can shift materially over time, calling into question the conventional wisdom about what qualifies as 'defensive'. 

**Challenging some conventional wisdom:** A central question to portfolio discussion is whether an asset will go up more or less than the broader market. But assumptions here can often be overly subjective or influenced by recent events. A broader perspective and a quantifiable framework are warranted. 

**A scorecard for what is 'reliably low beta':** The defensiveness of an asset can vary significantly over time. We systematically rank assets based on three components: i) Their beta to global equities; ii) The stability of that beta; and iii) The ability of that beta to hold when the overall market sells off. 

**This is especially relevant when discussing the US dollar, US (versus RoW) equities and growth (versus value):** All three assets are frequently categorised as 'low beta', with investors expecting outperformance under market stress. But history gives a much more mixed picture (for all three), and on a trailing threeyear basis all have been acting like high-beta assets. 

**What's 'low beta' today?** Within equities, utilities, staples and RoW equities show up well on our beta scorecard. Within fixed income, G3 rates, JPY and DM IG credit are low-beta assets with good stability and persistency.

## Are you sure that's 'low beta'? 

Beta, a measure of risk arising from exposure to overall market moves, is important to and frequently used in investment decisions. Allowing an investor to measure the sensitivity of the investment to overall market moves, one can identify the amount of risk an investment adds to a portfolio. 

**However, this sensitivity to the overall market often changes over time:** In this report, we identify 'low-beta' assets which have low, stable and reliable beta to global equities, and also highlight assets currently considered low beta that have a more chequered history. 

## **How is 'beta' different from looking at correlation?** 

Correlation measures how closely two assets move in relation to each other. It captures merely the _direction_ of the move, not the magnitude. Beta, on the other hand, not only takes into account how correlated asset moves are to the overall market, it also measures the magnitude by which this asset moves _relative_ to the market. 

This difference is especially crucial when making portfolio decisions. The beta of an asset to an overall portfolio encompasses not just the correlation of this asset to the portfolio but also the incremental volatility this asset would add to the portfolio.

## Does it diversify when you need it? 'Upside' and 'downside' betas give the full story 

The overall beta may be a useful number to keep track of, but splitting the overall beta uncovers some interesting differences between how an asset performs during equity upmarkets and during down-markets. We discuss some important beta shifts in this section. 

## How to read this table 

We compute three measures of beta based on weekly returns in Exhibit 10 and compare the current value on its 20-year range: 

**1.** Overall beta: Beta to global stocks over a three-year horizon; 

**2. Upside** beta: Beta to global stocks when stocks are **rallying;** 

**3. Downside** beta: Beta to global stocks when stocks are **falling.** 

The further the dot is to the right (in the red area), the higher the beta is now on its own 20-year range. On the contrary, the further the dot is to the left (in the green area), the lower the beta is now on its 20-year range. 

For example, S&P 500 has a downside beta to global equities of 1.17 now. This is not only higher than its overall beta (1.04) but also extremely high on its own historical 20-year range. This means that over the past three years, S&P 500 has been a higher-beta market and has sold off more during a broad equity market sell-off.

## Constructing our beta scorecard 

The level of beta matters. But we think it's more than just the level of risk that an investor should care about. The stability and persistency of the beta are equally important. Our beta score incorporates these three different measures, which we use to systematically identify a 'low-beta' asset: 

**1.** Low/negative beta to global equities; 

**2.** Stability of that beta across time; and 

**3.** The persistency of that beta in market sell-offs, which we define as downside beta (i.e., the beta works your way when you most need it to!). 

All these measures are important and we explore below how we quantify them. 

## Level of beta (40%) 

For the level of beta, we compute a **12-month beta to global equities.** Now, what does this number mean? And what exactly is considered 'low beta'? Admittedly, it's hard to assign a hard and fast number for what 'low beta' actually means. 

Instead of arbitrarily determining a 'low-beta' threshold, **we derive a scoring system by using a min of -0.5 and a max of 1.5 to determine a score from 0 to 100,** allowing us to very easily rank different assets to identify assets with relatively low beta to global equities. This takes into account ~90% of the observations across major asset classes while ensuring that the score does not get thrown off by outliers.

## Beta stability (30%) 

Betas can be quite volatile across history. It's hard to predict how the beta of an asset will evolve in the future. But by looking at the stability of the beta over the last 10 years, we are able to identify assets which have a relatively stable beta to global equities. As a proxy for beta stability, we use an interpercentile range (5th versus 95th percentile), a measure of variability, across the last 10 years. **The narrower this interpercentile range is, the more stable the beta has been across history.** We prefer assets with a low and reliably _stable_ beta to global equities.

## The scorecard versus 'owning low beta' 

Generating a historical times series of our beta scorecard using data up until that point of time, we look at how the beta scorecard has fared across history. In the interest of creating a multi-asset portfolio ranked by our beta scorecard, we include cross-market equity trades (for example, going long utilities versus the market or going long value versus growth) to ensure the equity sectors and styles are well-represented within the multi-asset portfolio. By systematically buying the top five assets on our beta scorecard, **we find that our beta scorecard strategy has generated a risk-adjusted return of ~0.5, which is higher than a buy equity vol strategy** . 

**Does this work better than just going long assets with low beta?** Historical performance data suggest yes. Buying assets based on our aggregate beta scorecard has generated better risk-adjusted returns than just looking at the level of beta. This attests to the importance of looking at not only the level of beta but also the stability and persistency of that beta.
