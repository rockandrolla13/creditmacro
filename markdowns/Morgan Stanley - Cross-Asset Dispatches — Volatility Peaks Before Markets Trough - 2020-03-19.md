## Volatility Peaks Before Markets Trough 

Volatility repricing is historic and broad-based. We see a strong case for volatility to peak before a final risk markets trough. 

**All volatilities now extreme:** After lagging early on, volatility across rates, credit, commodities and currencies has jumped sharply. We are short of superlatives to describe what vol markets are pricing here. The S&P 500 had seven consecutive days with daily moves larger than 4%, beating the record from 1929. Optionsimplied daily moves are clearly possible in the near term, but 3-month implied levels are historically rare, even during 2008. Term structure and skew are also at historical extremes. 

**Volatility should peak** _**before**_ **markets trough:** We think that volatility needs to stabilise before the broader market can heal. There is precedent for this; in 2008, 2011, 2015 and 2018, equity volatility peaked well ahead of the ultimate low. The intuition? After a shock, markets _first_ become comfortable with the level of uncertainty (volatility), _then_ with the level of price. We think that risk/reward for markets is improving; this remains a key unticked box on our checklist.

## Volatility should peak before markets trough 

In our previous arguments against 'buying the dip', we've cited five factors we're watching: 

**1.** Our cycle models were in 'downturn', suggesting worse-than-average returns. 

**2.** The peak in new US and European cases still lay ahead. 

**3.** Policy, via rate cuts, was not particularly effective. 

**4.** Valuations were not cheap. 

**5.** Volatility kept making new highs. 

These are starting to shift. With valuations significantly cheaper, and policy becoming more aggressive, **we think that risk/reward is improving,** and have gradually started to add exposure to equities and credit, and reduce weights to bonds (see Cross-Asset Dispatches: Too Far, Too Fast – Add Some Exposure, March 13, 2020). So far our changes are modest as we wait for more of this checklist to be ticked off (see CrossAsset Playbook: Brace for Recession, March 17, 2020). 

## **A key to the next leg of raising exposure would be a peak in volatility:** Both implied and 

realised volatility have been extreme by any historical measure, quite the feat considering what markets have gone through over the last 30 years: 

- The S&P 500 had seven consecutive days with daily moves larger than 4%, beating the record from 1929. 

Equities and Brent options are now priced for daily implied moves of around 5%. 

- Credit saw the worse single-day move since 2008, pushing the implied daily moves for CDX IG from 1.1bp at the start of the year to ~9bp a day. 

Even FX, which had been lagging, saw some big shifts, with USDJPY and some EMFX vol spiking very close to the all-time highs. These level of options-implied daily moves are clearly possible in the near term, but 3-month implied levels are historically rare, even during 2008. 

## **We see a case for volatility to moderate, and position for a peak in implied volatility at** 

**these levels:** First, markets now imply levels of volatility that have rarely, if ever, been realised over a 1- or 3-month horizon, even in the GFC. Second, there is a strong precedent for volatility markets peaking before _spot_ markets trough. In 2008, 2011, 2015 and 2018, the high print for VIX came several weeks before spot markets troughed. Why? **When bad news arrives, markets** _**first**_ **have to become comfortable with the level of uncertainty (volatility),** _**then**_ **become comfortable with price.**

## What's in the price? 

Implied daily moves for options are now are at levels which are clearly possible in the near term (one month) but likely hard to sustain/realise over three months. One way to look at this bar is in terms of daily implied moves – the S&P 500 is priced for a ~5% implied daily move, as is Brent, while CDX IG has gone from pricing daily implied moves of ~1.1bp at the end of December to 9bp now. 

Strangle breakeven ranges or +/-1 σ tails are one indication of how far vols have moved now. The options-implied 1yr +/-1 σ tails are ~1860-3440 for the S&P 500, 0% to 2.0% for US 10yr and 97.6-113.2 for USDJPY. We think that these ranges are quite wide already and vols don't need to reprice a lot more to cushion the worse scenarios. **Trade:** 

## **We like selling S&P 500 3m 25delta strangles, which earn a premium as high as 9%.** 

The primary risk is that the S&P ends outside the breakeven range of 1900-3200 and the trade loses money. In a similar vein, we like selling 3m 25delta strangles on USDJPY which earn 2.5% with a breakeven range between 98.2 and 113.4.

Another measure of what's in the price is the implied peak-to-trough (defined as the return from the 52-week high to options-implied -1 σ level). The implied peak-to-trough decline in the price is about 45-55% for most of the major stock indices. Considering that the average equity bear market is about a 34% peak-to-trough decline (see Cross-Asset Dispatches: The Bear Market Almanac 2020 Edition, Updated, March 13, 2020), a lot is in the price already from an options perspective. 

**Where could underwriting/covered shorts be attractive?** The implied peak-to-trough (defined as return from 52-week high to the -1 σ level on the current options-implied probability distribution) is now nearly 50-60% for European indices, surpassing the median bear market. In FX, BRL and MXN stand out for implying a 33% decline in contrast to a 12% decline for KRW. 

In these markets, covering existing shorts in the underlying asset with selling OTM puts is attractive, e.g., in the S&P 500, 3m 10% OTM (25delta) puts yield 6.5% and break even below 1950. In USDMXN, 1yr 10% OTMF calls pay ~3% and break even if USDMXN rises above 27.5. 

**Where could overwriting be attractive?** The implied trough-to-peak (defined as return from the 52-week low to the +1 σ level on the current options-implied probability distribution) is more than 50% for a number of equity markets. In some of the higher dividend-yielding countries such as Spain and the UK, the yield on equities taking into account the FX-hedged + selling calls can top 10% (5-6% dividend yield + 1-2% carry from FX hedge + 3.5-4.5% premium from 30delta calls). Even after haircutting the dividend yields to account for potential reductions, this can be an attractive yield.

The scale of the recent moves means that front-end vols have risen the most. Term structures in stocks, rates, Brent and select FX are now on a par with the credit crisis. Positioning for further asset weakness using a normalisation of the term structure is attractive at such extreme inversion levels. We like zero-cost calendar trades to take advantage of this: 

**Trade: Buy 6m 15% OTM puts and sell 1.7x of 2m 15% OTM puts on the S&P 500:** The trade is flat if the S&P 500 ends higher than today in two months' time and does best if the S&P 500 is around 2050 in early May, and vol term structure normalises. The trade loses money if the S&P 500 ends below 1850 in May. 

Our rates strategists like a similar trade – 2m/4m calendar spread on 30yr rates (see The Extremes Of The Rate Vol Market, February 29, 2020).

## Appendix: Strategy Risk Factors 

Buying calls or call spreads: Investors who buy call options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes below the strike price at expiration. Investors who buy call spreads (buy a call and sell a further OTM call) also have a maximum loss of the entire up-front premium paid. The maximum gain from buying call spreads is the difference between the strike prices, less the upfront premium paid. 

Buying puts or put spreads: Investors who buy put options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes above the strike price at expiration. Investors who buy put spreads (buy a put and sell a further OTM put) also have a maximum loss of the upfront premium paid. The maximum gain from buying put spreads is the difference between the strike prices, less the upfront premium paid. 

Selling calls: Investors who sell covered calls (own the underlying security and sell a call) risk limiting their upside to the strike price plus the upfront premium received and may have their security called away if the security price exceeds the strike price of the short call. Additionally, the investor has full downside exposure that is only partially offset by the upfront premium taken in. Investors short naked calls (i.e. sold calls but don’t hold underlying security) risk unlimited losses of security price less strike price. Investors who sell naked call spreads (i.e. sell a call and buy a farther out-of-the-money call with no underlying security position) have a maximum loss of the difference between the long call strike and the short call strike, less the upfront premium taken in, if the underlying security finishes above the long call strike at expiration. The maximum gain is the upfront premium taken in, if the security finishes below the short call strike at expiration. 

Selling puts: Put sellers commit to buying the underlying security at the strike price in the event the security falls below the strike price. The maximum loss is the full strike price less the premium received for selling the put. Put sellers who are also long a lower dollar-strike put face a maximum loss of the difference between the long and short put strikes less the options premium received. 

Buying strangles: The maximum loss is the entire premium paid (put + call), if the security finishes between the put strike and the call strike at expiration. 

Selling strangles or straddles: Investors who are long a security and short a strangle or straddle risk capping their upside in the security to the strike price of the call that is sold plus the upfront premium received. Additionally, if the security trades below the strike price of the short put, the investor risks losing the difference between the strike price and the security price (less the value of the premium received) on the short put and will also experience losses in the security position if he owns shares. The maximum potential loss is the full value of the strike price (less the value of the premium received) plus losses on the long security position. Investors who are short naked strangles or straddles have unlimited potential loss since if the security trades above the call strike price, the investor risks losing the difference between the strike price and the security price (less the value of the premium received) on the short call. Additionally, they are obligated to buy the security at the put strike price (less upfront premium received) if the security finishes below the put strike price at expiration. Strangle/straddle sellers risk assignment on short put positions that become in the money. Additionally, they risk having stock called away from short call positions that become in the money.
