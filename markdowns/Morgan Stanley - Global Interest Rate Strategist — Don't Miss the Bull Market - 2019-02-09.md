## The cash balance-QE fallacy 

As we have written about in the past, the US Treasury needs to get its cash balance held at the Fed down to a certain level ($202 billion) on March 1 (see Money Markets in Penitent Patience). After that, it can build it back up, and we expect it to do so (see Exhibit 3). When Treasury reduces its cash balance, the cash that would otherwise end up at the Fed ends up in the banking system as reserves. 

Some analysts see this injection of reserves into the banking system akin to quantitative easing (QE) which also injected reserves into the banking system. Unfortunately, that's where the similarity ends, in our view. As we discussed in Insight into the Balance Sheet, QE was effective because it removed assets with longer duration from markets just as the demand for those assets was increasing, thanks to the Fed's zero interest rate policy (see Exhibit 4). In exchange, the Fed offered reserves with near-zero yield.

## **Italian growth deterioration, cause for concern? Revisiting Italy** 

Since the end of January 2019, the Italian 10y/German 10y spread has widened more than 40bp to a spread level last seen in mid-December 2018 just prior to a budget resolution being agreed with the EU. The recent widening has occurred despite the strength observed at the 6 February 30y syndication that was more than 5x oversubscribed and attracted a record €41bn in interest . A series of events over the last week have coincided with and been viewed as the collective catalyst behind the recent price action including: 

- On January 31, Italy reported its second consecutive quarter of GDP contraction, meaning it has entered a technical recession. 

- On February 1, Italy’s manufacturing PMI was reported to have fallen considerably below the consensus estimate to its lowest level since Q2 2013, at 47.8. The decline also marked the 4th straight month that Italy’s manufacturing PMI remained below 50. 

- On February 5, Italy announced plans to issue a new 30y bond via syndication, the timing of which potentially surprised the market, as well as plans to raise $2bn from property sales to help cut the debt. 

- And finally, on February 7, the EU Commission lowered its forecast for Italy’s 2019 GDP growth to 0.2%, a full percentage point below its previous estimate of 1.2% contained in the Autumn 2018 forecasts. 

Given the growth centric theme of recent developments, we suspect that the widening in the Italian spread has largely been related to market concerns that growth estimates contained in the budget will end up being too optimistic. And that, in turn, the weaker growth profile could potentially lead to larger than expected gross and net issuance needs during 2019, a wider budget deficit than projected should the Italian government follow through on its fiscal agenda and a risk that recent slight declines in the debt to GDP/ratio experienced over the last couple of years could begin to reverse, all while the ECB is no longer expanding its balance sheet. 

5 

Additionally, concerns about a protracted recession could lead to an increase in general uncertainty surrounding not just the strength of the political coalition but also about the health of the banking sector given the still large stock of non-performing loans. 

## **But have poor PMIs been coincident with wider spreads?** 

As previously noted, the last time that Italy’s PMI figures were this low was in 2013, and we thought it important to analyze whether any actual coincident and inverse relationship had previously existed between the Italian/German 10y spread and poor levels of either PMI growth or increased economic uncertainty in the past. 

To do so, we conducted a very simple analysis during the period post the financial crisis to determine whether spread levels became particularly susceptible to deterioration in PMI data and/or a general increase in uncertainty given the significant increase in Italy’s debt to GDP ratios from around 115% in 2010 to close to 132% of GDP in 2015. Two indicators which we found to have strong relationships with the Italian/German 10y spread level were Italy’s composite PMI and Italy’s news-based economic policy uncertainty index, which analyzes news article content related to the economic and policy backdrop from local newspapers. 

As shown in Exhibit 5, there has been an inverse and coincident correlation (-0.818) between Italy’s composite PMI and the Italian/German 10y spread since 2010 that would suggest continued growth deterioration in Italy’s PMIs would historically be consistent with a potentially wider spread. However, during periods of heavier ECB purchases, spread levels became less susceptible to moves in the composite PMI back towards 50 than during periods when QE was either non-existent or began to be tapered.

## **The Fog of Brexit** 

The market's strong reaction to the February MPC meeting and QIR would suggest there was some element of surprise. Gilts richened 3-5bp across the curve, led by 10y, upon the release, though some of that move was unwound during the press conference. The biggest change versus the November QIR was the downward revision to the near term growth outlook, which reflected a deterioration of both domestic and global data, compounded by the "fog of Brexit". 

The vigour and magnitude of the reaction was somewhat surprising to us, given the data deterioration has been well scrutinized and very much in the public domain. That the Bank has been held back by the “fog of Brexit” is not news either. But there is some evidence to suggest a build-up of complacence in recent weeks, particularly on the Brexit front, which the market may have been jolted out of upon the MPC release. 

Examples of complacence include the fact, as we noted, that there was a muted reaction in the gilt market to the failure of the Cooper amendment on January 29th, which would have provided the legal framework by which to extend Article 50 and avoid no deal. We attributed the sanguine reaction to the fact that Theresa May made the second vote an amendable motion, suggesting one further opportunity for such amendments to pass, among other factors. An additional sign of complacence in the market was that there was no reaction to the weak UK services PMI data earlier in the week, and gilts only outperformed after US data disappointed later in the day. 

The reason the market was so surprised by the MPC was likely down to the lack of optimism in the new growth forecast, and the idea that uncertainty in and of itself has been, and will continue to be, extremely damaging to the growth outlook. At the same time as consensus has been growing around the idea of an extension of the timeline regardless of the eventual outcome (a consensus which in and of itself could explain the complacence around the failure of the Cooper amendment), the UK economy is less and less able to weather the uncertainty, which is almost guaranteed to extend. The news around the potential for the second meaningful vote to be pushed to the end of February (or even later) simply reinforces this narrative. 

While we continue to think the cross-market underperformance of gilts through yearend will reverse going into the APF, as noted before, we do continue to think the MPC remains fundamentally hawkish – and that this may be something the market continues to underestimate, though we do not think that now is the right time to fade market pricing. We think the MPC’s underlying hawkishness came through in the press conference, where Carney’s focus on two-way risk to rates even in the tail risk scenario, as well as his emphasis on the tightness of the labour market, helped the gilt market give back some of its knee-jerk outperformance on the release of the minutes and Inflation Report. 

10

We are surprised by the view expressed by some that balance sheet normalization shouldn't have had an impact on financial conditions in December. We are also baffled by the view that the Fed should concentrate its securities portfolio in T-bills. Former New York Fed President Dudley expressed both of these views in a recent economics editorial for Bloomberg titled, "Stop Worrying About the Fed's Balance Sheet". In what follows, we explain what we think Dudley and others who espouse these views are missing and what we think they should consider instead. 

## Don't forget about leverage 

According to Dudley, "the balance sheet contraction had been underway for more than a year, without any modifications or mid-course corrections. Thus, this should have been fully discounted." Dudley continued, 

_Moreover, if anything, the run-off of the Fed’s balance sheet had a smaller-thanexpected impact on the yields of those securities. Longer-term Treasury yields remained low, and the spread between them and the yields on agency mortgagebacked securities didn’t change much. It’s hard to see how the normalization of the Fed’s balance sheet tightened financial conditions in a way that would have weighed significantly on stock prices._ 

This dichotomy – that Treasury yields and agency MBS spreads did not increase while financial conditions tightened – was also highlighted in a Wall Street Journal article by Nick Timiraos titled, "A $4 Trillion Scapegoat for Market Volatility: the Fed’s Shrinking Portfolio": 

18 

_...many economists and Fed officials, in public comments and interviews, said they don’t think the central bank’s portfolio runoff is a major culprit in the market volatility. Their evidence: Long-term interest rates have gone down of late, not up, reflecting plentiful demand for new Treasury debt. And the Fed’s bond redemptions are much smaller than the amount of new Treasury debt needed to finance rising deficits._ 

From this, it seems that "many" Fed officials take the following view: if Treasury supply (related to balance sheet normalization) didn't make Treasury yields go up, then balance sheet normalization didn't make credit spreads widen or equity prices go down. But are equity prices only supposed to react to movements in Treasury yields? Not according to research presented to the FOMC in March 2013 on the efficacy and costs of asset purchases. 

_...studies likely miss some of the effects of LSAPs on equity prices to the extent that they materialize over longer periods of time, as we think occurred following the first two rounds of LSAPs, which were undertaken when uncertainty and risk premiums were exceptionally high but subsequently moved down noticeably. The evidence also indicates that the earlier rounds of LSAPs led market participants to expect a more accommodative stance of conventional monetary policy, which could have produced a particularly strong transmission to equity prices._ 

## In other words, **if talk of continued balance sheet normalization led market participants to expect a less accommodative stance of conventional monetary policy, it could have produced a strong transmission to equity prices in December.** 

## **More importantly, the Dudley view misses a key part of what moves financial markets:** 

**the ability of investors to employ leverage.** In our view, and as we explained in Pricing in Balance Sheet Normalization, the price of assets that the central bank impacts directly can move well before the price of assets the central bank does not. For example, as expectations for Treasury supply change, based on central bank communications relating to balance sheet normalization or balance sheet expansion, prices/yields move. These movements typically occur before supply actually changes because central banks like to communicate changes well in advance. 

How do prices move beforehand? Investors can employ leverage to either buy or sell before the actual supply changes. Consider balance sheet normalization. Investors know that it means additional Treasury supply is on the way. Given more Treasury supply should put downward pressure on prices, all else equal, investors with a profit motive can borrow a bond they don't own and sell it short into the marketplace – effectively increasing the supply beforehand, which moves prices beforehand and makes the future increase in supply more easily digested (because of more attractive valuations). 

19 

If this is the case, couldn't investors also employ leverage to move corporate credit spreads and equity prices before balance sheet normalization begins? Yes, they could. But investors that employ leverage do so at great peril. They must have a greater level of confidence than usual to invest with leverage than without. That level of confidence can be captured in the probabilities investors assign to outcomes. 

As we described in Stock, Flow, and Portfolio Balance Channel Effects, when it comes to future supply in Treasuries related to expected Fed action, investors can typically assign high probabilities. If the FOMC tells you it will remove $300 billion Treasuries from the market in the next 6 months, we can assign 100% probability to that outcome (as the Fed did, and investors did, on March 18, 2009). With such a high-probability outcome expected, investors should feel very comfortable employing leverage to move prices before the supply is actually removed. 

What about the impact on corporate credit and equity markets? The outcome is less certain. Once the Fed purchases those Treasuries from the market, what happens to the money? As we discussed in A Birdie for the Balance Sheet, QE and QT can flow through balance sheets in different ways. Exhibit 23 shows that QE can work either by affecting the banking sector balance sheet only, or by affecting the nonbanking sector balance sheet. If QE affects the nonbanking sector balance sheet, which asset classes will it affect and when?

## Daunting duration risk and remittances to Treasury 

The other claim Dudley made is that the Fed should hold mostly bills to reduce its exposure to interest rate risk and increase its ability to ease policy in the future: 

_Second, on the Treasury side, what should the composition be? Before the crisis, the Fed held Treasury securities across the maturity spectrum. Now, with a much bigger balance sheet, there is more reason to shift to Treasury bills. Holding mostly bills would reduce exposure to interest rate risk and increase the firepower available to fight future economic downturns. Should quantitative easing be needed, the Fed would have greater scope to extend the maturities of its holdings, not just increase the size of its balance sheet._ 

As we discussed in We're Buyers of Bonds, the consensus also believes that the Fed should hold mostly T-bills, though we see several flaws in this idea: 

**1. Active vs. passive tools:** Reducing the duration of the SOMA portfolio is inconsistent with the Fed's desire to have investors view the balance sheet as a passive tool. Ceasing the normalization of the balance sheet size only to begin to normalize the balance sheet duration sounds like an active use of a passive tool, especially if the federal funds target rate range – the active policy tool – is inactive (which it may very well be when the Fed ceases balance sheet normalization). 

**2. Uncertain impact:** The Fed is uncertain about the impact of balance sheet normalization on the economy and financial conditions. We don't think the Fed would feel comfortable reducing the duration of its portfolio until it has a better handle on the effect of reducing its size. We don't expect the Fed to have an understanding of this for some time to come, just as it took the Fed years to understand how quantitative easing impacted the economy and markets. 

**3. MEP vs. QE:** Research from the FRB San Francisco suggested that the MEP had the smallest impact on Treasury yields among the first 3 large-scale asset purchase (LSAP) programs. In addition, research from the FRB Kansas City suggested that the MEP had the lowest implied signaling effect of the first 3 LSAPs. Why would the Fed see another MEP as a preferred option to another QE program? We don't see a reasonable case. 

**What about the duration risk of the Fed's portfolio?** Dudley makes the case for reducing it, but doesn't elaborate. We have a hard time understanding this concern, given that others on the Fed rarely, if ever, speak about it publicly. We believe Dudley's concern about interest rate risk relates to the Federal Reserve's income and remittances to Treasury. If, instead, his concerns related to unrealized losses on the Fed's securities holdings, then the Fed should have reduced its holdings before raising interest rates. 

How does duration risk relate to the Fed's income and remittances to Treasury? We will allow Jon Faust to explain it as he did to the FOMC on March 20, 2013: 

_...as the recovery progresses and interest rates rise, the Federal Reserve’s interest_ 

21 

_expense for IOER will rise. Higher interest rates will also imply unrealized capital losses on SOMA securities holdings, losses that would be realized if the assets are sold... In the event that this net income is not sufficient to cover Federal Reserve expenses, dividends, and required additions to capital, remittances to the Treasury would go to zero. In this situation, a deferred asset is booked on the Federal Reserve’s balance sheet until subsequent net income offsets the deferred asset._ 

Bond investors can think of this "deferred asset" as negative carry on the Fed's securities portfolio. If the Fed is paying more to banks on reserves (IOR) than it collects in coupon income, it will not remit anything to Treasury and declare, instead, a "deferred asset". Why is this a problem? Let's use Chairman Powell's own words in March 2013 to describe it: 

_The second major cost, again, is that of the realization of losses, low remittances, and depleted capital. And I want to say that I think this scenario captures my concern. It’s in one of the many memos. We buy another $500 billion, which gets us to $1 trillion starting on January 1, 2014. Rates are 100 basis points higher. The first part of that is a near certainty at this point, it seems to me, at least in the market’s expectation. The second is quite plausible. That gives us five years of zero remittances, $300 billion in losses, and we sprout a deferred asset of $63 billion in 2019. I’m very uncomfortable with that for the political risk reasons that have been elaborated. Fortunately, there are ways to mitigate it at no cost to policy._ 

Powell suggested that zero remittances to Treasury, unrealized losses on the Fed's securities portfolio, and the declaration of deferred assets would raise political risks. Others on the FOMC also raised these issues during their March 2013 discussion (see Alternatives for Federal Reserve Remittance Policy and Foreign Central Bank Remittance Practices). 

At the same time, and as Powell noted, there are ways to mitigate this risk "at no cost to policy". Jon Faust, at the same meeting, offered several suggestions. In summary, and truncated to account for current circumstances, they are: 

**1.** The FOMC could alter its approach to normalizing the portfolio by allowing agency MBS to run off rather than selling these securities. This would avoid realizing capital losses and help smooth income. 

**2.** The Fed could adopt a policy of retaining income now to cover future losses. Many central banks around the world use some approach like this, though "smoothing" income may not have political benefits. 

**3.** The Fed could continue its proactive communication aimed at making clear the likely net benefits of securities purchases. A number of central banks around the world have actually suffered substantial losses while succeeding in retaining (political) support because their policies were viewed as appropriate.

_...my take on political risk. I don’t think we should obsess about this risk, particularly at the expense of doing the right thing for the economy for the long term. _In terms of what we can do about these financial stability risks, I think they can be_

Most notably, the absolute level of real yields has shown the most consistent reactions to a pause, more than breakevens or real yield curves. This

The Waterfall Methodology now used for Libor submissions "requires Panel Banks to base their Libor submissions ineligible wholesale, unsecured funding transactions to the extent available". Exhibit 47 illustrates a summary of that methodology. Generally speaking, a bank that issued 3m CPs or CDs on the submission day uses the yield on those CPs for its submission, which will be considered a Level 1 submission. Otherwise, if it has a submission from the past 3 days, it can use the yield on that prior issuance for its submission (after adjusting it for moves in OIS rates or rate futures) and the submission will be defined as a level 2 submission. 

If the bank has no recent CP or CD issuance, then it resorts to Level 3, which is based on a potential list of factors listed in Exhibit 44. Some or all of these factors are used as inputs in a predetermined formula to submit Libor. 

A possible scenario is that a few banks had recent CP issuance over the past week that they had been using to set Libor using Level 1 or Level 2 approaches. However, if CPs for a number of banks fall off the allowed 3-day window, then the banks would have to switch to a Level 3 approach and there is no guarantee (as far as we know) that the Level 3 submission produced by the predetermined market-data-based formula would be close to the prior day's submission. **As a result of this dynamic, a banks submission could shift abruptly simply as a result of a shift between the methodology used. The likelihood that more than one bank would run into this situation on the same day is low; however, we would guess this likelihood increases as:** 

**1.** There is more divergence in CP yields in the market (e.g., A1+, A1 and A2-rated CPs), which is currently the case as we discussed in the UST Spreads into Year-end. 

**2.** Libor-OIS is priced to tighten over the next month or two as implied by FRAOIS futures. This is because FRAOIS futures are one eligible input into Level 3 formulas and if they are already implying an expected tightening, that tightening would materialize if enough banks are basing their submission (at least partly) on what ED futures are implying. 

**3.** The gap between the 5th and 12th submitter is wide, which as Exhibit 42 illustrates is more likely to occur when Libor-OIS is "wide". 

Aside from the technicalities that could have caused this path for 3m Libor over the past two weeks, the CP-OIS market suggests that we could see a few more bps of 3m L-OIS tightening over the coming week or two. However, as we discussed in our prior weekly, we expect funding pressures to return by March as foreign banks increase issuance and T-bill supply picks.

## **Definition of terms** 

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period. 

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period. 

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period. 

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period. 

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase. 

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease. 

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation.
