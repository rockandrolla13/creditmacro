**“The theory of buying the highest-yielding obligation of a sound company** – It follows also that if any obligation of an enterprise deserves to qualify as a fixed-value investment, then all its obligations must do so. Stated conversely, if a company’s junior bonds are not safe, its first-mortgage bonds are not a desirable fixed-income investment. For if the second-mortgage is unsafe the company itself is weak, and generally speaking there can be no high-grade obligations of a weak enterprise. _The theoretically correct procedure, therefore, is first to select a company meeting every test of strength and soundness, and then to purchase its highest yielding obligation, which would usually mean its junior rather than its first-lien bonds.”_ (emphasis added) 

Securities Analysis (2nd Edition) by Benjamin Graham and David Dodd. Published 1940. 

The notion that there are “no bad bonds, only bad bond prices” is both one of the oldest jokes in fixed income, and the closest thing our market has to a Universal Law. It is a simple, yet incredibly meritocratic idea, suggesting that all manner of investments are free to succeed or fail. At a low enough price, even the ugliest subprime mortgage bond is a great buy. At a low enough yield, bonds of the highest-quality issuer can be less desirable than cash. More often than we’d like to admit, “what something’s priced for” is more important than “what something is”. 

This notion has helped corporate hybrids come back into vogue, with the market doubling in size over the past year. Corporate hybrids often provide a way for investors to get better spreads from better companies (via a more junior ranking), a pick-up that would otherwise require taking credit risk of a much lower-quality organisation. This extra spread is even more attractive in the current environment, where bond yields and credit spreads are near their post-crisis tights. The benefits of higher back-end coupons in a rate rise scenario have also buoyed interest. 

_What_ hybrids are, though, still matters. Their subordination in a bankruptcy means recovery in default should be virtually nil. 

They proved more volatile and less liquid than vanilla bonds in the recent crisis. And importantly, given expectations of rising rates, structural features can add complexity and negative convexity. All these risks must be weighed against the optically better spreads investors get from corporate hybrids. 

In this report, we answer some frequently-asked questions from investors on hybrid market technicals, terms and structure, and valuations. Our examination indicates: 

- **Hybrids have seen attractive risk-adjusted returns in the past** (page 5), due to their relatively low volatility **but hybrid-specific compensation has compressed a lot making us less constructive today.** Hybrids may still be the preferable forms of beta in some portfolios today but there is a need to differentiate opportunities and focus on relative value **as valuations have normalised** (page 24) 

- **Dramatic growth in the size of the investable hybrids universe,** to about €43 billion in €-denominated paper, and €12 billion in £-denominated paper (page 4) 

- Perp features, structure and language can play significant parts in influencing an issuer’s decision to call; most **European hybrids have structures which incentivises issuer to call at the first call date** (page 11) 

- **Rating Event Call could pose substantial downside risk to hybrids, given most trade above their REC call price;** such calls are also susceptible to being exercised on senior ratings downgrade to HY, and/or methodology changes form rating agencies (page 15) 

- **High coupon step-ups and loss of equity benefit after the call date make hybrids highly likely to be called,** implying lower rate duration than their long maturities suggest; this means that **hybrids trading to call dates are as resilient as their bullet counterparts to yield rises** (page 19) 

Our preferred way of taking beta is through subordinated financials (see _The Outlook in Depth: Top Trades for 2014_ , December 13, 2013), but we believe that for those investors who have little headroom in their sub-fins bucket, or those who are invested in HY callables, hybrids offer a preferable way to take on beta.

## **Overview of the European Hybrid Market – Supply, Demand and Returns** 

_**How Much Issuance of Corporate Hybrids Has There Been? How Big is the Investable Universe?**_ 

- **€31 billion of European corporate hybrids issued in 2013 alone, €8 billion so far this year** 

- **Market has more than doubled in size over the past year, with the amount of €-denominated hybrids outstanding at €43 billion currently, and another €12 billion outstanding of £-denominated paper** 

_**Why Do European Corporates Issue Hybrids?**_ 

- **Hybrid instruments are typically treated as part equity by rating agencies and under IFRS, while still being tax-deductible** 

- **Companies which have limited ratings headroom, or those wishing to raise equity-like capital without diluting shareholders, could find issuing hybrids attractive** 

Hybrid issuance surged in 2013, with €31 billion of supply last year, up from €5 billion in 2012 (see Exhibit 1). The strong supply has continued this year – in January alone, we saw €8 billion of new corporate perps come to the market. 

While the majority of the supply came – as with previous years – from the Telecoms and Utilities sectors, 2013 also saw representatives from Autos (VW) and Food Retail (COFP) join the fray. We expect more core, cyclical companies could try out the hybrids market this year. 

The overall market size now stands at about €61 billion, with 70% of this (roughly €43 billion) denominated in €, and 20% (about £10 billion) denominated in £.

For corporates, hybrids provide capital whose cost (interest) may be tax-deductible, yet which can count (at least partially) as equity by rating agencies and IFRS, due to their subordination and potential for coupon deferral. Because of this benefit, **companies which have limited rating headroom, or are in need of funds without wishing to worsen leverage ratios, may see issuing hybrids as a cost-effective way of financing** , even if it is often more expensive compared to senior unsecured funding. Bonds have to pass government-based criteria to qualify for the interest deduction, while the ‘equity benefit’ lies firmly in the hands of rating agencies (see page 8). The divergence between tax treatment, accounting treatment and rating agency treatment of hybrids partly explains the myriad of structures one sees across European jurisdictions – some are dated, some not, some have cumulative language, some don’t. **It also partly explains why we’ve not seen a stream of US corporates issue hybrids** – fully meeting rating agencies’ criteria could mean losing tax-deductibility status.

## **Overview of European Hybrid Terms and Structures** 

_**What Is the Typical Structure of a Corporate Hybrid?**_ 

- **Coupon and maturity structure:** NC5, second call and reset  in Year 10, third call and reset in Year 25 

- **Coupon deferrals/ suspension:** most European hybrids have optional deferrable coupons; some have mandatory deferral language 

- **Structural subordination:** all European hybrids rank junior to senior unsecured debt 

**Coupon and maturity structure:** Historically, hybrid structures are often similar to what is found in bank Tier 1 debt – fixed coupons until a first call date (often after ten years), which step up to a Libor or swaps-linked coupon for perpetuity (or 60-100-years maturity) if not called. 

However, this structure has been gradually changing. Hybrids issued in 2010-11 generally have a fixed coupon until the first call date (five years from issuance), whereupon there is a one-off coupon reset until a second point another five years down the road (i.e., ten years from issuance). If bonds are not called at this second point, the coupon generally switches to a floating-rate coupon, with a margin of 100bp or more above the original spread at issuance.

In Europe, new hybrids issued since 2012 have evolved again. They have tended to have an _additional_ call date another 15 years after the second call (i.e., 20 years from first call date). As with previous structures, the margin on the final floating-rate leg typically is 100bp above the spread at new issue. **Coupon deferral/suspension:** In almost all cases, coupons can be deferred at the _option_ of the company. These skipped coupons typically accrue _cumulatively_ in the case of optional deferral, and generally must be paid in full – sometimes with interest – as a prerequisite for paying a dividend to common or preferred stock. As such, the historical importance of dividends to the company’s strategy, and its reliability in their payment, becomes important. Interest can typically be deferred for a substantial period (often five years) before constituting a default under the securities, although at this time they would become immediately payable by the issuer. Cumulative coupons are one of the major structural differences to the greater part of the Tier 1 space, and a driver of value difference (see p 27 for a comparison between corporate hybrids and subordinated financials). 

For some older vintages of European hybrids, _mandatory_ coupon deferral language exists. For example, the BAYNGR € 5% stipulates that interest would be suspended if the consolidated cash flows of the issuer is less than 7% of its consolidated revenues. Other hybrids, such as the HENKEL € 5.375% and VATFAL € 5.25% also have similar language. 

**Structural subordination:** All European hybrids rank junior to unsecured debt in the event of insolvency, and with no limitation on the issuance of more senior debt above the hybrid. As such, we think the most conservative assumption for recovery on a hybrid in the event of a default should be similar to equity (i.e., close to zero).

## **How Are Hybrids Generally Rated versus Seniors?** 

- **Hybrids are generally rated 2-3 notches below senior unsecured debt from the same issuer** 

Moody’s, S&P and Fitch generally rate corporate hybrids 2-3 notches below the senior unsecured debt from the same issuer, with the notchings going wider if the credit is sub-IG. 

Part of the notching stems from subordination (one notch), but part of it also comes from the risk of deferral/extension. Rating agencies’ treatments differ between securities with mandatory versus optional coupon deferrals. Mandatory is seen as potentially riskier, and therefore warrants another two notches on top of the one notch for subordination risk (i.e., three notches in total), while optional demands for just one (i.e., two notches in total). Exhibit 7 summarises the rating notchings across Moody’s, S&P and Fitch. It should be noted that, at the end of the day, the rating agencies have some discretion on final notchings for individual instrument.

_**How Are Hybrids Treated under IFRS?**_ 

- **Hybrids with specified maturity are accounted for as a financial liability** 

- **Perpetuals are accounted for as equity** 

The classification of hybrid securities under IFRS is independent of rating agencies’ treatment. It is also, for our purposes, a lot more straightforward. 

**Dated hybrids:** Security is classified as financial liability on the balance sheet throughout its life. Coupons are accounted for as interest expense. 

**Perps:** Security is accounted for as equity throughout its life. Coupons are included in financing cash flow, and are not expensed. 

Exhibits 8 and 9 show how the IFRS treatment stacks up compared to the rating agencies’ equity benefit criteria. 

_**How Are Hybrids Treated by Rating Agencies? What Is Equity Benefit?**_ 

- **Most European corporate hybrids are considered 50% equity, 50% debt by S&P, Moody’s and Fitch** 

- **Exception: hybrids from HY issuers receive 0% equity credit from Moody’s** 

- **Equity benefit falls away after a while, typically at the first call date from S&P, and various other points after for Moody’s and Fitch** 

Given that hybrids have both characteristics of debt and equity, when a corporate issues a perp, should that mean its leverage has gone up because debt has increased, or down because it is better capitalised? 

Rating agencies tend to get around this problem by treating a portion of the securities as debt and a portion as equity (the ‘equity credit’) in their financial ratio calculations. Its existence is one of the drivers of companies issuing hybrid instruments – it allows them to borrow without significantly impairing their leverage ratios or cash flow metrics as calculated by the rating agencies and, hence, offers some protection against downgrades. However, this benefit only lasts _as long as the hybrid security possesses equity-like features_ . 

Under current methodology, S&P and Fitch classify basically all rated European corporate hybrids as 50% debt and 50% equity. For Moody’s, the criteria is slightly different – corporate perps from _IG_ issuers are considered in the same way, but securities from HY companies would get 0% equity credit. If a security at some point in time no longer meets the relevant criteria – including time to maturity, whether replacement language is in place – it would be reclassified as pure debt from the rating agencies’ perspective, adding to an issuer’s leverage as calculated for rating purposes. 

We’d note that this equity benefit falls away from hybrids at different points in time according to different agencies. To make things a little more complicated, the criteria also varies depending on whether a hybrid is dated or non-dated.

- **S&P:** Hybrid loses its 50% equity credit _**20 years before the ‘effective maturity’**_ – defined as the call date at which the cumulative step-up reaches >100bp; **the point where equity benefit falls away is typically the first call date for new vintage of hybrids**

_**What Are Replacement Capital Covenants/ Replacement Intention Language?**_ 

- **Issuers signify their intention to redeem hybrids only with funds from newly issued hybrids or equity within replacement language** 

- **Legally enforceable (and hence, stronger) form called Replacement Capital Covenant** 

- **While a differentiator in pre-2012 hybrids, they hold little analytical value for ‘new-style’ European hybrids, all of which have intention-based replacement language** 

Replacement Capital Covenant (RCC) puts constraints on how an issuer could redeem its hybrids, typically by stating that it could only be done with funds from newly raised hybrid or common stock. Most European hybrids would carry a nonlegally enforceable form of replacement language, which we think offers little analytical purposes or differentiation. So why do RCCs – where they exist – matter? Because they can increase an issuer’s incentive to redeem at the first call date. 

Replacement language was initially intended to protect the issuer’s _senior_ bondholders. While the covenant is in force, corporates have an obligation to their senior creditors to preserve credit quality, such that in the face of potential calls, they must either shore up their capital base, or simply choose not to call. And replacement language in some of the older vintages kept with this spirit. European hybrids such as the SIEGR € 5.25% and LINGR € 5.25% issued pre-crisis tend to have the RCC in effect _from issuance_ , a signal of intent on the issuers’ part to keep the hybrids as, well, perpetual capital. Because of this, rating agencies have in the past treated hybrid instruments with RCC as 50% equity until the expiration of the covenant. 

Many hybrids issued in 2010 such as RWE € 4.625% and the TENN 6.655% also have RCCs in place, although these tend 

to come into effect _after_ the first call date. Rather than a signal that issuers intend to keep hybrid capital outstanding, the RCC became an incentive for companies to call at the first call date, before the covenant takes force and replacing the hybrids become more expensive. Until S&P clarified its methodology in March 2011, such securities still warranted 50% equity credit, even though they were debt-like in essence, with little uncertainty about effective maturity and cash flows. 

The newest European hybrids (those issued since 2012) don’t carry the legally enforceable RCC. Rather, they all have replacement intention language from issuance, which only holds if the senior rating of the issuer is below a certain level. Because of the ubiquity of such language in new-styled European hybrids, and the fact that it’s not legally enforceable, replacement intention serves little analytical purpose or differentiation for this latest vintage.

## _**What Are Special Event Calls?**_ 

- **Special Event Calls give issuers the option to redeem a hybrid early, usually when the perp no longer functions as intended at issuance** 

- **These include: Rating Event Calls, Change-of-Control Calls, Accounting Event Calls, Gross-Up Calls, Withholding Tax Event Calls and Clean-Up Calls** 

European corporates have issued hybrids because the instruments are accounted for as capital fully or partially by rating agencies and under IFRS, yet whose cost (interest) is usually tax-deductible. If any one of these factors changes, the hybrid could potentially become an expensive form of senior debt for the issuer, giving them a big incentive to redeem the bond before its first call date via Special Event Calls (SECs). **In effect, SECs are call options sold to the issuer by the investor to give corporates protection in how hybrids are treated.**

There are six main types of Special Event Calls contained in European corporate hybrid prospectuses: 

**1) Rating Event Calls:** Also known under ‘Capital Event’ or ‘Rating Methodology Event’ calls. **These give issuers the option to redeem a hybrid early,** _**if the equity benefit of the instrument allowed by rating agencies deteriorates (subject to conditions)**_ **.** Similar language also exists for the subordinated financials space, as our banks credit research team has long flagged. See page 15 for a more in-depth examination on the topic. 

**2) Change-of-Control Calls:** As a subordinated claim with no protection against the addition of senior debt above, hybrids are particularly vulnerable to a leveraging event, such as an 

LBO. To address this, about 34% of European corporate hybrids contain the option for the issuer to redeem if a Change-of-Control (CoC) Event has occurred, commonly at par. If not called after a CoC Event, the coupon would step up immediately by 500bp. Unlike the other types of Special Events Calls in hybrids which typically benefit the issuers, **CoC calls can be thought of as providing protection to hybrid holders in a leveraging scenario.** 

What investors should be aware of is what constitutes a CoC Event. As the name suggests, a CoC Event generally comprises: 

- some sort of **change in ownership** – the exact definition of which would typically be with reference to takeover laws of the issuers’ domestic jurisdiction, AND 

- a **ratings downgrade or withdrawal** , within a certain window from the CoC, that is the result of the CoC; if a deterioration in rating is unconnected to the CoC, then the CoC call would not be triggered. 

There are some variations to the above though. For example, the TENN and VOEAV hybrids require only a CoC without any subsequent rating actions for the option to call to be triggered. Moreover, while for the majority of hybrids with CoC language, a downgrade/withdrawal from _any_ rating agency would be enough, for some – for example, the KPN hybrids, negative action from a _majority_ of agencies is needed for the CoC call to be triggered. 

**3) Accounting Event Calls:** Accounting Event call gives issuers the option to redeem if a hybrid is no longer classified as “equity” or “financial liability” under IFRS, or the applicable accounting standards. The majority of hybrids with this type of call are redeemable at 101. 

**4 and 5) Gross-Up/ Withholding Tax Event Calls:** Most European hybrids contain a Gross-Up Event call, sometimes known as a Withholding Tax Event. In essence **, it gives issuers the option to redeem early if they have to pay additional amounts to hybrid holders to make up for any taxes withheld or deducted due to changes in tax legislation.** 

**6) Clean-Up Calls:** Clean-up call, otherwise known as Substantial Repurchase Event Call, **allows issuers the option to redeem when the amount outstanding of a hybrid drops below a certain level, typically about 10-25% of the amount originally issued.** Nearly 90% of corporate perps have such call language; the majority is redeemable at 100, with the rest evenly split between callable at 101 or max of principal or Make-Whole.

- _**If An Issuer Is Junked, Could its Hybrid Be Called at 101?**_ 

- **For hybrids with a Rating Event Call, if an issuer is junked** _**by Moody’s at the senior level**_ **, the REC could be triggered, and bond called at 101** 

Moody’s changed its hybrids criteria in July 2013 such that perps from HY issuers would no longer receive equity benefit. One recurring question investors have asked on the back of this change has been **‘If an issuer gets junked by Moody’s, could the hybrid be called at 101?’** The answer, as with many things hybrids-related, is dependent on the vintage. 

**Hybrids with no REC, typically issued pre-2007 – No:** For this class of hybrids, an issuer getting junked would have no implications on early redemption. 

**Older hybrids issued before 2010 – Yes:** Many of these hybrids have REC language indifferent to the reasons for a deterioration in equity benefit. As long as the hybrid is no longer accounted for as partial capital, whether it be from an upgrade or a downgrade, REC is triggered, and the issuer could potentially redeem the hybrid before the first call date. 

**Hybrids issued after 2012, pre-July 2013 – Yes:** Given that Moody’s methodology change occurred _after_ these hybrid’s issue date, if an issuer is junked and its hybrid loses equity content according to Moody’s new methodology, it would trigger a call (e.g., TITIM’s call) 

**Hybrids issued after July 2013 –** _**Generally**_ **Yes:** European hybrids issued since Moody’s criteria change typically include **REC language that takes into account of Moody’s revised criteria** (e.g., ALLRNVs and TELEFOs). Under this type of language, if an issuer rated by Moody’s is downgraded to high yield, and the hybrid loses its equity content as a result of this, REC is triggered. An issuer could then potentially redeem the hybrid before its first call date at 101. 

## **Recent Hybrid Methodology Changes and Impact** 

**S&P:** S&P refined its methodology on assigning equity credit in April 2013, making it more difficult for **new and existing** hybrid instruments to attain ‘high’ equity content – i.e., hybrid is treated as 100% equity (see _Assigning Equity Content to Corporate Entity and North American Insurance Holding Company Hybrid Instruments_ , April 1, 2013, from S&P). 

This has had a limited impact on the overall European hybrids market, as almost all corporate perps had been classified as having ‘intermediate’ equity content – i.e., accounted for as 50% debt and 50% equity by S&P – and so were not affected by the new criteria. Of the three € hybrids which saw a deterioration in equity benefit – DONGAS € 7.75, and the Australian ORGAU € 7.875 and STOAU € 8.25 – only the DONGAS has been redeemed early. 

**Moody’s:** Moody’s revised in July 2013 its approach for determining equity benefit for **hybrids from companies which are rated high yield at the senior level** , effectively making these securities eligible for 0% equity benefit as long as the issuer remains sub-investment grade (see _Debt and Equity Treatment for Hybrid Instruments of Speculative-Grade Non-Financial Companies,_ July 31, 2013, from Moody’s). High yield companies which are upgraded to investment grade would see the equity content of their hybrids assessed under the existing ‘Moody’s Hybrid Tool Kit’. Investment grade companies which are downgraded would see equity benefit decline to zero. **Note that even if a** _**hybrid**_ **is high yield, as long as its** _**issuer’s senior rating is investment grade**_ **, it would not be impacted by this methodology change.** 

Given that the majority of European hybrids have been issued by investment grade companies, the criteria change only impacted two European hybrids from high yield issuers (MTNA $ 8.75 and WIEAV € 6.5). MTNA $ 8.75 were called in January this year. 

For those hybrids issued after July 2013 which _don’t_ have explicit language covering a Moody’s downgrade, RECs could still be triggered. The RECs within many of these hybrids typically don’t stipulate the criteria has to have occurred _**after**_ the hybrid’s issue date (e.g., AMXLMM and COFP). If a hybrid loses its equity content in the future, due to a methodology change that happened in the past before the hybrid’s existence, this could theoretically still trigger a call at 101. 

In our view, given that RECs are included in hybrids for the benefit of the issuer, **investors should assume that when a corp perp loses its equity content, the issuer will exercise the call at some point in time if the hybrid is trading above the call price.**

## **Hybrid Valuation** 

_**How Should Investors Value Corporate Hybrids? How Should Investors Interpret a Hybrid’s Yield-to-Worst?**_ 

- **A hybrid’s yield-to-worst can be broken down into a number of spread components – rates risk, senior risk, subordination risk, and ‘hybrid compensation’** 

- **Hybrid compensation represents the spread investors are paid for taking on hybrid-specific risks; it can also be viewed as the spread that investors get for selling different call options to the issuer** 

- **Given two hybrids from similar issuers, with similar call dates, the perp with higher hybrid compensation holds more value** 

One way of analysing this is to focus on spreads, in essence, **decomposing a hybrid’s yield-to-worst (YTW).** Since a hybrid theoretically has several possible ‘yields’, depending on when it’s redeemed, YTW provides a conservative approve by representing the lowest (i.e., worst) yield across all possible scenarios. We’ll focus on one hybrid, GSZFP € 3.875%, which has structure and terms common to most European hybrids issued post 2012 to build up these metrics, and then expand this approach to the broader universe.

**Senior risk:** Spreads on senior unsecured bonds of the same issuer indicate the compensation investors get for taking on bullet, non-deferrable, senior unsecured credit risk. Where cash spreads are unavailable, we use CDS spreads as a 

proxy, adjusting for the basis between cash and CDS. Interpolated cash spreads to 2018 (the first call date, and the time to ‘worst’ yield of the bond) comes to 36bp, and is denoted by the light yellow bar in Exhibit 19. 

**Subordination risk:** If investors take up subordination, but do not take other risks, where would the credit trade? We think that the ratio of reward (spreads) should be similar to the ratio of risk (loss given default). Assuming that senior unsecured loses 65% of par in a default (i.e., 35% recovery), and that deeply subordinated risk loses 100%, subordinated risk should trade 100%/65% = 53% wider. In the case of GSZFP, this would suggest that 4.5-year subordinated risk, with 0% expected recovery, would trade at around 20bp, as shown by the orange bar in Exhibit 19. 

**Compensation for hybrid-specific risks:** GSZFP € 3.875% hybrids trade at a spread (to worst) of ~215bp over swaps, while its senior unsecured bonds would suggest that the risk based solely on default and recovery in these securities should cost ~56bp. The 159bp difference, in our view, represents the compensation investors are receiving for the hybrid’s other risks. **Theoretically, if two hybrids from** 

**issuers with similar credit profiles have similar call dates, the security with the higher hybrid compensation should hold more value.** But what kind of risks are investors being paid for? 

_**Coupon Deferral Risk:**_ Hybrid coupons can be suspended in a period of extreme stress, yet this is a relatively small risk in terms of likelihood of occurrence or the impact on yields. Corporate hybrid coupons are generally deferrable and cumulative, meaning that skipped coupons must be paid in full before dividend payments can resume. For many perps, the deferred coupons also bear interest such that the economic impact on bond yields for issuer or investors if coupons are deferred is minimal. 

_**Duration Extension Risk:**_ Hybrids can be redeemed on specified dates **at the option of issuers** , meaning that investors are exposed to potential non-call risk. See page 11 for more on this topic. 

_**Special Event Calls Risk:**_ Apart from _non-_ call risk, hybrid investors also suffer from early call risk. Special Event Calls which allow issuers to redeem a hybrid early under certain circumstances, before its first call date, capping potential upside. In effect, ‘hybrid compensation’ is the spread received for selling early call options to the issuer. See page 14 for more on Special Event Calls.

_**How Sensitive Are Hybrids to Changes in Yields? What Factors Impact This Sensitivity?**_ 

- **Corporate hybrids have lower duration than their maturities would suggest, as many trade to call date** 

- **Call schedules and coupon resets influence a perp’s sensitivity to yield changes** 

Corporate hybrids have extremely long final maturities, but, in our view, the terms and structure of a corporate hybrid can, in certain cases, leave it with lower duration than one might otherwise expect. In fact, corporate hybrids tend to have duration comparable to bonds in the 3-5y bucket, even as their yields are similar to bonds in the 10y+ bucket (see Exhibit 20). We emphasise the phrase “in certain cases”. Hybrid duration (how much a change in yield affects price) and convexity (how much a change in yield affects duration) can vary meaningfully.

Two properties of hybrids can cause their sensitivity to changes in yields to differ greatly from traditional long-dated debt: 

_**1) The call schedule:**_ Hybrids are generally callable, meaning that at times of low market yields (as we see today), yield-to-call (YTC) will be substantially lower than yield-tomaturity (YTM) for a large portion of the universe. The market, in turn, will price the bond based on the worst of these outcomes (yield-to-worst, or YTW), which in many cases means pricing to a shorter-dated and shorter-duration YTC. 

_**2) Coupon resets:**_ Corporate hybrid coupons generally reset after the first call date. The terms are often substantially different from the ‘resets’ that exist in bank capital, linking the new coupon to a fixed spread with the prevailing 5-year swap rate at the time of the call. Because the new coupon resets based on where interest rates are in the future, the bond enjoys a natural interest rate hedge (see page 7). 

_**What Happens to Corporate Hybrids if Yields Rise?**_ 

- **Rise in hybrid yield for perps trading to call would see price fall – but impact is similar to that of yield move on bullet bond with same coupon and maturity equal to hybrid’s call date** 

- **Rise in rates expectations benefits hybrid investors as it pushes out YTW, meaning it would take a larger increase in hybrid yield until perp starts to see duration extends** 

When investors ask what happens to hybrids when yields rise, they can mean two things – how perp price reacts to changes in the hybrid’s own yield, or how perp price reacts to shifts in rates more generally. In both instances, **hybrids trading to call dates are as resilient as their bullet counterparts to yield changes.** Let’s walk through both scenarios. In the examples below, we use GSZFP € 3.875% for illustrative purposes, given it has structure and terms that are similar to those of most European hybrids issued after 2012.

## Exhibit 32 

_**How Do Hybrids Compare Against Long-End Bullets?**_ 

- **Frame hybrids as a long-dated subordinated bonds with options sold to the issuer; hybrids’ spread-toperp should always trade at least as wide as a longdated subordinated bond** 

- **If investors are not being paid for the early-call options sold to the issuer, switches into a long-dated senior bullet may make sense** 

While for most part of the past two years, hybrids have tended to be the highest-yielding obligation of companies, steeper curves mean that investors can often find long-dated senior bullets with similar yield levels as perps from the same issuer. So how should investors evaluate hybrids against long-dated bullets? Similar way to how one always look at a hybrid – by decomposing a perps’ yield, this time, focusing on yield-tomaturity (YTM) instead. 

The logic is simple. Long-dated senior bullets have higher potential recovery, less cash flow uncertainty and no negative convexity. Hybrids have lower potential recovery, higher cash flow uncertainty, and vulnerable to negative convexity. If we analyse a hybrid as an extremely long-dated subordinated bond with a number of options sold to the issuer (which leads to the cash flow uncertainty and negative convexity), a 

**hybrid’s YTM should always trade wider than a (hypothetical) long-dated subordinated debt, as investors should require some spread to compensate them for these options sold to the issuer.** If this sounds familiar to our readers, it should. All we are really saying is that Hybrid Compensation _at perp_ should be greater than zero. See page 18 for more on hybrid compensation.

It also means that any ENELIM £ hybrid should trade _at least_ as wide as 366bp on a spread-to-perp basis.

## _**How Do Hybrids Compare Against Financial Tier 1s?**_ 

- **Financial Tier 1s offer a better way of taking on beta than corporate hybrids, in our view, driven by fundamentals, regulatory technical and valuations** 

Corporate hybrids share some broad structural similarities with financial Tier 1 – subordination, long-dated/ perpetual, calls, etc. Rather than provide a full analysis of their similarities, we choose to focus on some important differences, and why we think financial hybrids offer a better way to take on beta than via their corporate counterparts. 

**Deleveraging:** European banks have continued to delever, while corporates have started to see fundamental improvements stalling. The divergence in fundamentals should lead to financials outperformance, in our view (see _IG Fundamentals: Financials Beating Corporates, Closing Peripheral O/W_ , January 17, 2014). 

**Regulatory technicals:** European banks see high incentive to call step-up Tier 1s from a regulatory perspective, post EBA’s FAQ, which clarified on how these instruments would lose all regulatory value at their first call date (see _Step Tier 1: Death of an Asset Class_ , December 19, 2013). Corporate hybrids face no such regulator argument to encourage a call at the first call date. While coupon step-ups and reputational risk encourage such action, these forces seem weaker than what will be driving Tier 1. 

**Valuation:** Last but not the least, bank Tier 1s still trade roughly 80-100bp wider in € and £ to corporate hybrids (see Exhibit 33), using yield-to-call, despite the advantages noted above.

_**How Do Hybrids Compare Against High Yield Callable Paper?**_ 

- **Calls embedded in many European HY paper constrain upside** 

- **Can be compared to hybrids using convexity measures such as upside/downside ratio** 

- **Investors should switch out of HY bonds with low upside/downside ratios (poor convexity) to betterrated hybrids with higher upside/downside ratios (good convexity)** 

Corporate hybrids are not the only securities which are vulnerable to negative convexity in the non-financials space – callable high yield paper is also constrained on the upside by embedded calls. The best way, in our view, to compare the two assets would be to focus on potential negative convexity, as measured by the upside/downside ratio. 

The key risk facing HY bonds today is high dollar prices and the associated negative convexity from pricing to a call date. Hybrids, on the other hand today, are much more likely to be called on the call date. There are incentives for the hybrid to be called including step-ups, loss of equity benefit, etc., which minimise extension risk. In the case of HY, the lack of such incentives makes the decision to call largely an economic decision. This makes the downside asymmetric compared to for hybrids. The one flipside, however, is that the hybrid also has the risk of being called immediately due to Rating Event Calls. 

Given the potential surprise downside faced by HY investors from embedded calls, we think investors should focus on the convexity profile of HY callables versus hybrids. The upside/downside ratio – the ratio of hybrid returns between a 100bp decrease and increase in yield – is one such way of measuring convexity. The higher this ‘upside/downside ratio’, the better the convexity profile. 

Overall, we prefer to be in select Hybrids over HY callables pricing to a call date (see _High Yield Mailbag_ , February 7, 2014). Apart from the issue of convexity, fundamentally, in a weaker growth environment, taking subordinated risk on a high quality credit using hybrids is preferable to taking unsecured exposure on a weak credit. Valuation-wise, hybrids offer better yields, better ratings for similar maturities today.

## Conclusions 

The European corporate hybrid space is very different from what it looked like a year ago – the market has grown larger, issuers more diverse, and hybrid structure and language have also become (slightly) more homogenised. While it remains the only space where one can find IG paper at >3% yields from non-financial, non-cyclical companies, with relatively low volatility, we have to acknowledge that the returns an investor gets for taking on hybrid-specific risks are compressed (see Exhibit 34). We are less constructive on the asset class as a result. 

We think hybrids might still be a preferable form of beta for certain portfolios – HY investors facing call constraints or bank subordinated investors facing allocation limits. Otherwise, in our view, relative value strategies will become increasingly important for investors looking to eke out returns in this space. From our analysis, these include: 

- Relative value within the perps space; look for hybrids offering above-average hybrid compensation 

- Switching out of selective hybrids into long-end senior bullets 

- Switching out of selective hybrids into selective Tier 1s 

- Switching out of HY callables into hybrids.
