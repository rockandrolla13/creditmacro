## CRR2 Agreed; ADI Boost for AT1 

We now have the CRR2 final text which should enter into force in June. Grandfathering of non-compliant capital instruments runs to mid-2025, which at current levels now becomes a tailwind for AT1 valuations, in our view. At last the ADI definition will be changed – a relief for Deutsche.

**The Council has approved the capital and resolution package,** which includes the CRR2 (capital regulation) and the BRRD2 (directive on MREL). Parliament should approve this in the week of April 15, and we expect Entry into Force (20 days after publication in the Official Journal of the EU) in June. Implementation is two years thereafter, but there are exceptions. While most things are as expected and as detailed in our Outlook chapter on CRR2, a few surprises came up. 

## **Grandfathering for capital instruments set as Entry into Force plus six years,** 

taking us to around June 2025 (Article 494b, p531). The possibility of a far shorter grandfathering period was a real concern for much of last year, as non-CRR2compliant AT1s traded well above par. Clearly with the market having now repriced, this is a tailwind, in our view. Most non-CRR2-compliant AT1s shown in the Exhibits below have call dates before June 2025, and we believe that banks will be more likely to call such bonds (that do not meet current regulatory definitions) at the first call date. In any case we have a 'final call date' of June 2025, taking any fears of real perpetuity away. In contrast to this grandfathering, and as expected, SPV-issued debt capital will be compliant only until December 31, 2021 (Article 494a). 

**MREL will be grandfathered until maturity in most cases:** Bonds issued prior to the entry into force of CRR2 will be allowed to qualify as MREL with no end date, even if they do not meet all the criteria in Article 72(b), which sets out the rules for eligible liabilities. However, this doesn't allow universal eligibility for all outstanding debt since a few requirements must still be met, such as being directly issued, subordinated (albeit only to the extent subordination requirements apply) and not secured. This is as expected and clearly rules out the potential for large volumes of bail-in debt losing MREL eligibility – however, we expect the SRB to keep pressure on the banks to improve the subordination level of their MREL, as noted below. 

**The 8% MREL subordination requirement will only apply to G-SIBs:** The Council's version of CRR2 stated that resolution authorities should ensure that banks with total assets over €100 billion would require 8% of total liabilities and own funds in a subordinated format, which the European Parliament proposed revising down to 6.75% (in line with TLAC). The final BRRD2 landed at 8% subordination for total liabilities and own funds for G-SIBs only (Article 45(b)), with non-G-SIB banks with assets greater than €100 billion set far lower, at 13.5% RWAs or 5% of leverage exposure (Article 45(c)). **As expected, regulatory capital instruments can only be written under EU law, or have legally enforceable contractual provisions which recognise EU writedown powers** (Articles 52 (AT1 - page 72 of the CRR2, paragraph (q)) and Article 63 (Tier 2 - page 78, paragraph (o)). As noted above, these will be grandfathered until June 2025 – in the Exhibits below, we also include English law bonds without contractual recognition of write-down powers, considering Brexit. Also for debt capital (p72 for AT1s, for example), the instruments cannot be subject to set-off or netting arrangements. On page 11, 21(d) of the recitals (and as we noted in our Outlook), this requirement is not intended as contractual. Hence, the relevance of having waiver of set-off rights in a capital instrument will depend on the applicable insolvency law of that country. In some instances, setoff may actually not be operable, which makes the presence of a clause redundant in any case. This means that even if an AT1 or Tier 2 has no contractual waiver of set-off rights, the national insolvency law may mean that there are no set-off rights to begin with – hence the bond is still compliant with CRR2. We can reduce the list of 'set-off' bonds worthy of checking though by ignoring those that have both no waiver of set-off rights and a law problem (Exhibit 1). 

## **ADI calculations should change for German and Austrian banks, in time for** 

**2020 coupons,** earlier than expected – see p40 definition change and p559 implementation on entry into force. Instead of a previous version of the change altering Article 52 (l) which defines AT1 (from stating distributions are 'paid out of distributable items', to 'paid out of distributable items, or reserves built under national law'), instead the definition change comes in the recitals which go at the front of the regulation itself. Looking at Article 3 (a), page 559, the provisions of many parts of the CRR2 apply from Entry into Force, including 'the provisions related to own funds' – a phrase which has been added into this final text. Hence the ADI calculation will change in June of this year, in good time for 2020 AT1 coupons and beyond – adding ~€42 billion to Deutsche's ADI (using FY17 parent bank GAAP numbers; FY18 expected next month). 

## **The European Parliament's accelerated timescale to meet the leverage** 

**requirement has been dropped:** A minimum leverage ratio of 3% must be met by January 1, 2022, giving banks an extra two years to achieve this compared to the parliament's proposal of two years earlier (Article 92(d)). As expected, G-SIBs will have to hold a leverage buffer calculated at 50% of their published RWA G-SIB buffer, although CRR2 has stopped short of requiring leverage buffers for O-SIIs. One development on the leverage ratio is the introduction of distribution restrictions for banks that breach their leverage capital requirements, similar to those that exist on RWA-based capital requirements, which has been introduced by Article 141(b) of CRDV and must be transposed by member states 18 months after Entry into Force. This means that investors will need to be aware of MDA restrictions on two different capital ratios going forward, and is a negative for the gap to action of those banks that have relatively light leverage capital buffers.
