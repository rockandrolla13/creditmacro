## For Whom the Tariffs Toll 

We see increasing downside risks to the economy and global rates from rising trade tensions. We continue to see the most upside in US duration, with the biggest risk being headlines that make a trade deal seem more likely at the June 28-29 G20 summit. We would favor buying such a dip. 

## **Government Bonds** 

In the US, we remain bullish on the Treasury market and would buy any dip driven by hopes of a trade deal into the June 28-29 G20 summit. In the euro area, we discuss market volatility in Italy, the near-term outlook for Bund yields and the inversion of the euribor curve. In the UK, we stay cautious through Brexit uncertainty; over the medium term, we expect the MPC to express its underlying hawkish bias upon resolution. In Japan, we have our JGB yield outlook and maintain our bullish view for the long end. 

## **Inflation** 

We present our mid-year outlook for global linker markets. In the US, we expect real yields to continue their decline, while breakevens could recover slightly. In Europe, we expect breakevens to recover from their lows. In the UK, we expect a tail-risk premium to continue to be priced in the asset class until Brexit is resolved, after which point we expect breakevens to decline and real yields to rise. For Japan, given rapidly changing political conditions, we present our outlook under two separate scenarios (base case and risk).

## **United States** 

In the past two weeks, what we thought would develop into a range trading environment quickly turned into a classic buy-the-dip trade. We suggested investors buy the dip toward 2.40% on the 2y note and 2.60% on the 10y note, and had expected to suggest investors liquidate those positions toward the bottom of the range. However, given developments on the tariff front, we no longer think it makes sense to return to an equal weight, or neutral stance, on duration. We would buy any dip driven by hopes of a trade deal into the June 2829 G20 summit in Osaka, Japan. 

At this point, we still see risks to the economic outlook skewing to the downside, given trade tensions, and we do not see these tensions abating in the near term. In fact, our public policy strategists see risks skewed toward another tariff hike on remaining imports from China. Of course, the Treasury market now prices nearly a full 50bp rate cut from the Fed over the next 12 months. So isn't that pricing in this downside risk? We don't think so. Our economists believe the Fed would open an easing cycle with a 50bp rate cut if the higher tariffs remain in place for 3 to 4 months, let alone if tariffs are placed on the remaining goods imported from China. 

So, if the Fed were to cut rates 50bp in September, that would leave 8 months of the 12 month period with no other moves priced, versus where the market is priced today (a 50bp cut in the next 12 months). And when we look back at every “first” rate cut the Fed delivered in the past, the next rate cut came an average of 13 weeks later, or 3 months later. And the next cut after that? An average of 5 weeks later. The point is this: if the Fed actually delivers a rate cut this year, and it’s a 50bp cut, the Treasury market is still well underpriced for what the Fed may eventually deliver.

## **United Kingdom** 

We outline our outlook for gilt yields, expanding on previous work. We also explore supply and demand dynamics in the UK. With Brexit uncertainty set to prevail for longer than formerly envisioned, and with growth likely to remain subtrend for longer, we expect gilt yields to remain capped for much of 2019. Our economists expect a soft deal to be ratified towards the end of 2019, upon which the MPC will shift its rhetoric to reflect its underlying hawkish bias. We expect nominal yields to rise more quickly into 2020. 

The supply picture is likely to support gilts in the near to medium term, as net supply is at a local low, even as gross issuance has increased. The source of the increase in the funding requirement is primarily redemptions. Going forward, we continue to expect a reduced proportion of linker issuance, which will continue to go into shorts and mediums, supporting our flattening bias over the medium term (although we expect the rate expectations channel to be more important here). 

On the demand side, foreigners continued to purchase gilts since the referendum, while domestic investors have sold. Given that the pension deficit on an s179 basis has closed, the ability to take advantage of the funding picture to de-risk further could keep the term structure supported over the medium term. 

## **Japan** 

In Japan, we detail our JGB outlook for the next 12 months. We see JGB yields continuing to bull-flatten, led by the long end, on the back of strong yield-hunting demand from domestic real money players, and we maintain our JGB20s40s flattener. We also see the risk for yields skewed to the downside (our bull case). 

Our bull-case scenario for the JGB market is based on a bear-case scenario for the economy in which an escalation of US-China trade tensions causes the US and broader global economy to fall into recession. In such case, our economists expect the Fed to cut the rates by 50bp initially to cushion the impact on growth. Accordingly, we expect the JPY yield curve to be under further bull-flattening pressure led by the long end. 

We also expect long-end swaps to outperform JGBs under such a scenario. If the UST yield curve were to steepen as a consequence of a Fed rate cut, then (depending on the size of that cut) life insurers could conceivably start to lose interest in super-long JGBs in favor of JPY-hedged UST or MBS positions. 

Given that rate cut expectations are unlikely to take hold in Japan given their side effects, we expect foreigners could end up crowding into long-end JPY swap receiving positions. Therefore we take off our 30y ASW long positions. 

We shift our 2y2y paying positions to 1y1y paying positions for better rolldown. We still see the short-end swap yield curve inversion being corrected eventually (based on our belief that the BoJ has very limited room to cut rates).

_High frequency data such as daily power coal consumption MTD suggests continued growth softness in May. ## Watch closely the probability of a 50bp cut in the next 6 months 

As a result of rising trade tensions, the Treasury market now prices nearly a full 50bp rate cut from the Fed over the next 12 months. Isn't that overly aggressive? If the Fed doesn't cut rates this year, then that pricing seems aggressive, indeed. But what if the Fed does cut rates this year? Our economists believe the Fed would open an easing cycle with a 50bp rate cut if the higher tariffs remain in place for 3 to 4 months, let alone if tariffs are placed on the remaining goods imported from China. 

Let’s say the Fed cuts 50bp in September, given ongoing trade tensions and a tightening of financial conditions that results. Versus where the market is priced today (a 50bp cut in the next 12 months), that would leave 8 months of the 12 month period with no other moves priced. When we look back at every “first” rate cut the Fed delivered in the past, the next rate cut came an average of 13 weeks later, or 3 months later. And the next cut after that? An average of 5 weeks later. 

The point is this: if the Fed actually delivers a rate cut this year, and it’s a 50bp cut – consistent with what our US economists think can happen in one of the tariff-related downside risk cases (see US Economics Mid-Year Outlook: Waiting Patiently) - the Treasury market is still well underpriced for what the Fed may eventually deliver. 

Of course, the market underpricing an easing cycle would not be unusual. As you can see below, fed funds futures continuously underpriced the last two easing cycles considerably (see Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6 which shows the pricing of the fed funds futures markets the day before each FOMC meeting in each cycle). In effect, the market incorporated a positive _ex-ante_ term premium that, if investors expected the Fed to cut more aggressively and positioned accordingly, was monetized. 

At this point, we prefer to position to monetize this yet-to-be-realized positive term premium, given the downside risks that exist today, and our view of the Fed's reaction function to downside vs. upside surprises, i.e., an aggressive rate cut vs. a patient approach to the next hike.

However, news that the US planned to delay auto tariffs by at least 180 days on both Europe and Japan offset a portion of the continued deterioration in global sentiment from the breakdown in US/China trade discussions, leading to a near-immediate reversal in risk markets, including BTPs, resulting in the spread tightening back throughout the remainder of the day. 

By the end of the week, 10y BTPs closed only marginally wider to 10y Bunds relative to where they closed the week prior, despite a close-to-full unwind of Friday’s tightening during the last hour of the session, following a late-day headline suggesting that the EU would write to Italy on debt clarifications after the EU Parliamentary vote.

## **Bunds: Approaching a historically challenging time of the year to be short duration** 

10y Bund yields declined to their lowest level of the year, briefly touching -0.13% during the week, bringing the month-to-date decline in May to roughly 17bp. Thus far, the decline in Bund yields has mostly kept pace with the decline in 10y US Treasuries, while Bunds have outperformed Canadian 10s and more recently underperformed UK 10s. 

Relative to a broader and equally weighted basket of US/UK/Canadian 10s, Bunds have been fairly subdued, with the spread trading within a 4bp range since mid-April. In other words, Bunds have been along for the ride, but have done little in the way of displaying leadership in either direction versus a broader basket of global bonds during this latest global duration rally. 

Some technical indicators such as RSIs suggest that 10y Bund yields have declined to a level that would historically be consistent with an oversold yield condition, with the signals conveniently occurring simultaneous with a break of a multi-year low in yield. On a cross market basis, 10y Bunds are the first market to have technically reached these oversold conditions on measures such as RSIs, while 10y Treasuries, Gilts, and Canadian 10s have yet to reach them. 

However, technically oversold yield indicators alone may not be enough to justify a significant move higher in 10y Bund yields, and we suggest that investors looking to fade recent Bund strength do so only tactically. 

While we acknowledge that our base case remains that that 10y Bund yields will ultimately move higher in the back half of the year, particularly as inflation begins to bottom and then potentially re-accelerate to levels above the ECB’s current expectations for both 2019 year-end core (which surprised to the upside in the in final reading at 1.3% YoY) and headline, the near-term outlook remains filled with negative developments in the types of ‘pervasive uncertainty’ that have contributed to the ECB’s downside risks to growth. 

Furthermore, Bunds (and US Treasuries) are about to enter a period of the year where seasonality has historically been favourable for further yield declines. ## Mid-Year JGB yield Outlook (Base case) 

The BoJ adjusted its forward guidance to make it much clearer in the April monetary policy meeting. Now it has stated that it will maintain the current extremely low levels of short- and long-term interest rates for an extended period of time, at least through around spring 2020. Given the subdued inflation outlook, our economists also expect the forward guidance to be pushed back to “at least through fall 2020” in early 2020. 

Although our economists expect the BoJ eventually to move from NIRP to ZIRP in Oct 2020, clear forward guidance and the subdued actual core CPI numbers should keep rates expectations well anchored below 0% over our forecast horizon, in our view. Hence, fluctuations in term premium led by supply/demand and overseas yield development will likely continue to drive the market. 

From a supply/demand perspective, we believe that Japanese lifers continue to be channeled into super-long JGBs, which are the only ones able to provide positive yields in the JGB curve. Our US rates outlook suggests that FX-hedged USD bonds continue to be less attractive for them over the forecast horizon. Given that European bonds and non-FX hedged foreign bonds cannot pick up all the slack, we expect the JGB yield curve to face further flattening pressure, with the 40y sector continuing to outperform on the curve. **Our top trade is still JGB 20s40s flattener.** 

Favorable supply/demand coupled with our lower UST outlook suggests that JGB yields will go lower further into 2Q2020. We expect 30y JGB to end at 0.45% in 2Q2020. As for short- to medium-term JGB yields, we expect them to be well anchored below the level of short-term policy rates, thanks to the sustainable demand from overseas investors who take advantage of deeply negative xccy basis on the belly of the curve.

**bond issuance for the next fiscal year,** thereby not leading to an increase in calendarbase issuance. The main threats to this outlook would be: (1) a rise in US interest rates if stronger-than-expected US economic performance causes the Fed to turn more hawkish and/or; (2) a rise in rates expectations due to a weakening of the BoJ's forward guidance commitment in response to an unanticipated quickening of domestic inflation. ## **Bull case for the JGB market** 

Our bull-case scenario for the JGB market is based on a bear-case scenario for the economy in which an escalation of US-China trade tensions causes the US and broader global economy to fall into recession. Our economists expect the government to adopt an expansionary fiscal stance under this scenario. 

18 

However, so-called "calendar base" market issuance of JGBs should not increase so significantly as to generate major selling pressure, given that the government should basically be able to make any necessary adjustments by reducing the size of frontloaded issuance of refunding bonds for the next fiscal year. 

Our economists would also expect the BoJ to extend its policy commitment (via a further rewording of its forward guidance) and deploy additional monetary easing measures. However, **they anticipate that a lowering of the short-end policy rate deeper** 

**into negative territory would be deemed too harmful to financial institutions** , and that the next step is therefore likely to entail "qualitative" expansion such as increased purchases of TOPIX-linked ETFs and other risk assets. JGBs would thus stand to benefit to only a limited degree from additional monetary accommodation (see What If The C- Tax Hike is Postponed?).

## **Bear case for the JGB market**

JGB yields would likely face gradual upward pressure under this scenario, but we see little danger of rates expectations being dramatically repriced given that the BoJ's forward guidance (policy commitment) is currently "valid" through at least spring 2020. Repricing of the term premium is thus likely to be the main driver of interest rate movements. The yield curve may face bear-steepening pressure if life insurers start to postpone their super-long JGB purchases in anticipation of yields moving higher, but lifers and banks will likely be quite keen to buy at levels of around 1% and 0.15% for the 30y and 10y yields respectively, thereby helping to limit the severity of any selloff.

If the Fed does indeed end up cutting rates, then we would naturally expect further declines in UST yields to generate downward pressure on the JGB curve. 

However, whereas the Fed now has quite ample scope to cut rates, the BoJ's short-end policy rate is already in negative territory, and any further lowering might be deemed too potentially harmful to financial institutions. BoJ Governor Haruhiko Kuroda has indeed insisted that a rate cut might be one of the choices in the event that further easing becomes necessary, but has also stressed that its eventual choice of easing method will depend on a full comparison of likely costs and benefits. 

As mentioned above, we expect the BoJ to rely on "qualitative" measures such as an increase in equity ETF purchases—as opposed to a lowering of the short-end policy rate deeper into negative territory—in the event that our economic bear-case scenario does end up necessitating additional action. 

## **Markets are also likely to recognize that the BoJ will be reluctant to cut its Tier 3 interest on excess reserves (IOER) rate to any lower than –0.1%, for which reason we see only limited downside for short-end swap rates and expect a decline in UST yields to provide greater support for the super-long sector.** 

## **Swap rates likely to fall faster than JGB yields if the Fed opts for a rate cut** 

That said, we would expect long-end swap rates to fall faster than super-long JGB yields under such a scenario (see Exhibit 26). We say this because short-term speculators may be relatively quick to take profits on super-long JGBs after having repeatedly seen the BoJ cut back its purchases when rallies are deemed "excessive". Life insurers may also become more reluctant to buy super-long JGBs if yields fall too far. Foreigners are meanwhile the main players in the JPY swaps market, and do not, to this point, appear especially uncomfortable with the degree to which UST yields have fallen. 

If the UST yield curve were to steepen as a consequence of a Fed rate cut, then (depending on the size of that cut) life insurers could conceivably start to lose interest in super-long JGBs in favor of JPY-hedged UST or MBS positions. Rate cut expectations are meanwhile unlikely to take hold in Japan, meaning that foreigners could end up crowding into long-end JPY swap receiving positions, in our view.

We have stuck with 30y ASW long positions until now in anticipation of strong demand for super-long JGBs among life insurers, but we intend to exit as we see any further rally in JPY rates to be driven by long-end swaps. On the other hand, we are sticking with the view that short-end yield curve inversion will be eventually corrected (based on our belief that the BoJ has very limited room to cut rates). We suggested paying 2y2y positions to position for this view, but we would like to shift from 2y2y to 1y1y paying position for a better rolldown profile (see Exhibit 27). ## **Tariffs – Impact on CPI and front-end TIPS** 

**I know what tariffs did last summer:** TIPS breakevens are one of the most directly affected asset classes due to tariffs. With additional tariffs imposed by the US administration on $200 billion of Chinese imports, the focus on the potential inflation impact has again taken center stage for the front end of the TIPS market. During the first round of tariffs, implemented in September 2018, markets had to rely on making assumptions about pass-throughs, substitutability of Chinese products, and the currency impacts, to suss out the impact of tariffs on CPI, an exercise fraught with wide error bands. 

24 

A silver lining for this latest round of tariffs is that markets have seen the actual behaviour of inflation in response to tariffs, which allows us to look at the inflation impact empirically, rather than making difficult assumptions along the way. 

**Tariffs on consumer products flow into CPI fast:** There have been several rounds of tariffs so far, but the tariffs in January 2018 on washing machines and the ones on $200 billion of goods in September 2018 allow the easiest study on the impact of tariffs on CPI. Exhibit 33 shows how washing machine prices, evident in CPI, jumped nearly one for one with the 20% tariff rate, implying that consumer goods had a high risk of passthough. This first round of tariffs taught us that **consumer goods could reflect tariffs as early as 1 month after announcement, and peak around 4-6 months.** 

This made assessing the impact of 10% tariffs on $200 billion goods slightly easier. Given the precedent of the flow-through of tariffs on washing machines, we assumed that the impact of these tariffs would be visible in 1-4 months after tariffs were announced. Therefore, one could expect that the impact from the tariffs enacted in September 2018 play out in the September to December 2018 CPI prints. Prima facie evidence, looking at the core goods CPI on a 3m rolling basis, would suggest this is true (see Exhibit 34).

## **Are the markets positioned for such a rise in inflation?** 

Given the possibilities of tariffs affecting headline CPI inflation meaningfully, we think the lack of market moves in the front end reflects skepticism about either (1) the tariffs coming to fruition or (2) the pass through of tariffs meaningfully affecting consumer prices, and thereby consumer price inflation. 

To assess the market impact, we look at inflation risk premiums embedded in the front end of the CPI swap curve. To extract inflation risk premiums, we compare the consensus Bloomberg expectation for CPI forecasts 12 months out vs. the pricing of 1y and 2y CPI swaps. Exhibit 37 shows that markets tend to be anchored around the 12m CPI forecasts with some addition or subtraction of inflation risk premiums. 

While we do understand why some investors believe it is difficult to assign probabilities to the implementation of tariffs, we think the idea of a low pass-through is at odds with what we have seen so far, especially given that there was a stronger reaction when the tariffs were imposed on $200 billion goods at 10% (see Exhibit 38). This is also at odds with the market reaction in equities and other risk markets. With a 25% tariff rate, we think the market should price in even more additional risk premium for inflation driven by tariffs. 

However, there remains he possibility that trade tensions could be resolved in the next 3-4 weeks, and therefore we can understand why the market pricing is muted. However, we see this as a binary risk wherein if more tariffs are enacted, the market could quickly and sharply price in the impact on CPI. We stay on the sidelines waiting for clarity on tariffs for now.

## G4 Smarter (beta) Trading Strategy

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts" 

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. ## **How to read the xBMIs** 

The "FX/Rates" row displays the FX/rates relationship signal. The "Combined BMI differential" row displays the difference between the relevant BMI(10) signals after having applied the signal strength check, i.e., abs(signal) >= 1.5. The "Average xBMI" row displays the average of the "FX/Rates" and "Combined BMI differential" rows. And the "Overall" score requires that the sign of the "Average xBMI" signal match the sign of the "Combined BMI differential" signal and be ≥ the absolute value of 2.
