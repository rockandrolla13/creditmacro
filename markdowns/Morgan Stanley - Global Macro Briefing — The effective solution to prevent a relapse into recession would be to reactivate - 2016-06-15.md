## 1937-38 Redux? 

Inflation expectations and private capex demand are slipping. As we are approaching the limits of monetary policy, we think fiscal policy needs to be activated, but policy-makers are either hesitant or constrained by the political cycle, keeping the risk of a relapse back into recession alive. 

**The current macroeconomic environment has a number of significant similarities with the 1930s:** The critical similarity is that the financial shock and the relatively high levels of indebtedness changed the risk attitudes of the private sector and triggered them to repair their balance sheets. Moreover, the tighter macroprudential policies and regulatory environment also posed as constraints, made the deleveraging process more complicated and led deflationary pressures to persist. Hence, private demand will likely stay weak for longer, and inflation expectations should also remain subdued, while the risk of inflation expectations getting unanchored also increases. 

**Premature tightening of macro policies brings the risks of a relapse:** In 1936-37, the premature and sharp pace of tightening of policies led to a double-dip in the US economy, resulting in a relapse into recession and deflation in 1938. Similarly, in the current cycle, as growth recovered, policy-makers proceeded to tighten fiscal policy, which has contributed to a slowdown in growth in recent quarters. 

## **The effective solution to prevent a relapse into recession would be to reactivate** 

**policy stimulus:** Activating fiscal policy, particularly at a time when the monetary policy stance is still accommodative, could lead to a virtuous cycle where the corporate sector takes up private investment, and sustains job creation and income growth. Policy support should also remain in place until inflation expectations have stabilised, and until the private sector’s risk attitudes have shifted towards risk-seeking again. Our base case is that the policy response remains somewhat hesitant and that global growth remains below trend.

## 1937-38 redux? 

## Global economy still stuck with demand deficiency and lowflation risks 

2016 will likely mark the fifth consecutive year that global growth will be below its 30year average. Inflation expectations in a number of key economies, as inferred from bond markets and household surveys, have decelerated towards fresh lows in some cases, underlying trends in the actual inflation data are subdued, private capex growth is slipping and productivity growth is weak. The risk of a global recession (i.e., full-year growth of below 2.5%Y) remains elevated at this stage. We think that the structural headwinds arising from the 3D challenge of debt, demographics and disinflation is the key factor why the global economy has remained in a weak state. Out of the top 10 DM and EMs, 15 of them have a rising age dependency ratio, 14 have debt/GDP close to or above 200%, and inflation is subdued in 16 of them. 

## Similarities between the current cycle and the 1930s 

We think that the current macroeconomic environment has a number of significant similarities with the 1930s, and the experiences then are particularly relevant for today as it was one of the time periods when a number of major economies faced the 3D challenge. Indeed, the unproductive build-up of debt was the cause of both the Great Depression of the 1930s and the Great Recession of 2008. Moreover, weaker demographic trends in a number of economies during both time periods would have also been weighing on potential growth. The drop in private demand due to the subsequent debt deleveraging process created intense disinflationary pressures. 

The critical similarity between the 1930s and the 2008 cycle is that the financial shock and the relatively high levels of indebtedness changed the risk attitudes of the private sector and triggered them to repair their balance sheets. During the deleveraging process, the private sector becomes risk-averse and shifts its attention towards restoring health to its balance sheets. Moreover, the deleveraging of financial intermediaries at the same time has also had the impact of exacerbating and prolonging the adjustment cycle. Meanwhile, the tighter macro-prudential policies and regulatory environment also posed as constraints, made the deleveraging process more complicated and led deflationary pressures to persist. Hence, private demand will likely stay weak for longer, and inflation expectations should also remain subdued, while the risk of inflation expectations getting unanchored also increases.

## **A review of what happened in the 1930s** 

The US was the epicentre of the economic shock, which eventually transmitted to the rest of the world via both trade and financial linkages. The build-up of debt and the tightening of monetary policy in 1928 and the stock market crash of 1929 have been identified as key triggers for the onset of the Great Depression. 

As aggregate demand contracted sharply, intense deflationary pressures began to build among the major economies in the 1930s. The strict adherence to the gold standard, at that point in time, further exacerbated the deflationary tendencies. The Federal Reserve also maintained a hawkish monetary policy stance for an extended period of time, as exemplified by the fact that it allowed the money supply to contract by a third over 1929-33. This reinforced the deflationary pressures, keeping the US economy in deflation for four consecutive years from 1930-33. The sharp drop in demand and intense deflationary pressures were also felt in a number of European economies, with the key economies of the UK, Germany and France experiencing deflation over the same period from 1930-33. 

From 1933 onwards, a series of policies to stabilise the banking system and the decision to devalue the US dollar (and not to sterilise the consequent gold inflows) contributed towards more favourable monetary conditions in the US. The expansionary monetary and fiscal policies helped the US economy to return to positive growth and inflation from 1934 onwards. 

From 1936 onwards, after a period of recovery, policy-makers tightened both monetary and fiscal policy and caused the economy to double-dip in 1937-38. The recession in 1937-38 then led to another reversal in the policy stance, with the Treasury ending its policy of sterilising gold inflows in February 1938, the Federal Reserve cutting its reserve requirement ratio in April that year, and the fiscal deficit widening to 3% of GDP in 1939. The combination of monetary and fiscal easing supported a growth recovery in 1939, though it was not until a year later that inflation moved back into positive territory. Subsequently, monetary and fiscal policies were both fully expansionary, and the resulting significant boost to aggregate demand led to a recovery, though no doubt triggered by the needs arising from World War II. 

Normalising inflation expectations and addressing the private sector’s risk attitudes are key 

To address the demand deficiency and lowflation risks, monetary and fiscal policy should work in tandem. During the 1930s, the initial, overtly tight monetary policy stance by the Fed prolonged the recession and deflationary episode between 1929 and 1933. However, the strong rates of monetary expansion and an expansionary fiscal policy over 1933-36 paved the way for a return to positive rates of growth and inflation from 1934 onwards. As the financial crisis first broke out in 2008, it seemed that policymakers had heeded the lessons from the 1930s as they eased monetary policy to address the sharp fall in output. Rapid interest rate cuts and successive rounds of quantitative easing meant that the Federal Reserve was able to keep real interest rates in negative territory, helping to smooth the deleveraging process. Fiscal policy was also deployed in the immediate aftermath of the global financial crisis, and the boost to public demand helped to offset the weakness in private demand. 

3 

However, premature tightening of macro policies brings the risks of a relapse. In 1936, the Federal Reserve doubled the reserve requirements for banks in a series of steps and the Treasury also began to sterilise gold inflows in 1936, which slowed the growth rate of high-powered money. Fiscal policy was also tightened. Social security payments were also collected for the first time and the fiscal deficit narrowed from 5.1% of GDP in 1936 to 2.4% in 1937 and subsequently to 0.1% in 1938. The premature and sharp pace of tightening of policies led to a double-dip in the economy, resulting in a relapse into recession and deflation in 1938.

The key DM economies have all taken up fiscal tightening in recent years and particularly so in US, euro area and UK. ## Policy response and effectiveness still hold the key to the macro outlook 

The sluggish private demand and weakening inflation expectations are signs that the repair process for the private sector’s balance sheets is not yet complete. If global growth stays weak for longer, the corporate sector will have to continuously adjust down its return expectations, leading to a negative feedback loop. 

At this juncture, the effective solution to prevent a relapse into recession would be to reactivate policy stimulus. While there is still some policy space left for monetary policy, we are approaching the limits of monetary policy and the effectiveness of monetary easing is waning. Fiscal policy can be employed as a tool to boost demand, but policymakers are either hesitant or constrained by the political cycle to fully employ it as a counter-cyclical tool. Indeed, we think that there is undue concern over running high fiscal deficits, particularly at a time when the need of the hour is to ensure that aggregate demand is supported via boosting public demand. 

Activating fiscal policy, particularly at a time when the monetary policy stance is still accommodative, could lead to a virtuous cycle where the corporate sector takes up private investment, and sustains job creation and income growth. To be sure, structural reforms that boost productivity growth have an important role to play in lifting potential growth, but we think that activating fiscal policy will be critical at this juncture to crowd in private investment. 

Reflecting the ongoing deleveraging process, we think that policy support will have to remain in place until inflation expectations have stabilised, and until we see signs that the private sector’s risk attitudes have shifted towards risk-seeking again. But for now, the evidence is still pointing towards a risk-averse private sector, which means that the key factor that will determine the macro outlook from here will still hinge on an effective policy response. Our base case view is that the policy response remains somewhat hesitant and that global growth remains below trend.

## Key debates in global economics 

## **Our core views on the global economy** 

**We expect global growth to remain broadly stable at 3.0%Y in 2016 as compared to 3.1%Y in 2015. Relatively strong consumer spending and monetary policy is expected to partly offset the headwinds from weaker capex spending and slower trade growth. However, the risks remain skewed to the downside, particularly from upcoming political events. With monetary stimulus likely becoming less effective, fiscal policy and structural reforms gain importance for shaping the macro outlook.** 

**Growth: Potential headwinds from politics:** Growth in EM has stabilised recently, and the DM consumer has held up relatively well. However, there is a potential risk that upcoming political events could impact DM consumer sentiment and spending, which would add to the existing drag on growth from capex and weak external demand. In China, the reduction in policy support (slower M2 and government spending growth) has led to a weakening of activity indicators, and the mini-cycle recovery has likely peaked. For EM ex China, we expect recovery to be constrained as the macro adjustment process is still under way. 

**Inflation: Not back to central bank targets over our forecast horizon:** Output gaps, commodity prices and currency movements are the key factors influencing the inflation trajectory. We don’t expect inflation in DM to hit the central banks’ target over our forecast horizon. EMs are still undergoing a disinflationary phase due to wide output gaps. Inflation in EM commodity exporters is normalising as the impact of the previous sharp currency depreciations begins to fade. China’s CPI inflation has been elevated due to the spike in food prices, but we expect this to gradually soften over the remainder of the year. 

## **Policy response: Monetary policy to remain accommodative, picking up the pace of fiscal easing:** We expect the global monetary policy stance to remain 

accommodative. We don’t expect the next Fed rate hike until late 2016, and expect further monetary easing by the ECB and BoJ in the coming quarters. Central banks in EM should also remain on the easing path, and we pencil in further rate cuts for Brazil, China, India, Russia and Taiwan in 2016. However, we think that the effectiveness of monetary policy in boosting growth and inflation is waning, particularly for DM economies. We view fiscal policy as a useful complement to help boost aggregate demand directly. Fiscal policy is already expansionary in China and moderately so in Europe, and we expect Japan to respond with fiscal easing soon. 

**Key risks: Financial conditions and political crisis:** With the global economy still stuck in a low-growth environment, it remains vulnerable to shocks, including: i) Central banks losing control of domestic financial conditions (Fed, ECB and BoJ) or international capital flows (EM, notably China); and ii) A broad range of political and geopolitical risks, notably a number of local situations (Brexit, US elections, Brazil) and more broadly the European refugee crisis and the Middle East conflict. 

## **Country highlights** 

**US:** Alongside headwinds from external trade and destocking, a substantial slowdown in domestic demand led to a further deceleration in 1Q growth. Incoming 2Q data point towards a pick-up in growth to 2.5%Q amid solid consumer spending. 

**Euro area:** Growth was stronger than expected in 1Q, in part related to one-off weather and calendar effects. But growth looks to have moderated in 2Q, with our tracking model currently pointing towards 0.3%Q. 

**Japan:** Growth was stronger than expected in 1Q, although the leap-year impact distorted the numbers upward. Key focus in coming weeks will be the BoJ decisions and the government’s fiscal stimulus package. 

**China:** May activity data confirmed that China’s growth has been back to a downward trend after the short-lived mini-cycle improvement in the spring. We expect growth to moderate in the coming months on the back of less accommodative policy and a moderation in the property market. 

**Brazil:** Risks are more balanced now as the political crisis comes closer to a resolution. The biggest imbalance continues to be fiscal policy, which needs to be addressed. The stronger currency and deep recession are helping to bring inflation down and our base case is rate cuts this year, albeit a little later than what markets are pricing. 

**India:** The recovery in growth is becoming more broad-based, with the pick-up in public sector capex and rise in foreign direct investment inflows spreading to discretionary consumption (both urban and rural). 

**Russia:** High-frequency activity data show signs of stabilisation. Rising oil prices and consequent currency appreciation have eased inflation pressures, and allowed the CBR to start an easing cycle and cut the key rate by 50bp to 10.5% in June. 

For our global forecasts, see Spring Global Macro Outlook: Lower Growth, Higher Risks, 13 Mar 2016

## What's new in the global economy 

Nonfarm payrolls in the US rose only 38,000 in May, one of the weakest few months so far in the recovery. While the unemployment rate fell to 4.7%, the fall was due to a drop in the participation rate. 

Consumption indicators in the euro area suggest that consumer spending probably stabilised in 2Q16. In the UK, the polls and betting markets for the EU referendum (as of June 13, 2016) point to a 33% 'tracking' probability of a vote to Leave. 

In Japan, Prime Minister Abe has announced that the consumption tax hike (initially scheduled for April next year) would be delayed until October 2019, and he hinted at taking “comprehensive and extensive measures on the economy” this autumn. 

May activity data and our proprietary MS-CHEX index confirmed that China’s growth is back to a downward trend, following reduced policy support and softer property market momentum.
