## **US Interest Rate Strategy** 

## Who Is Going to Buy Treasuries? 

MORGAN STANLEY & CO. LLC Matthew Hornbach STRATEGIST Matthew.Hornbach@morganstanley.com +1 212 761-1837 

We think foreign official buyers have started buying Treasuries again after a brief hiatus around year-end. In addition, foreign investors actually net purchased Treasuries last year, contrary to popular belief. Higher yields in the long end will attract foreign and domestic investment alike. 

**The New York Fed data show that custody holdings of Treasuries for foreign official and international accounts have increased by $58bn in the four weeks since January 24, 2018.** That is the largest fourweek increase since June 2017 when 10-year yields were below 2.20%. 

**We think that persistent weakness in the US dollar pushed currency valuations to levels that reserve managers found unpalatable.** The increase in international reserve assets (ex-gold) in 2017 was very consistent with the addition of US Treasury securities to foreign official balance sheets, and we think this will continue. 

- **A popular narrative developed over the past year that foreign investors have been selling Treasuries.** Weakness in the US dollar throughout 2017 lent credence to this idea. The underperformance of Treasuries relative to Bunds, gilts, and JGBs in early 2018 provided the narrative momentum as well. 

**However, in 2017, foreign investors actually bought $20bn Treasuries on net – in stark contrast to the $325bn of net Treasury sales in 2016.** The UK was one of the biggest buyers of Treasuries in 2017, purchasing $164bn Treasuries. Given that the UK is a financial center, transaction bias may obscure the domicile of end investors. 

- **Investors in the euro area net sold $50bn Treasuries in 2017,** split between $12bn of net sales in 2H17 and $38bn of net sales in 1H17, while **investors in Asia sold $21bn Treasuries on net in 2017,** split between $41bn of net sales in 2H17 and $20bn of net purchases in 1H17. Full-year net sales of $21bn were minuscule compared to net Treasury sales of $269bn in 2016 and $153bn in 2015. 

In our view, the higher yields available to both foreign and domestic investors today should attract enough demand to clear the coming increase in Treasury supply. **We still like UST 2s30s curve flatteners.**

## Wherefore art thou foreign official buyer? 

## **An appropriate focus on foreign buying of Treasuries** 

Investors overseas remain an important fixture in the US Treasury supply/demand equation. Despite the recent decline, foreign ownership of Treasuries remains significant at close to 40% of outstanding (Exhibit 1). Nevertheless, foreign ownership has declined, driven primarily by a decline in foreign official holdings. Exhibit 2 shows that foreign official ownership as a percentage of overall foreign ownership has declined from 75% to 64% since the financial crisis.

So, it's no secret that foreign official investors have not been big buyers of Treasuries recently (Exhibit 3). In our recent report, We Like The Long End, we suggested that, as the US is running a current account deficit with the rest of the world and to the extent that foreign central banks intervene in FX markets as dollars procured from trade are converted into local (non-US) currencies, the foreign official investors should buy more Treasuries. 

Indeed, we saw foreign official buying increase in 2017 and look for even more in 2018. The increase we've seen in foreign official holdings of Treasuries over the past year is consistent with the long-standing relationship between changes in international reserve assets and changes in Treasury note/bond holdings (Exhibit 4). 

This will especially be the case if US businesses and consumers spend their tax windfalls on goods and services produced outside the US. As a result, we have been on high alert for signs that foreign official investors are returning to the US Treasury market in 2018. And now the New York Fed's custody holdings data show that these buyers have returned with a consistency worthy of note.

Exhibit 5 shows the Treasury data (TICS), which are delayed by two months (latest data are from December), as well as the New York Fed's custody holdings data, which are released weekly. **The New York Fed data show that custody holdings of Treasuries have increased by $58bn in the four weeks since January 24, 2018.** That is the largest fourweek increase since June 2017 when 10-year yields were below 2.20%. 

The New York Fed data suggest that foreign official investors have been buying Treasuries, and that we will see that show up in the TICS data eventually. Of course, the TICS data may not match the New York Fed data exactly. There are many reasons why the two series are not more aligned, as covered in the Treasury FAQ (see How do the TIC data on Major Foreign Holders of U.S. Treasury Securities compare with FRBNY custody holdings?). Here are two of them: 

**1.** Custody holdings at the New York Fed are reported at face value, whereas TICS data are a hybrid of market and face values. 

**2.** "Foreign official institutions may also transfer Treasury securities into or out of the custody of FRBNY from other U.S custodians. Such a shift would show up as an increase or decrease in custody holdings at FRBNY, but (appropriately) would not show up as a foreign official transaction in the TIC data because there is no U.S. cross-border transaction." 

Despite the lingering uncertainty, we think that an increase in foreign official buying of Treasuries, if it occurred, makes sense. We discussed this possibility in Float Like a Butterfly, Sting Like A Flattener. We suggested that the weakness in the US dollar made it more likely that China would intervene to stem the strength in CNY. The abrupt reversal of strength in the trade-weighted CNY in February – coincident with the strength in USD/CNY – suggests that some of the increase in the New York Fed's custody holdings may be related to intervention.

## Have investors in Europe been selling? 

A popular narrative developed over the past year that investors in Europe have been selling Treasuries to buy sovereign securities at home. Strength in the euro relative to the dollar in the middle part of 2017 and as the year turned into 2018 supported this idea, at least anecdotally. The underperformance of Treasuries relative to Bunds in early 2018 also seemed supportive. In addition, analysis from the ECB and published by ECB Executive Member Benoît Cœuré hinted that private investors may rebalance their portfolios after a long period of increasing exposure to US debt securities (see What yield curves are telling us, January 31, 2018). 

We checked the progress of the narrative and found that the answer to the question – have investors in Europe been selling US debt? – depends on how you define "Europe". According to the US Treasury, in the second half of 2017, investors in the "euro area" sold a total of $8bn US securities on net (Exhibit 6). Over those six months, euro area investors sold $12bn US Treasuries and $9bn corporate bonds, against buying $8bn US agency-backed securities and $5bn corporate stocks. 

While $8bn of US security sales doesn't seem like a lot over a six-month period, euro area investors sold a lot more US securities in the first six months of 2017. From January to June, euro area investors sold $42bn securities (selling $38bn Treasuries, $8bn corporate bonds, and $5bn corporate stocks vs. buying $9bn US agency securities). In that sense, the warning in Cœuré's speech – which he delivered in January 2018 – came well after the fact.

The Treasury discusses these biases in its FAQ (see What are the problems of geographic attribution for securities holdings and transactions in the TIC system?). The two biases are: 

**Custodial bias in the holdings data:** In short, if a US Treasury security purchased by a foreign resident is held in a custodial account in a third country, the true country of ownership of the security will not be reflected in the data. 

6 

**Transactional bias in the transactions data:** In short, when a foreign resident purchases US securities and the transaction is executed by a financial intermediary in London, the purchase of US securities is recorded against the United Kingdom rather than the country of that foreign resident. 

Unfortunately, the biases in the TICS data make it difficult to know whether European investors – including those in the UK – were buying or selling Treasuries on net after adjusting for transactional biases. 

## What about selling from Asia? 

Investors in Europe aren't the only ones who have been blamed for the lack of sponsorship for US securities over the recent year. Investors in Asia usually take some of the blame as well. What the data suggest, however, is that such blame was better placed in 2015 and 2016, not 2017. Last year, in fact, investors in Asia bought $100bn of US securities on net, driven by a $120bn increase in US agency securities. And although investors in Asia sold $21bn US Treasuries on net in 2017, those sales were minuscule compared to their Treasury sales of $269bn in 2016 and sales of $153bn in 2015.
