## Balance Sheet Challenges 

## Global growth vs. the Fed's balance sheet 

On the first Friday of 2019, Chair Powell gave his view on the drivers of the recent market volatility. In short, he suggested that markets were pricing in downside risk related to concerns "about slowing global growth, particularly related to China, about ongoing trade negotiations, about what maybe let’s call general policy uncertainty coming out of Washington, among other factors". 

Powell stressed the global nature of the downside risks that markets were pricing, thereby downplaying the domestic factors. Given strength in the US economic data, Powell concluded that the Fed's monetary policies, its balance sheet normalization policy specifically, wasn't an "important part of the story" at least with respect to "the market turbulence that began in the fourth quarter last year". 

Sidestepping the 225bp of conventional policy tightening and its impact, Powell suggested the Fed thinks of its balance sheet normalization policy as having a mechanical effect. He even reminded the audience of former Fed Chair Yellen's view that the normalization process should be about as interesting as watching paint dry. 

_I think we are of the view that what really happens mechanically when – you know, when our securities mature is Treasury issues a comparable amount of securities, but they do so across the yield curve. The amounts are not that big. They haven’t been that big compared to the issuance Treasury has already undertaken. So we don’t believe that our issuance is an important part of the story of the market turbulence that began in the fourth quarter last year._ 

Powell is correct in that, on average, the monthly reduction in the Fed's security holdings has been less than half of the monthly increase during QE1, QE2, and QE3 (see Exhibit 1). Nevertheless, **even though the balance sheet has been shrinking at a slower pace than it grew, the Fed has already removed 9.5% of the cumulative support from its portfolio through December 2018** (see Exhibit 2). 

It's also worth recognizing that the Fed grew its balance sheet when it had the policy rate at the zero lower bound. In contrast, the Fed began reducing its balance sheet with the policy rate above 1.00%, and the pace of reduction has only quickened as the policy rate has risen. **This difference in the risk-free rate has almost certainly changed the portfolio balance channel dynamics** we summarize below and discuss in detail in Pricing in Balance Sheet Normalization. We conclude that balance sheet normalization – both the process itself and policymaker communications – played an important role in the late 2018 market volatility.

## The portfolio balance channel in brief 

When the Fed implemented its large-scale asset purchase programs (LSAPs), the Fed purchased Treasuries, agency debt, and agency MBS from banks with electronic cash. As such, the assets in the banking system change from Treasuries (which are term deposits with the US Treasury) and agency securities to reserves (which are electronic cash deposits with the Fed). At the same time, the assets held by the public changed from Treasuries and agency securities to bank deposits (see How the Fed Changes the Size of Its Balance Sheet and The Case of Mortgage-Backed Securities). 

3 

This exchange of assets between the Fed and the banking system/public raises two important questions related to the portfolio balance channel: 

**1.** Were the assets the Fed exchanged with the banking system and the public substitutes? 

**2.** If not, what do the demand curves look like for each asset? 

The duration of the assets that the Fed exchanges with the banking system and the public differs dramatically. With quantitative easing (QE), the Fed removes assets of long duration and replaces them with assets of zero duration (electronic cash). With balance sheet normalization, or quantitative tightening (QT), the Fed removes assets of zero duration and, indirectly, replaces them with assets of longer duration. 

When the Fed implemented QE, the demand was rising for longer-duration assets, e.g., longer-maturity fixed income securities and equities, relative to shorter-duration assets. The low yields on shorter-duration assets, thanks to the near zero policy rate, reduced demand for them. As the demand rose for longer-duration assets, the Fed reduced the supply of them. As a result, QE drove the prices of longer-duration assets significantly higher. 

Now, the Fed is implementing QT by removing zero-duration assets and allowing the US Treasury and mortgage originators to add longer-duration assets back to the public. If the policy rate was still near zero, the relative demand for longer-duration assets may have remained robust. However, **the Fed is implementing QE with a much higher policy** 

**rate. The higher policy rate has led to higher demand for shorter-duration assets, relative to longer-duration assets, just as the Fed is reducing their supply. This should drive the prices of longer-duration assets lower.** 

In short, the implementation of QE in a period with near-zero interest rates both encouraged and incentivized private investors to increase investment risk. As a result, private sector balance sheets became riskier during the QE era. Now, **with QT in a period of rising rates, the Fed is encouraging and incentivizing private sector balance sheets to de-risk.** 

## The most interesting paint we've ever seen dry 

In this sense, we have a different view than Powell on the importance of balance sheet normalization to recent market behavior. In our view, balance sheet normalization on its own has played an important, and clearly underappreciated, role in market dynamics, and one that has likely been exacerbated by the normalization of policy rates. And we aren't the only ones with a different view on the balance sheet's impact on markets. Powell noted that the Fed is "hearing a lot from different groups of people about the role that the balance sheet normalization may be playing in the markets". 

As Powell implied, market participants have been expressing much more concern about the impact of balance sheet normalization as risky asset prices have swooned. We think these concerns may have developed too quickly in one direction over recent months without the correct understanding of the dynamics at play. At the same time, we think market participants weren't concerned enough throughout most of 2018. 

4 

The Fed's current view, one that both Chair Powell and Vice Chair Clarida admitted may need to change in the future, misses a key aspect of how different markets price the portfolio balance channel. **While the process may seem "mechanical", as Powell** 

**suggested, market pricing is much more dynamic and nuanced** – a topic we illuminate in Pricing in Balance Sheet Normalization. From the moment expectations began forming for balance sheet normalization – after the 2016 US presidential election – the mural formed by the process and market pricing has been the most interesting paint we've ever seen dry. 

## Other balance sheet challenges 

The start of the balance sheet normalization process was formally announced in September 2017 and implemented from October 2017 onwards (though its plans had already been laid out much earlier in Fed documents). Since then, the FOMC has stayed on the exact same path as it had announced and adopted almost more than 12 months ago, essentially keeping to the “passive and predictable” doctrine which had been laid out for some time. 

As a result, it may appear odd that the balance sheet normalization process has had an impact on financial conditions in 4Q18. Aside from the related portfolio balance channel effect, described in Stock, flow, and portfolio balance channel effects, we think the normalization process ran into a number of challenges, particularly in the last quarter of 2018: 

## The pace of normalization and its global counterparts 

The Fed had planned to gradually raise the reinvestment caps until, combined, they hit $50bn ($30bn for Treasury securities, $20bn for agency debt and MBS). In October 2018, the caps reached these maximums as planned – allowing the balance sheet normalization process to accelerate further in 4Q18. 

Moreover, **we believe that the global central bank balance sheet, including the ECB, BoJ and BoE, played a role in influencing financial conditions in the US.** To the extent that other central banks approached an inflection point around the same time period, it should have also played a role in influencing financial conditions in the US, in our view. 

## Uncertainty over optimal size and composition 

Even though the Fed communicated the pace of the unwind well ahead of its start, uncertainty remains regarding the final, optimal size of the Fed’s balance sheet. The earlier guidance the Fed provided in its policy normalization principles is that its holdings will “continue to decline in a gradual and predictable manner until the Committee judges that the Federal Reserve is holding no more securities than necessary to implement monetary policy efficiently and effectively”. Regarding composition, the Fed suggested it would “hold primarily Treasury securities”. 

5 

Just as importantly, the Fed has not indicated the optimal average maturity of its holdings of Treasury securities. The minutes of the December meeting said, "Several participants noted that a portfolio of holdings weighted toward shorter maturities would provide greater flexibility to lengthen maturity if warranted by an economic downturn, while a couple of others noted that a portfolio with maturities that matched the outstanding Treasury market would have a more neutral effect on the market." 

The lack of guidance on these issues represented an increasing source of uncertainty among market participants in 4Q18, and continues to do so. Even if the Fed halts the monetary tightening coming from the size of its balance sheet, it may very well continue the tightening through its composition. Given that the current market consensus is for the Fed to reduce the balance sheet's weighted-average maturity, increased confidence over an early end to balance sheet normalization may not suffice to calm market jitters. 

## Preset path on balance sheet despite shifting outlook 

Finally, investors were concerned that the Fed was remaining on a preset course in its balance sheet policy, even though the US and global growth outlook had weakened. Despite robust trailing consumption growth and strong labor market dynamics, the US economy was unlikely to remain immune to slowing global growth. In addition, the recent tightening in financial conditions, plus the overhang of trade tensions, had affected capex intentions. Recognizing the onset of this slower growth environment, the Fed had signaled flexibility on the policy rate path in 4Q18, but had not signaled a similar flexibility on its balance sheet policy – something it did only recently.

## Pricing in Balance Sheet Normalization 

## Stock, flow, and portfolio balance channel effects 

In order to understand the impact of balance sheet normalization on asset prices, we need to have a basic, but nuanced, understanding of the stock effect (SE), flow effect (FE), and portfolio balance channel effect (PBCE) from quantitative easing (QE) and quantitative tightening (QT), a.k.a., balance sheet normalization. 

The SE is thought to represent the long-term effect of the Fed's balance sheet as a whole on US Treasuries, agency debt, and agency MBS – i.e., what is the total, ongoing impact today from the increase or decrease in the balance sheet size? The FE is thought to represent the short-term impact on US Treasuries, agency debt, and agency MBS from each QE or QT operation – i.e., how large was each flow and what was the impact at that time? The PBCE is thought to represent the impact on assets other than US Treasuries, agency debt, and agency MBS. 

**In today's marketplace, the most important aspect of balance sheet normalization is the PBCE.** Former Fed Chair Bernanke discussed the PBCE in relation to QE in his August 2012 speech titled, Monetary Policy since the Onset of the Crisis: 

_One mechanism through which such purchases are believed to affect the economy is the so-called portfolio balance channel, which is based on the ideas of a number of well-known monetary economists, including James Tobin, Milton Friedman, Franco Modigliani, Karl Brunner, and Allan Meltzer. The key premise underlying this channel is that, for a variety of reasons, different classes of financial assets are not perfect substitutes in investors' portfolios._ 

_Imperfect substitutability of assets implies that changes in the supplies of various assets available to private investors may affect the prices and yields of those assets... as investors rebalance their portfolios by replacing the MBS sold to the Federal Reserve with other assets, the prices of the assets they buy should rise and their yields decline as well._ 

We have viewed and continue to view changes in the Fed's balance sheet as an important driver of markets via the SE, the FE, and the PBCE. We also think each effect impacts markets differently and, importantly, at different times (see Trading the Fed's Balance Sheet). It's why we spent time trying to understand when the balance sheet would stop shrinking (see A Surprisingly Early End to Balance Sheet Normalization). In what follows, we discuss our framework for which markets price these three effects, how they price them, and when. 

7 

Before we discuss each effect, we place assets into two groups. 

**1. Group 1:** Assets that the central bank impacts directly. In the case of the Federal Reserve, Treasuries, agency debt, and agency MBS.[1] 

**2. Group 2:** Assets that the central bank impacts indirectly (everything else not in Group 1). 

When estimating the stock effect (SE) and flow effect (FE), we do so in relation to Group 1 assets. When estimating the portfolio balance channel effect (PBCE), we do so in relation to both Group 1 and Group 2 assets. How and when does each effect get priced into assets? 

## The stock effect (SE) – Group 1 

## **The SE relates to the long-term impact on Group 1 asset prices from the overall change to the central bank's balance sheet and its impact on the stock of available Group 1** 

**assets.** Despite the long-term nature of the effect, Group 1 assets price in this effect _when investor expectations form_ for changes to the stock of Group 1 assets. The formation of expectations usually occurs over a much shorter time frame, but can vary widely depending on how the central bank communicates its plans. And expectations may form in a probabilistic manner, depending on the specificity of central bank communications. 

For example, if the central bank says it will buy $300bn US Treasuries over the next six months, investors can assign a 100% probability to those purchases over the next six months. As a result, US Treasury prices can move to fully price in investor expectations of the supply impact before the central bank removes the supply. However, if the central bank says it will buy $50bn US Treasuries a month until it decides to stop, investors may only be able to assign 100% probability to the first monthly purchase while assigning lower and lower probabilities to each month thereafter, all on the day of the announcement. 

## The flow effect (FE) – Group 1 

**The FE relates to the short-term impact on Group 1 asset prices from each flow that changes the size of the central bank's balance sheet.** Group 1 assets price in this effect _as the flows actually occur_ , and the flows are spread out over time depending on the central bank’s plans. The impact of each flow depends on how well investors anticipated the size and timing of each flow beforehand. As discussed above, as investors form expectations for future flows, prices change – i.e., the stock effect. The FE tells you how closely investor expectations came to predicting, and as a result setting up for, the market impact of the actual flows. 

Treasury supply, and the auctions that deliver it, make for a good case study. The FE from a Treasury auction is known as an "auction concession" and, for the most part, is a small effect because the US Treasury tells investors how many of each Treasury security it plans to sell well ahead of time. Because the Treasury sets investor expectations ahead of time and, in doing so, moves Treasury prices accordingly, i.e., the stock effect, measuring the FE from Treasury auctions is challenging. 

8 

The underperformance of the sector being auctioned on any given day or week can occur in various ways. Auction concessions can occur outright (yields higher), on swap spread (Treasury yields higher vs. LIBOR swaps), on the yield curve (steeper/flatter/butterfly), versus the performance of risky assets (e.g., Treasuries underperform the recent relationship to equities, credit), versus other highly correlated sovereign yields, or versus the pre-auction when-issued (WI) market. Given all the possibilities and the small size in general, measuring the flow effect is not straightforward. 

## The portfolio balance channel effect (PBCE) – Group 1 and 2 

Understanding (1) the difference between Group 1 and Group 2 assets, (2) the expectations-related and probabilistic nature of how Group 1 assets price the stock effect, and (3) the reconciliation that occurs during the flow effect, is necessary to understand the portfolio balance channel effect (PBCE) because they all play a role. The PBCE impacts both Group 1 and Group 2 assets and incorporates the pricing elements of both the stock effect and the flow effect. 

As Bernanke suggested, the PBCE occurs due to the imperfect substitutability. As the Fed reduces the stock of Group 1 assets available to the public via QE, it forces investors to deploy their cash into imperfect substitutes which become acceptable. As a result, competition heats up for other Group 1 assets and Group 2 assets, which drives up their prices. In other words, investors may rebalance their portfolios – the essence of the PBCE. 

For example, some believe the portfolio balance channel worked this way during the QE years: Investors sold Treasuries and agency mortgages to the Fed. Those investors rebalanced their portfolios by adding corporate debt. The effect was felt first in investment grade (IG) and subsequently in high yield (HY). The increased demand for corporate debt weighed on yields and made it attractive for corporations to issue more debt. Corporations took the proceeds from their increased debt issuance and bought back equities. 

## Naturally, **investors can rebalance their portfolios in many different ways – making it nearly impossible to place a high probability on how and when exactly those** 

**rebalancing flows will occur.** Even if we narrowed down the choices of where the flows could go, we would still face the issue of assigning probabilities to those flows. Let's consider a simplified example. An investor who sold a 5y Treasury to the Fed (indirectly via a broker-dealer) may: 

**1.** Buy a 10y Treasury (a Group 1 transaction involving an extension out the curve to pick up yield), 

**2.** Buy a 5y investment grade corporate bond (a transaction in a Group 2 asset intended to pick up yield at a similar duration) , or2 

**3.** Buy an agency MBS (a transaction in a Group 1 asset offering a yield pick-up). 

**4.** Hold the proceeds in cash deposits. 

9 

Much like how the prices of Group 1 assets move as expectations about their future stock form, the prices of Group 1 and Group 2 assets will move as expectations form for the flows related to the portfolio balance channel. But to what extent? The inability of market participants to understand with certainty how the portfolio balance channel cascades through all asset classes results in their inability to assign high probabilities to those future flows. 

In our example above, we aren't 100% certain which asset the seller of the 5y Treasury will buy. If we assign a 25% probability to each of the four options above, the prices of each will only move 25% of the full extent they will eventually move once the flow occurs. In other words, it's only when the portfolio rebalancing flows actually occur do asset prices fully adjust. 

The movement in asset prices driven by expectations and flows occurs for both assets affected directly by the Fed (Group 1) and for assets affected indirectly by the Fed via the portfolio balance channel (Group 1 and Group 2). However, **the degree to which expectations and flows can move asset prices depends on the nature of the Fed impact: direct or indirect. The more direct the Fed impact, the more expectations move prices. The more indirect the Fed impact, i.e., portfolio balance channel, the more flows move prices.** 

Is there evidence of a portfolio balance channel effect? 

Some have argued that empirical evidence for the portfolio balance channel does not exist. For example, Daniel Thornton at the St. Louis Fed suggested his empirical results provided "virtually no support for the portfolio balance channel" when analyzing "the relationship between a variety of measures of the public’s debt holding and various yield measures" (see QE: Is There a Portfolio Balance Effect). 

In our view, the analysis did not find evidence of the PBCE because the author used public debt data that was not likely to yield evidence. Thornton used measures of the public's debt holdings instead of measures of the public's _expected_ debt holdings. Remember, **Treasury yields (and, by extension, Treasury term premiums) move as expectations are formed for future changes in the stock of Treasury debt. Markets have an ability to discount, probabilistically, changes in supply before they occur.** 

By the time the stock actually changes, due to the flow, Treasury yields (and term premiums) don't move that much. That is why, when analyzing a time series of actual debt against a time series of Treasury yields, controlling for other factors that might move Treasury yields, the results are not likely to show much relationship between debt stock and yields. But that doesn't mean the portfolio balance channel effect doesn't exist. 

In fact, we have demonstrated how this effect has played out through growth in the credit mutual fund ownership of fixed income assets and the fact that the investible universe of corporate credit has outpaced that of agency MBS. Furthermore, the ownership trends of fixed income assets by life insurance companies also shows that, while their holdings of agency MBS have remained largely flat post-2009, their corporate bond ownership has grown over 2 times (see Trading the Fed’s Balance Sheet). 

10 

## The market impact of balance sheet normalization 

In Stock, flow, and portfolio balance channel effects, we discussed our framework for thinking about the effects of balance sheet policy on markets. And in The portfolio balance channel in brief, we summarized the high level effects of the portfolio balance channel. Below, we discuss the impact of balance sheet normalization on Treasuries, agency MBS, and corporate debt. 

## The impact on US Treasuries 

**Balance sheet normalization first affected Treasuries when expectations formed for the additional supply associated with less Fed reinvestment.** Investors began to form expectations for balance sheet normalization in the wake of the 2016 US presidential election. At the time, the Fed had suggested it would begin balance sheet normalization when it was "well under way" to normalizing policy rates. The Fed had been given this guidance since its December 2015 statement: 

_The Committee is maintaining its existing policy of reinvesting principal payments from its holdings of agency debt and agency mortgage-backed securities in agency mortgage-backed securities and of rolling over maturing Treasury securities at auction, and it anticipates doing so until normalization of the level of the federal funds rate is well under way._ 

Ahead of its January 2016 meeting, the New York Fed began polling market participants on their estimate of the most likely level of the target federal funds rate or range when the Committee would first change its reinvestment policy. In January 2016, the median market participant thought 1.38%. Then later in 2016, at the survey ahead of the November 2016 FOMC meeting and US presidential election, the median market participant thought 1.38%, but didn't expect the target to reach that level until 2Q18. 

In the eight months that followed the election, the Fed hiked rates three times to 1.001.25% with the third hike coming at the June 2017 meeting. In the survey ahead of that meeting, the median market participant thought the Fed would begin balance sheet normalization with the target fed funds rate at 1.38%. And by then the median participant had brought forward the time at which they thought the target would reach that level: in September 2017 – just three months away. 

**As market participants brought forward the timing of when they thought balance sheet normalization would begin, Treasury yields (and term premiums) rose.** In doing so, they priced in a healthy portion of the stock effect. Markets were able to price in the stock effect with 100% probability up to a certain point only because, under the balance sheet normalization plan, the Fed has not communicated an optimal size for the balance sheet. As a result, market participants didn't know exactly how many Treasuries the Fed would leave them to buy.[3]

## **Is there a difference between Treasury supply related to balance sheet normalization and Treasury supply related to fiscal deficits?** 

From the perspective of the Treasury Department, there is no difference. But from the public's perspective, the difference is important. When the US Treasury issues a bill, note, or bond related to financing a new deficit, the money to buy that security ultimately comes from the US government itself. When the US government spends more money it doesn't have (a budget deficit), it relies on the Treasury to issue a security to finance that spending. 

The Treasury issues a security and uses the proceeds to credit the government's account. The government debits its account at the US Treasury and credits a bank account in the economy. The money circulates around the economy until, eventually, it ends up buying the Treasury security that was used to fund the government spending in the first place. 

When the Fed removes some of those Treasury securities from the market, it replaces them with reserves/cash deposits in the banking system. The absence of those Treasuries is akin to the government having spent money without relying on the Treasury to issue securities beforehand. Without available Treasury securities to buy, investors put the cash to work elsewhere – driving up asset prices (see The portfolio balance channel in brief). 

If the Fed decides to hold those Treasuries for a long time, it will reinvest the principal upon maturity into securities newly issued by the Treasury Department (to fund the debt). Once the Fed decides to stop reinvesting the matured principal, thereby normalizing its balance sheet, the Treasury will have to find another buyer. This creates additional Treasury supply which the public will have to buy. 

But where does the public find the cash? The US government is not providing it because it already did so. As a result, investors must sell something to raise the cash to buy the Treasuries. In our view, investors are likely to sell whatever they bought with the cash that came from the Fed in the first place.

## The impact on agency MBS and credit 

The mechanics of how agency MBS were added to the Fed’s SOMA portfolio, as well as negative convexity which is an unique feature of agency MBS, bring a different dimension to the portfolio balance channel effect. 

We think it is instructive to take the perspective of an investor whose portfolio is benchmarked to a popular fixed income index (such as the Barclays Bloomberg Aggregate Index), which has an allocation of roughly a third each to agency MBS and investment grade (IG) corporates. The $1.7 trillion agency MBS were bought entirely from the secondary market by the Fed during the QE period. **The presence of a price-** 

**insensitive, programmatic buyer (the Fed) forced economically rational investors out of agency MBS and into an underweight agency MBS vs. corporates position within the benchmark index.** In addition, the Fed’s buying also removed the most negatively convex agency MBS from the market place, pushing mortgage spreads even lower, and further dis-incentivizing investors from owning MBS in their portfolios. 

We argue that corporate credit assets that squarely fall into the Group 2 category benefitted substantially from both Fed QE as well as global QE programs implemented by the ECB, Bank of England and the Bank of Japan. The combined effect of these programs pushed flows into corporate credit with global investors searching for yield. As a result of this demand, credit spreads trended tighter and yields spent much of this cycle at levels rarely seen before. 

Companies were incentivized to issue debt, and they did just that, with the investment grade, high yield, and leveraged loan markets collectively growing by 124% since 2009. Companies used the debt for M&A and to buy back stock, a few ways the benefits of Fed policy spread beyond fixed income. Given this 'debt binge,' leverage rose significantly across non-financial credit markets, and with investors so desperately reaching for yield for so long, credit quality deteriorated in many other ways at the same time. We have argued these dynamics would ultimately provide the fuel for a default/downgrade cycle as credit conditions eventually tightened down the line. 

As the Fed’s balance sheet was set to normalize, we thought many of these demand tailwinds would reverse, a key reason for our bearish credit view in 2018. We expected the flows into US credit to weaken, the MBS underweight vs. corporate credit to move towards equal weight, and corporate credit spreads to trend wider in the process. In 2018, we clearly saw some of these effects playing out. The flows into US credit weakened from nearly all sources. 

Money managers have become increasingly less underweight in their allocations to agency MBS. Credit spreads have widened across markets, first IG starting this past February, then HY in October, and finally leveraged loans in the last two months of the year. **Credit conditions are now tightening slowly and some of the fundamental** 

**challenges built up over the course of this long bull market are slowly coming to a head as a result, with one “idiosyncratic” problem after the next. As we argued in our 2019 US credit outlook, we believe this is the start of a long-slow turn in the credit cycle.** 

14 

Negative convexity of agency MBS introduces another dimension. We note that negative convexity in new production mortgages is getting worse as FICOs and WACs move higher. **With the price-insensitive Fed no longer being the buyer of the most negatively convex agency MBS, it means that investors need to be compensated for the higher negative convexity, more on the margin, which will keep mortgage OAS wider** which in turn accelerates the portfolio balance channel moves away from corporate credit.

## The future for IOER 

The outlook for IOER and the Fed's balance sheet depends in part on the evolution of the effective fed funds rate (EFFR) relative to IOER. As we discussed in A 15bp Fed Rate Hike? the EFFR-IOER spread is one of the main signs of reserve scarcity followed by the Fed and market participants. As the EFFR has been rising relative to IOER over the past months, the Fed adjusted IOER lower within the target range for the fed funds rate, which pushed EFFR lower relative to the upper bound of the target range, reducing the likelihood that EFFR would breach the target range. 

The potential for further IOER adjustments would depend on how the fed funds (EFFR) rate evolves relative to IOER. If EFFR rises another 5bp relative to IOER, then it would be back to only being 5bp from the upper bound of the range targeted by the Fed, which would lead to another IOER adjustment and potentially signal that reserves have reached a level where the Fed would have to halt or slow balance sheet normalization. 

So how long would it take before EFFR rises another 5bp relative to IOER? Exhibit 8 shows the historical relationship between the level of reserves in the system and moves in EFFR relative to IOER. A $1 trillion drop in reserves correlated with a 15bp rise in EFFR. Given that reserves currently shrink by around $0.5 trillion per year, by June EFFR would likely rise by 5-7bp, forcing IOER adjustment by the June FOMC meeting. 

However, aside from the shrinkage in the size of the balance sheet, other factors can also affect the evolution of reserve levels and consequently the EFFR-IOER spread. **In particular the Treasury cash balance can add notable volatility to the level of reserves around debt ceiling dates. The Treasury debt ceiling date on March 1 would require the Treasury to reduce its cash balance held with the Fed by about $200 billion, and that would translate into a temporary $200 billion rise in system reserves** . 

As Exhibit 9 illustrates, this would offset some of the reserve drainage resulting from shrinkage of the Fed's balance sheet and could make a 5bp rise in EFFR before the March FOMC meeting unlikely. **As a result, we think the next IOER adjustment is more likely to occur by the June FOMC meeting than at the March meeting.**

## **Summary of the balance sheet and reserves trajectory** 

Exhibit 12 illustrates the trajectory of the Fed's balance sheet under our base case scenario. Under our trajectory the balance sheet would reach its smallest of $3.8tn size in September 2019 (compared to $4.0tn now). Beyond that point, we expect the Fed to keep the level of reserves stable at $1.3tn. As we discussed in the 2019 Global Rates Outlook, this level of reserves should be enough to allow banks to meet their cash holding needs for liquidity purposes and provide a sufficient buffer above those needs so that the Fed can continue operating a Floor system for managing the Fed funds rate (as opposed to a corridor system). As we discussed in prior publications, the cash holding needs for banks have increased substantially post-2008 due to the Basel III and DoddFrank regulatory regime. 

We highlight that maintaining this level of reserves would require the Fed to start expanding its balance sheet at some point (which under our base case is January 2020). This expansion would be needed to offset the growth of other liabilities on the Fed's balance sheet; mainly currency in circulation which grows at a 5-7% annual rate as Exhibit 11 illustrates. This growth in currency in circulation and liabilities other than reserves is one reason why it is almost impossible to bring the size of the Fed's balance sheet to its pre-crisis levels. 

The expansion of the Fed's balance sheet that we expect to start in 2020 would take place through secondary market purchases of USTs. The decision of what maturities to buy in the secondary market would depend on the Fed's goal for the WAM of its SOMA portfolio. For example, as we discussed in A Surprisingly Early End to Balance Sheet Normalization, the Fed could choose to reinvest all MBS prepays into T-bills while spreading other UST purchases across the UST maturity spectrum. The impact of this scenario on the WAM of USTs held by the Fed is shown in Exhibit 5 (green line). 

On the other hand, the Fed could decide that after ending balance sheet normalization "early", UST maturities that would have otherwise have rolled off the balance sheet as a result of normalization should be invested into T-bills. On top of that, MBS prepays would be reinvested into T-bills. This scenario would result in a much faster drop in the WAM of the SOMA portfolio as shown in Exhibit 5. It is not yet clear which of those two scenarios (if either) would be favored by the Fed.
