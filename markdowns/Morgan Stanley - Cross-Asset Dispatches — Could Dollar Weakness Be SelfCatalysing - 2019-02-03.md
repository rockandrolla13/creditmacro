## Could Dollar Weakness Be SelfCatalysing? 

Dollar weakness could feed on itself if foreign investors are forced to consider the poor FX-hedged returns of US assets. 

**We remain USD bears:** A key theme of our 2019 outlook is that we see a weaker USD as a key 'turning point'. That bearish view is driven by our FX strategists' concerns about rich valuation and a shift in the US growth and policy narrative. Wednesday's FOMC meeting further supports the idea that a major USD peak is now in. 

**But what if this weakness is self-reinforcing?** We hear frequently that dollar weakness will be limited by the fact that it's now 'widely expected'. This overlooks powerful forces that argue for an overshoot to the downside, including the idea that USD weakness could be self-reinforcing. 

**The collision of a rich asset and high hedging costs:** For investors outside the US, this cost of hedging US assets back into local currency has almost never been higher. This didn't matter in an era where US assets were outperforming, and you _wanted_ the currency exposure. But if USD starts to weaken, then the maths suggests that investors should look at hedging. And when they look at poor FXhedged yields, there could be a temptation to sell. Selling unhedged US assets, and reinvesting locally, would only put more downward pressure on USD.

## Could dollar weakness be self-catalysing? 

We think that markets are underestimating a scenario where USD weakness becomes self-reinforcing, leading to larger downside moves. This is linked to the unusually high hedging costs of US assets for non-US investors. These hedging costs haven't mattered in an era of USD strength. They do now. 

## The Fed gives USD a push 

We'd thought that the Fed would have a hard time keeping the market happy, given rising market expectations for what the central bank would deliver. We were wrong. The Federal Reserve executed a major shift in its policy approach, the magnitude of which can hardly be overemphasised. Less than two months ago, with stocks lower than current levels, the FOMC indicated that it was comfortable with further rate hikes and saw no changes to its balance sheet policy. On Wednesday, it suggested that it no longer had a strong bias between moving rates up or down, and was considering changes to balance sheet policy (see US Economics & Rates Strategy: FOMC Reaction: Switching Off Autopilot, January 31, 2019). Wow. 

While the speed of the Fed's shift surprised us, the direction of travel did not. **Our key theme for 2019 is a major turning point between the US and RoW in policy and growth,** which should drive a reversal in a number of multi-year market trends. One of these reversals is for the US dollar, which we think will weaken significantly this year against both EM and DM peers. 

## The case for dollar weakness 

Our FX strategists' call for a weaker dollar is based on several pillars (for the latest, see Doves Rise, Dollar Falls, January 31, 2019). 

**1.** USD is (very) expensive on a variety of FX valuation measures, against both EM and DM. 

**2.** US growth momentum is set to slow materially this year, with Morgan Stanley economists significantly below consensus on US growth. 

**3.** That slowing growth momentum will force the Fed to pause, with markets likely to extrapolate such a pause as the end to the hiking cycle. 

**4.** USD _won't_ be a defensive asset in the next downturn, as the Fed is one of the few central banks with material capacity to cut in a recession. 

There are a number of additional layers to this story, covered here. Needless to say, valuations still look expensive, US growth still looks to be decelerating, the Fed has shifted to a materially more dovish tone and USD wasn't all that defensive during the December meltdown. So far, so good.

## But what if USD weakness feeds on itself? 

Sustained dollar weakness has major implications for global allocations. For the better part of the last nine years, the best trade global investors could make was to own US assets (both stocks and bonds) on an unhedged basis. US equities outperformed all other regions with lower volatility. US Treasuries and credit rallied sharply. And a rise in USD from cycle lows to cycle highs gave returns an additional push. 

This dynamic remained true even as a key shift began in early 2017. As the Fed hiked rates while other central banks stayed put, **the cost of hedging US assets back into EUR, JPY, CHF or GBP soared** (Exhibit 3). This meant that FX-hedged yields for US stocks and bonds deteriorated rapidly for a foreign investor, both outright and relative to local options. In Exhibit 4 and Exhibit 5, we plot the yield of US 10-year Treasuries after hedging costs are subtracted for an investor hedging back to EUR and JPY. In Exhibit 6 and Exhibit 7, we show the same hedge-adjusted levels for equity dividend yields. 

Why didn't this shift in hedging cost matter? It may be because USD continued to appreciate. The positive USD momentum meant that investors were still rewarded.

That mindset is now under threat. Sustained USD weakness would push more global allocators to view the world in FX-hedged terms. And viewed on those terms, US assets (especially equities) have rarely looked worse on a relative basis. It would be one thing if those US assets were particularly cheap in their own right, justifying ownership even with a high cost of hedging. That's generally not the case. 

The implication of such a shift would be clear. Consider a Japanese investor who has been happily holding unhedged US equities for nearly a decade. They now shift to thinking that USD will weaken on a strategic basis. They evaluate this position through an FX-hedged lens, and decide that it looks unattractive to hold. They sell those US equities to buy local stocks. They sell an unhedged USD asset to buy JPY, **pushing USD down even further,** and also putting relative pressure on US equities versus other markets. The shift would feed on itself. 

Not every allocator will think in this way, and even those who do will move gradually. We don't see this dynamic as the reason why USD falls initially, but rather as secondary factor that could kick in once that decline begins. However, the narrative is clear – a long-running case for allocating away from US assets, which is finally catalysed by dollar weakness, and which could feed on itself. 

## _What does history say?_ 

There isn't much precedent for USD being this expensive and the cost of hedging USD assets being this high. But a few comparisons are interesting. The closest instance where non-US investors faced an expensive dollar, and a high cost of hedging back to local currency, was late 1999. That didn't mark the top for USD, but it did mark a major end to US stock outperformance versus RoW. 

A second lesson from history is that **the conventional wisdom that "a weaker USD means better relative US equity performance" is wrong.** Comparing the Broad Dollar Index with relative US versus RoW equity performance, they peaked in the same month, and then reversed together for the next six years. This is our expectation for 2019 – a weaker broad dollar and RoW stocks outperforming the US, and both running on a sustained basis.

Investment implications – more meaningful for stocks than bonds 

Our FX strategists' expectation for broad weakness in USD remains a key 2019 view. They like USD shorts versus both DM (short USDJPY and USDSEK) and EM currencies (short USDBRL and USDZAR). 

The potential for USD weakness to catalyse allocation away from US assets gives this thesis further support, in our view. We think that this case is strongest in equities, where the differences in relative valuation, relative FX-hedged yield and Morgan Stanley price targets are least favourable for the US market. **We favour RoW equities over the US.** 

For US bonds, the impact could potentially be more muted. The difference in FXadjusted yield is much narrower between, say, US and German rates, and US and German equities. Our strategists also forecast a further ~20bp decline in 10-year Treasury yields through year-end, leaving scope for capital appreciation that can offset some of those hedging costs.
