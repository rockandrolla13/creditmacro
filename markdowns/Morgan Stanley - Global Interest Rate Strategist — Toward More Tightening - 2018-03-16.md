## **Think about the dots, but don't overthink the message** 

For the upcoming FOMC meeting, our economists highlight that, for the 2018 and longer-run projections for appropriate monetary policy, the median dots could either move higher or stay the same as they were in December (see FOMC Preview: A Hike And Hawkish Bias). The close call reflects both the position of the dots in the December 2017 dot plot as well as the fact that one less dot (Yellen's) will contribute to the median in the March 2018 dot plot. 

With fewer dots contributing to the calculation of the median (15 dots for the 2018~20 period and 14 dots for the longer run), the movements of each dot become more important for projecting the median. As a result, **we encourage investors to place more** 

## **emphasis on movements in the mean of the dots in each year and the longer run instead of movements in the medians.** 

## **An example of median vs. mean** 

Let's look at the longer-run dots. Assume Yellen's longer-run dot was at 2.75% in December 2017. We know her dot will disappear in the March 2018 dot plot. That means only one of the other 2.75% longer-run dots would need to move higher in order to shift the median higher. If one of the 2.75% dots moved to 3.00%, then the longer-run median dot would shift from 2.75% to 2.875%, i.e., the average of one of the 3.00% dots and one of the 2.75% dots would become the median because there are only 14 longer-run dots. As displayed in the SEP, the longer-run median would shift from 2.7% to 2.9%. 

Now, let's look at this scenario by analyzing the mean of all longer-run dots. With Yellen's longer-run dot at 2.75% in December 2017, the mean of the longer-run dots was at 2.78% – slightly higher than the median at 2.75%. By removing Yellen's 2.75% longerrun dot and shifting higher one of the other 2.75% longer-run dots to 3.00%, the mean longer-run dot would move from 2.78% to 2.80% in the March 2018 dot plot. In other words, while the median longer-run dot would rise by 0.125pp, the mean would only rise by 0.02pp. That is a large enough difference, in our view, to warrant looking at the changes in the mean longer-run dot instead of the median. 

## **The message in the means** 

Our economists think the median dots will move higher in 2019 only, while the mean dots will move higher in 2018 and 2019. Given our median vs. mean analysis of the longer-run dots above, we believe that even if the median longer-run dot moves from 2.7% to 2.9%, the mean longer-run dot is unlikely to move much higher than it was in December 2017. Putting that all together, we think it encourages more yield curve flattening, particularly between the 2y sector and the 30y sector of the yield curve. 

As we have shown before via Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6, the market-implied longer run rate (which includes term premium) is much higher than the longer-run median dot (which does not include term premium). The difference between the two can be thought of as a term premium. If this remains the case after the March 2018 dot plot, which we expect will be the case, then we think investors will remain comfortable expressing long duration views in the long end of the yield curve as a more hawkish path for policy should encourage investors to monetize the term premium in the long end.

## **The Japanese buying?** 

In addition, the other demand seasonality factor in the coming months is the behaviour of Japanese investors. March marks the end of the fiscal year for Japanese investors, where some seasonality is observed around the buying/selling patterns on EGBs. ## **Japanese investors' typical fiscal year-end activity** 

Let us next look at how major investor categories have tended to behave around the fiscal-year turn. 

**Banks:** MoF data indicate that banks have usually been big net sellers of foreign bonds —and indeed of JGBs—in April (see Exhibit 37). Profit taking is likely to have been the main driver, with banks locking in gains as early as possible in a bid to make their fullyear targets easier to achieve. Such selling tends to focus mostly on shorter-dated bonds and will thus usually have only a limited duration impact—and hence minimal overall market impact—even if flows are quite substantial. 

24 

Profit taking might be more limited than usual next month given that (1) sharp rises in interest rates will probably have pushed many bonds into the red and (2) profits might already have been taken to at least some degree so as to offset losses elsewhere. As discussed in our We Like The Long End report, major banks are already showing net valuation losses on their foreign bond portfolios as a whole, suggesting that it may no longer be all that easy to realize capital gains. 

Therefore, we actually expect banks to start out the new fiscal year in buying mode, with European bonds probably the main focus by virtue of their superior risk-adjusted carry and rolldown (see Exhibit 38). Some might feel a little more comfortable buying USTs now that the 10y yield's attack on the 3% level has been thwarted (at least for the time being), but with funding costs so elevated, we expect any long positioning to prioritize potential for tactical (short-term) capital gains over longer-term carry, and thus be limited in size by comparison with the European bond market. 

**Life insurers:** Investment plans for the first half of the fiscal year are typically announced around the end of April. The past few years have generally seen lifers buy mostly foreign bonds in 1H and then increase their allocations to super-long JGBs towards the end of the fiscal year(see Exhibit 39). ALM considerations mean that lifers have traditionally used super-long JGBs to balance their JPY-denominated insurance liabilities, but more recently they have also been buying foreign bonds for yield enhancement purposes (with JGB yields so low as to result in negative margins). 

Lifers will not need to be especially aggressive in reducing their duration mismatches early in the fiscal year, and are thus likely to opt for their "usual" approach of using foreign bonds to generate carry for at least the time being while hoping for JPY rates to reach more attractive levels before the year is out. 

We expect lifers to be favoring Europe in general and comparatively liquid and creditworthy German bunds and French sovereign bonds in particular. Investment plans have of course yet to be unveiled, but our impression is that exposure is already being increased (see Exhibit 35). Lifers typically fund their purchases of foreign-currency bonds either outright or via FX and currency swaps. 

With USD LIBOR–OIS spreads on the rise and solid economic indicators keeping the Fed in "hawk" mode, we see a high likelihood of USD/JPY hedging costs continuing to rise and thus reducing the appeal of JPY-hedged USTs to Japanese investors for carry-generating purposes (see Exhibit 36). 

Moreover, with USD perhaps set to keep weakening due to the impact of USD funding needs associated with globally strong growth, it is difficult to mount a case for investing in USTs on an FX-unhedged basis. We also expect demand for USD-denominated credit (spread product) to gradually drop away, with yields no longer much higher than those on European sovereign bonds once FX hedging costs are subtracted out. 

Conversely, JPY-based investors can actually earn a "hedge premium" by investing in European bonds, thereby enhancing their portfolio yields. With the ECB having signaled that it will be leaving its deposit rate unchanged for some time to come, funding costs should remain sufficiently stable to make European bonds the destination of choice for banks and lifers alike.

## **BEAT Tax is a TLAC, not a CP issuance, story** 

An alternative minimum tax provision, the BEAT tax (Base Erosion and Anti-abuse Tax) was introduced by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 to impair the ability of US firms to shift profits to an overseas affiliate firm in a country with lower tax rates. Previously, a firm could try to avoid paying high taxes on its US income by borrowing money from a foreign affiliate, then paying a high level of interest on that borrowing. The US firm can then deduct that interest expense from its US profits to reduce its taxable US income. This effectively shifts the income to the overseas affiliate firm. 

Media reports surrounding this provision highlighted that it could hit the funding structure of foreign-based banks. This is because a foreign bank funding its US subsidiary during the normal course of business would face double taxation; once in the home country on the interest income from the US subsidiary, while the US subsidiary would no longer be able to write off the interest rate expense. 

As a result, a narrative developed in the market that the BEAT taxes would strain funding needs of foreign banks, and it is argued that this is contributing to the Libor-OIS widening. We strongly disagree with that view. 

36 

**For one thing, shifting the issuance from the parent company to the subsidiary does not alter the total USD funding needs in the market. More importantly, issuance of CPs by US subsidiaries of foreign banks has been in steady fall as we will discuss in more detail later (see Exhibit 57).** 

**The BEAT Tax is actually relevant for TLAC (total loss absorbance capacity) issuance and that is the technicality raised in a letter from European Finance Ministers to Secretary Mnuchin last year.** The letter specifically mentioned TLAC issuance, but it is possible the media avoided the technical term TLAC and instead used the term funding, leading to a belief in the market that the clause is about funding in general. 

The foreign parent company is required by the Federal Reserve to issue a TLAC debt to its US subsidiary. It is basically long-term debt that absorbs losses in the event of bankruptcy. The idea is that if the US subsidiary fails, the Fed doesn’t want the foreign parent to just walk away, so they require the parent to have this TLAC debt tied up with the US entity. 

Parents are forced to issue this type of debt to the US subsidiary and cannot simply switch the issuance to the subsidiary. Thus, banks and EU finance ministers had feared that BEAT would punish them for interest on this debt that they are required to hold by TLAC regulations. 

## But there is a way around this. **The US subsidiary would have to be converted into a “resolution covered Intermediate Holding Company (IHC)” as opposed to a nonresolution covered IHC. This basically mean the subsidiary can enter bankruptcy** 

**proceedings on its own behalf.** If that reorganization is done, then the subsidiary would be able to issue its own TLAC debt rather be forced to borrow it from the parent entity. 

## **Indeed, we looked into the financial statements of European banks that have reported earnings. Their concern was that the BEAT tax is likely to offset some of the benefit** 

**from the lower US corporate tax rate.** Some of the banks mentioned that they will consider organizational changes to go around the issue. We suspect this change would be restructuring their US subsidiary (i.e., the IHC) as a "resolution covered IHC." This would allow the subsidiary to issue its own TLAC to the market (rather than to the foreign parent) and receive a tax deduction on the interest associated with this TLAC debt. 

We see evidence that there has been an increase in USD TLAC-type debt issuance from US subsidiaries of foreign banks so far this year, but it is too early to tell if this is a trend. It will likely become a trend, but, again, this should have no impact on money markets or Libor-OIS spreads in our view.

## **How is Libor set** 

We believe part of the reason why Libor-OIS has widened as aggressively as it did is the methodology now used to set Libor. 

The ICE took over the management of Libor from the BBA in 2014 and has worked to standardize the submission process ever since. As a result, a waterfall methodology was introduced that requires banks to base their submission on Level 1 data (primary market CP/CD issuance within the past few days) if available, and if not, the submitting banks have to rely on Level 2, then Level 3. 

Only 40% of submissions are based on Level 1, no submissions use Level 2 according to ICE, and 60% of submissions are based on Level 3. Given that Libor is a trimmed mean, it could be argued that Libor would mostly be based on the inputs used for Level 3 submissions. 

Exhibit 63 shows that Level 3 includes suggested inputs such as repo and FX swaps. This leads to an almost sure relationship between repo funding pressures and Libor levels. 

## **We also believe the waterfall methodology introduces a positive feedback loop in Libor that could lead to aggressive and volatile Libor trends in the years to come (both in** 

**widening and tightening).** This is because IR futures and FRAs are suggested inputs in a setting mechanism. To see why this is the case, imagine that banks consider the implied FRAOIS spread from the March 2018 contract to set Libor today, then widening in the March 2018 FRAOIS would likely lead to higher Libor submissions leading to a higher set in Libor. Since market participants look at the Libor setting to form expectations about forward FRAOIS spreads, the March 2018 FRAOIS is likely to widen as a result of the higher Libor setting, and so on and so forth.
