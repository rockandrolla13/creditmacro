## Introducing Credit Bond Market Indicator (credit-BMI) 

We present a framework to guide tactical recommendations on the US credit market. Our work shows predicting short-term moves is not a clear-cut task by any means, but we try to keep things simple by (1) focusing on just a few factors that have worked best in predicting short-term moves historically and (2) relying on a set of rules to guide our tactical views. After evaluating about 60 variables, we arrived at four key factors as signals for the one-month ahead direction of US credit excess returns in the following categories – volatility, momentum, sentiment, and liquidity. We standardize each variable, combine all four into our credit Bond Market Indicator (credit-BMI) and devise rules to guide tactical decisions based on the level of the score. Our key takeaways: 

**Bull vs. Bear-ometer** : credit-BMI scores range from -10 (max short) to +10 (max long). More extreme scores have higher success rates in predicting the direction of credit excess returns and are associated with larger magnitude spread changes. Hence, the absolute level of signal guides our degree of conviction for a tactical move ahead. 

**Risk-Adjusted Benefits of Tracking our Factors** : With a hit rate of 67% and 62% in correctly predicting the 1M ahead direction of excess returns for IG and HY, respectively, credit-BMI did not outperform in all environments. That said, our combined credit-BMI has a better hit rate than any of the four components included in the model, given relatively low correlations among the variables. 

**Current Signal – Constructive, but with Low Conviction** : Our credit-BMI model currently suggests being long credit over the next month, but with relatively low conviction. While volatility, momentum, and sentiment suggest being long, the liquidity/funding signal suggests a more cautious stance.

## Variable Selection 

**What’s in a credit spread?** To select variables for our model, we began by thinking about risks reflected in credit spread valuations. Over the long-term, credit spreads should theoretically comprise three main risk components: default, market, and liquidity (note: For our related work on spread attribution, please refer to A Premium for Liquidity, 1.28.2015). If markets were perfectly efficient, credit spreads would reflect “fair value” for each of these components. In reality, credit spreads rarely trade at “fair value”. In the short term especially, factors such as technicals and sentiment may cause valuations to “overshoot” fair value in either direction. Therefore, in our analysis, in addition to drivers of long-term fair value (i.e., default risk, market risk and liquidity risk), we also focused on factors more technical in nature that could impact directional moves. 

**Initial variables we considered** : We evaluated ~60 variables as candidates for drivers of short-term moves in credit spreads. These variables: (1) have been successful in predicting credit spread changes in other studies (as cited throughout this note), (2) are inputs in our other models (e.g., sentiment index, Getting Sentimental, 1.10.2016), and (3) are other metrics our team tracks. Given the goal to anticipate short-term market moves, we restricted our collection of explanatory variables to those with a high frequency (daily) time series and variables available in “real time” (i.e., at the end of each day vs. with a lag). And in order to “back-test” variables, we preferred metrics with time series data back at least two credit cycles or more. 

**Evaluating initial variables:** For the initial set of ~60 variables, we ran correlations and simple linear regressions of each variable (and changes of each variable) against 1Mahead IG and HY excess returns under various look-back periods. This was not an exhaustive approach, but a method to narrow our focus to those variables that were relatively better candidates for inclusion in our tactical model. Based on these results, we focused on a subset of potential explanatory variables. We standardized each raw variable (see Constructing credit-BMI ) and calculated each variable’s “hit ratio”, or the percentage of time an indicator correctly signaled a move wider or tighter in credit spreads. We excluded those variables correlated with each other (assumes a maximum 80% correlation threshold). 

Ultimately we arrived at four factors (see Exhibit 5 below) across the following categories: volatility, momentum, sentiment, and liquidity (see Variable Selection ). We are not suggesting these are the only important factors credit investors should track to inform tactical views. But based on our work, these signals have worked relatively well in the past, are available in “real time” for more than one credit cycle, and do not violate our tests for correlation.

## Constructing credit-BMI 

In constructing credit-BMI, we borrow from an approach developed by our interest rate strategy team (see Our Bond Market Indicators: A Powerful Systemic Approach, 3.6.2015, Hornbach). We convert each raw variable (summarized in Exhibit 5) into a z- 

score . Next, we use a cumulative distribution function to assign scores of -10 to 10 over1 time to that z-score. We create positioning “rules” based on the value of the score. We eliminate “weak” signals that hover near zero in the range of -1 to 1. We define a signal above 1 a “long”, while a signal below -1 is a “short”. Lastly, to combine the factors into one credit-BMI, we take the simple average of the four signals. We considered various weighting systems, but prioritize simplicity for our model, and therefore take the simple average.

## The Indicators 

Below we explain the intuition behind the selection of each variable included in our model and a summary of its historical success in working as an indicator for 1M ahead credit excess returns. 

## 1- Volatility 

**Intuition** : Volatility measures may influence perceived default risk, which could drive moves in credit spreads. “Structural models” affirm this intuition, as these models theorize debt is “a combination of a risk-free loan and short put option on the company.” 

2 Therefore, greater volatility increases the value of the put option in a structural model, which theoretically is linked to wider credit spreads (and vice versa). As a caveat, we assume volatility is positively correlated to spread moves, as we find this is often the case in the very short term (and we are attempting to predict very short-term spread moves), but acknowledge that an extreme reading on the VIX can eventually signal that a turn in markets is coming. 

**Variable:** We use the VIX Index as a proxy for volatility, as past studies have found lagged daily changes of stock market volatility to be positively correlated with subsequent increases in credit spreads . Specifically, we assume 1M changes in the3 absolute level of the VIX and a z-score for the full period back to 1999 to create our signal. We also considered volatility measures specific to credit markets, such as implied volatility of CDX. We chose the VIX for our volatility indicator because it is available far back in time, is relatively easy to track for most investors, and is most similar to “total company volatility” assumed in a structural model. 

**Back-test Results** : Since 1999, the volatility signal has a hit rate of 64% and 59% in correctly signaling the direction of 1M excess returns for IG and HY. Hit rates are generally better for “stronger” scores (Exhibit 6) and stronger scores were associated with larger-magnitude moves in 1M ahead spreads (Exhibit 7). The signal has a better track record for long (vs. short) directional recommendations, as shown in Exhibit 6.

## 2 - Momentum 

**Intuition** : Fund flows and returns may cluster together in credit markets, with flows chasing positive returns, and fleeing from negative returns. To the extent credit returns are self-fullfilling in the near term, momentum may be predictive of returns 1M ahead. Investors have used momentum strategies in other asset classes such as equities for some time . Momentum strategies are relatively newer to credit markets with mixed4 effectiveness . For example, within HY, past six-month winners outperform past six-5 month losers over a 6-month holding period, but results were statistically insignificant 

for IG issuers.  Our analysis differs from other work on credit momentum available in6 that we (1) test momentum as an indicator of broad market returns (vs. cross-sectional), and (2) focus on a shorter timeframe (just 1M ahead instead of a longer horizon such as 6M ahead). 

**Variable** : We use a decaying moving average of HY monthly excess returns over the previous 4 months, divided by a volatility metric, for our momentum variable. We assume a 1-week implementation lag, and do not use a z-score but directly normalize the raw variable. This approach is similar to a momentum calculation assumed here (see Our Bond Market Indicators: A Powerful Systemic Approach, 3.6.2015, Hornbach). 

**Back-test Results** : The momentum signal worked among the best in terms of hit rates, correctly signaling the direction of excess returns for IG and HY 62% and 61% of the time, respectively. Similar to the volatility signal, momentum has a better hit rate as a “long” strategy, versus a short. Despite a relatively strong hit rate, the momentum indicator is vulnerable to missing a sharp change in market direction. By definition, the momentum signal assumes the trend in excess credit returns with a 1-week implementation lag. Hence it's not surprising to see the momentum indicator fail to predict some sharp directional changes, such as during the most recent correction in credit in February 2018. The momentum indicator was the last of all our indicators to shift from recommending a “buy”, turning cautious on 2/21/18 after HY spreads had already moved wider 60bp (from 1/26/18 to 2/9/18).

## 3 - Sentiment 

**Intuition** : Looking forward just one month, we find changes in sentiment have a positive relationship to credit excess returns. In other words, sentiment trending in a positive/bullish direction is more likely to signal tighter spreads, while sentiment trending more cautious/bearish signals a move wider. However, when sentiment hits extreme bullish/bearish levels, it may actually be a “contra-indicator” for risk assets. Eventually if positioning matches sentiment, the market may be more vulnerable to surprises in either direction (see Leveraged Finance Insights: Getting Sentimental, 1.10.2014). 

**Variable** : For sentiment, we track the relative performance of risk assets across two risk markets: (1) EM vs G10 Bonds and (2) G10 Equity vs Bonds. Of the 10 sub-indices in Morgan Stanley’s Global Risk Demand Index (GRDI), these two worked best in predicting 1M ahead credit excess returns, and are also not correlated with the other variables in our model. Studies have shown the S&P 500 returns as a statistically significant 

predictor of credit spreads , which supports including it in our model. To create this7 indicator, we first take the geometric average of two indices mentioned, 1) EM vs G10 Bonds (“GRDI1 Index”) and (2) G10 Equity vs Bonds (“GRDI3 Index”). Next, we try to capture two opposing dynamics of how sentiment may impact 1M ahead credit excess returns: 

**1.** We first test if sentiment is at an “extreme” level, based on the z-score of the absolute level of our combined sentiment index.  This differs from #2 below in8 that we focus on the absolute level of sentiment vs. changes, and our look-back period is 6 months. If sentiment hits a level we consider "extreme", we automatically assume sentiment as a contra-indicator for credit excess returns going forward (i.e., very bullish sentiment is a sell signal). Otherwise, we assume sentiment is positively related to returns per the calculation in #2 below. 

**2.** If sentiment is not extreme (see #1), we assume sentiment changes are positively related to 1M ahead credit excess returns. To create the signal, we calculate the 1M change of the combined sentiment index, take a z-score over the full period, and convert the value to a score of -10 to 10. Relatively positive changes in sentiment are a buy signal and vice versa. We assume this variable as our sentiment indicator, unless overall sentiment hits extreme levels, as explained in #1 above.

## 4 - Liquidity 

**Intuition** : Weaker funding conditions may increase the liquidity risk premium priced into credit markets. We have explored the relationship of liquidity dynamics and credit valuations in detail in A Premium for Liquidity, 1.28.2015. 

**Variable** : Liquidity metrics specific to the corporate bond market are hard to come by, especially with a daily frequency and going back for more than one credit cycle. We assume LIBOR OIS (“USBG5 Curncy”), the average expected spread between 3M LIBOR and the Fed Funds rates over 5 years, for our liquidity indicator. When LIBOR OIS widens, it may suggest funding stress, which could be linked to liquidity conditions generally in the corporate bond market. We also considered using swap spreads for this variable, but choose LIBOR OIS as it may be less tied to flows. We note past studies have shown swap spreads to be statistically significant in predicting credit spread 

changes.  To create the indicator, we take 1M absolute changes in the variable,10 calculate the z-score over the full period back to 1999, and normalize the score between -10 to 10. 

**Back-test** : The liquidity signal has the lowest hit rate of all the indicators, correctly predicting 1M ahead changes just 55% and 51% of the time for IG and HY, respectively. However, this indicator was important in signaling certain market moves that were large in magnitude, such as prior to the selloff in credit during 2011 and the taper tantrum in 2013.

## Other Variables We Considered 

Our credit-BMI includes 4 indicators, but we tested several other variables that we did not include in our final model. These variables may still be important for the direction in credit, but generally (1) are predictive over a longer-term time horizon (vs. our onemonth-ahead focus) or (2) have a “coincident” (vs. predictive) relationship to credit excess returns. 

**Economic backdrop** : GDP growth is a statistically significant predictor of long-term credit spread changes and a factor we include in our top-down 12M-ahead credit spread forecasts. Since GDP growth is only available quarterly, we tested alternative metrics that capture economic trends but are available in real time, such as the economic surprise index. An economic surprise indicator had a hit rate of about 50% in predicting 1M ahead credit excess returns, with no pattern of higher hit rates at greater scores, and so we do not include this variable in our model. 

**Interest rates** : “Higher rates, tighter spreads” is generally a rule of thumb in credit, but we find the relationship between rates and credit returns is not necessarily predictive over the short term. Nominal interest rates do have a statistically significant relationship in predicting changes in credit spreads, with an increase in the risk-free rate lowering credit spreads regardless of rating.11 This makes sense as nominal interest rates may reflect perception of economic health, which is tied to default and recovery risk. That said, we did not find a strong predictive relationship in the short term between nominal rates and 1M ahead excess returns. Changes in yield curve slope may also impact credit spreads, but the directional (positive or negative) relationship of curve slope to spreads is inconclusive.12 We also considered real yields as a candidate for our model, as studies have found a significant relationship between real rates and default rates.13 At the end of the day, after testing several variables related to interest rates for our model, we concluded none is a great indicator for short-term moves. 

**Moving Averages** : We also tested moving averages such as 50-DMA/200-DMA crossovers, current spreads vs. 50-DMA, and current spreads vs. 200-DMA as indicator in our model. With a hit rate of ~50% since 2010, we conclude moving averages are not optimal tactical indicators, a similar conclusion reached by our cross-asset team (see Do Moving Averages Work?, 10.29.2017). 

**Oil** : With 15% and 10% of HY and IG par in the energy sector, respectively, oil prices have been an important factor of moves in credit this cycle. We created an oil indicator based on the 1M percentage changes in crude oil prices. Focusing on oil as an indicator solely in 2014, the indicator correctly signaled the 1M direction of excess returns 75% and 63% of the time for IG and HY, respectively. However, outside of the recent 2014-2016 energy default cycle, the oil indicator had a much lower hit rate and therefore we do not include it in our model. 

**Relative Strength Index** : In addition to momentum of excess returns, we considered other momentum indicators such as RSI (Relative Strength Index) for credit, a momentum oscillator which measures recent price strength relative to preceding prices 

12 

(see Do Extreme RSI Levels Matter?, 1.25.2018). The hit ratio for the RSI indicator was lower compared to using momentum of excess returns, and so this is not the best indicator for momentum in our model. 

**Supply** : A boost in supply could create a more challenging technical backdrop in markets, which could bias credit spreads wider. We tested net supply (defined as HY new money less refinancing) but did not find it to be reliable predictor of 1M changes in credit spreads. 

**Valuations:** Factor investing strategies have shown allocating based on a “valuation” metric provides statistically significant returns within the corporate bond market .14 Intuitively, it makes sense spreads may be more likely to tighten (widen) if credit is trading relatively wide (tight) to fair value. We tested various valuation metrics as predictors of 1M ahead excess returns for the broad credit markets (versus for crosssectional returns on which other studies have focused). Specifically, we looked at (1) valuations adjusted for fundamental credit quality (assumed as spread per leverage) and (2) a rich/cheap valuation metric assumed as the actual credit spread vs. our estimate for fair value. Neither of these variables worked well in predicting 1M ahead short-term moves, consistent with our view that valuations can remain dislocated for some time and not the best indicator for tactical decisions.
