## **Corporate Credit Research | North America** The Cause of the Pause 

The recent dovish shift in Fed rhetoric was likely a key contributor to the bounce in credit. However, longer term, remember the reason the Fed stops hiking may ultimately matter more than the policy shift itself. This week, we explore the impact of changes in Fed policy in late-cycle environments. 

**First, a word on our tactical call:** The easy money has likely already been made in this bear market rally. To the extent credit spreads continue to tighten, lessliquid risk may have to do the heavy lifting, and we do not want to chase this part of markets. That said, while we do not think this is a bad time to again start lightening up on credit, we are holding off on a bigger short-term sell call, at least for now. While we fully expect volatility to remain elevated, that is not the same as calling for the next big move wider in spreads in this bear market. 

**Credit bear markets continue beyond the last hike:** Toward the end of 2018, we repeatedly heard the argument that if the Fed backed off in 2019, credit would do okay. We agreed that a dovish shift could boost an oversold market. But longer term, we see it differently. Remember, the bulk of the spread widening in a cycle actually typically happens from the point when the Fed finishes hiking until it is deep in a rate-cutting cycle. Why? Simply because that is when growth expectations are typically rolling over and recession risks are rising fastest. 

**Is this time different?** Of course, we can’t forget that monetary policy in this cycle has been unique. The Fed is not just tightening with rate hikes but also by shrinking the balance sheet at the same time. And given the meaningful negative impact that balance sheet normalization has had on markets, in our view, it is possible that the end of quantitative tightening could serve as more of a tailwind for credit than the end of rate hikes has in past cycles. Or said another way, QT may pull forward some of the spread widening that typically would have occurred only after the economic data turned more notably. 

**It’s still ultimately about growth:** However, we still firmly believe that the key question for 2019 is NOT simply whether the Fed will or will not keep tightening, but instead, the extent of the slowdown in growth and earnings this year, and whether markets interpret this slowdown as just a step on the way to an ultimate end of the cycle. In fact, we think the bull case for credit in 2019 is an improvement in growth expectations, even if that means the Fed ends up hiking a few more times than the market currently expects. The bear case, in our view, is that growth rolls over more than we anticipate, even if that means the Fed is done tightening.

## Update on Our Tactical Call 

We have had a cautious long-term view on US credit for some time, and have not changed that stance. We think credit is in a bear market, and that a long, slow turn in the credit cycle has likely begun. However, given much more bearish sentiment to end 2018, as well as significant spread widening in 4Q18, we turned tactically constructive on US credit on January 7 (see: US Corporate Credit Strategy Brief: Finally Fair, 2019.01.07). In a nutshell, we thought it was unlikely that HY would go from cycle tights to distressed valuations in the course of 4-5 months. That is typically not how bear markets work. And with HY yielding around 8% to start the year and IG spreads near 160bp, the cost of waiting for that next big leg wider was much higher. Ultimately, sentiment moves sharply in both directions in bear markets, and after the negativity that built up in the fourth quarter, we thought markets were due for a swing back in the other direction. Though we did not pinpoint a specific catalyst, we noted that the ~20% sell-off in the S&P 500 made it more likely that Fed rhetoric would continue shifting in the dovish direction and that trade tensions could de-escalate, at the margin. 

To be honest, we did not expect such a quick snapback in such a short period of time. HY is ~115bp tighter since the wides on 1/4/19 (full disclosure: 40bp of the tightening came the day before we published our tactical report), loans are over 50bp tighter, and IG is about 20bp tighter. So now what? The easy money has been made, in our view. To the extent credit spreads continue to tighten, less-liquid credits may have to do the heavy lifting, and we do not want to chase illiquid risk. That said, while we don't think this is a bad time to again start lightening up on credit, we are holding off on a bigger short-term sell call, at least for now. We fully expect volatility to remain elevated, but that is not the same as expecting the next big move wider in spreads in this bear market. 

While there is clearly no set rule, looking back in time, these tactical rallies tended to last at least a month, and often much longer. At the risk of being too greedy, we were hoping to see sentiment turn even more bullish again before making a bigger short-term sell call – where the consensus is again highly confident that the cycle is intact, that recession risks are low, and that the Fed is ahead of the curve. Yes, investors are far more optimistic today than at the end of 2018, but we would argue still somewhat guarded. As just one example, the Morgan Stanley GRDI index (Global Risk Demand Index – US Pat. No. 7,617,143) has increased from -2.98 on 12/24/18 to +0.64 today, but still below the ~2.0x reading that has been consistent with heightened bullishness (and hence short-term market tops) in the past. 

Of course, it is possible that sentiment never gets to these lofty levels in the near term. What could get us there? As one example, we think a trade deal (or trade de-escalation) in the next month coupled with an end to the government shutdown could lead investors to believe that policy headwinds are fading. And if we did get a big bounce in sentiment on the back of these types of headlines, that is something we would sell into, as we think investors would soon realize that while trade risk may have exacerbated the other challenges that markets are facing, it is not the primary issue. In our view, the bigger story is that liquidity conditions have been tightening and now growth is slowing fairly quickly, while the risk of an earnings recession has risen, and none of these challenges are going to disappear so easily.

## A Dovish Tilt – Implications for Credit 

Given the substantial tightening in financial conditions in 4Q18 and the resulting shift in communication from Fed policymakers, our economists now believe that the Fed will pause its rate-hiking cycle. Specifically, they no longer believe the Fed will hike in March, but instead are assuming the next hike comes in June (see: US Economics & Rates Strategy: Patiently Pushing Out Hikes, 2019.01.15, for the full revised forecasts). Additionally, we stick with our long-held view that the Fed will need to stop balance sheet normalization in September of this year as well. 

On the surface, this outcome should be bullish for credit. And we think many investors see it that way. During our outlook meetings in December, many investors made the point that if the Fed remains on its hawkish path, credit will struggle, but if they back off, markets should do fine. We think it is not quite that straightforward. 

In the short term, we agree that this dovish shift in Fed rhetoric was a key contributor to the easing in financial conditions since the start of the year. In late December when sentiment was most bearish, we heard growing concerns that the Fed could be making a policy mistake, with some even arguing at the time that the Fed put is gone. As we noted in the report cited on the prior page, the Fed may have shown investors that the put is further out of the money than some had previously assumed. But at the same time, we made the case that the sell-off in the S&P 500 was very likely enough to drive a shift in Fed rhetoric, which we assumed would help markets temporarily, especially given how negative sentiment became towards the end of 2018. 

However, thinking a bit longer term, in our view, it is not only about the Fed. Yes, all else equal, easier monetary policy should help credit. But, in fact, as we show below, the bulk of the spread widening in a cycle actually happens from the point when the Fed finishes hiking until it is deep in a rate-cutting cycle. Why? Simply, because that is the point when growth is typically rolling over and recession risks are rising. In other words, the reason the Fed stops hiking and/or begins cutting can matter as much, if not more for markets, in our view, than the policy shift itself.

Of course, we can’t forget that monetary policy in this cycle has been unique. The Fed is not just tightening with rate hikes but also by shrinking the balance sheet at the same time. And given the meaningful impact that balance sheet normalization has had on markets (a key reason for our bearish credit view in 2018), it is possible that the end of QT could serve as more of a tailwind for credit than the end of rate hikes has in past cycles. 

Unfortunately, we can’t use historical examples to help us assess how markets will perform when this tightening cycle fully ends (both rate hikes AND balance sheet normalization) because we are in such uncharted waters. We think it is very possible that QT may pull forward some of the spread widening that typically would have occurred only after the economic data turned more notably. **However, we still firmly believe that the key question for 2019 is NOT simply whether the Fed will or will not keep tightening, but instead the extent of the slowdown in growth and earnings this year, and whether markets interpret this slowdown as a just step on the way to an ultimate end of the cycle.** 

We think the current cycle provides several examples supporting this view. Yes, shifts in Fed policy have clearly impacted markets and financial conditions and will continue to do so. But as we show below in a chart we have used many times, the two major selloffs in credit in this cycle, when IG and HY traded as wide as ~250bp and ~850bp, respectively, came in 2011 and 2016 when growth expectations turned sharply lower.

We think price action in HY in 2018 tells a similar story. For example, monetary policy was getting tighter all year, yet HY only cracked in the fourth quarter. Some attributed the resilience of HY for much of 2018 to the lack of supply. And while very low new issuance likely helped, remember supply was also minimal in the fourth quarter. Not a single HY bond was issued in the month of December. So what changed in the final few months of the year? The Fed did step up the pace of QT, which likely contributed to the weakness in markets, but we think the bigger change was the notable shift in growth expectations that occurred heading into year-end. 

Remember, for most of 2018, investors could always fall back on the belief that markets would have a hard time completely unraveling with a booming US economy and 20%+ earnings growth. Hence, the most levered credits in particular were able to outperform. We think it is no coincidence that HY (and especially CCCs) finally turned, right about when investors started to worry about a peak in growth, a peak in earnings growth, and rising margin pressures. 

Adding everything up, we continue to believe that tighter Fed policy and especially the shrinking of the Fed’s balance sheet were meaningful headwinds for credit in 2018, and if these headwinds go away, all else equal, that should help. But we should not forget how cycle turns typically work. At some point the Fed stops tightening and even starts cutting rates because growth is rolling over. But at the same time, that is often when earnings growth turns, weak balance sheet quality become a more near-term problem, and default expectations start rising, driving spreads wider, DESPITE easier central bank policy. At this stage, the credit bear market becomes less about technicals (weaker flows on the back of tightening liquidity conditions) and more about fundamentals (rising credit stress), and as we argued in our 2019 outlook, we think the shift from technicals to fundamentals began late in 2018, and may continue over the course of 2019. 

With this all in mind, we actually think the bull case for credit in 2019 is an improvement in growth expectations, even if that means the Fed ends up hiking 1 or 2 more times than the market currently expects. The bear case, in our view, is that growth rolls over more than we expect, even if that means the Fed stops tightening for the cycle at the same time.

## Investment Implications 

What are the investment implications? First, if the market continues to believe the Fed is done with its rate-hiking cycle for now, and growth expectations continue to weaken, high-beta credit should eventually start to underperform once again, as it did in 4Q18. We remain up-in-quality. 

Second, while the Fed is hiking rates and investors are worried about rising rates, flows into the loan market should outpace flows into HY. But when the market starts to expect the end of rate hikes and/or rate cuts as well as lower Treasury yields, loan technicals should weaken more meaningfully at the margin, as we saw in the last six weeks of 2018 when loans funds experienced $13 billion in outflows. Note, we shifted our preference from loans to HY earlier this month.
