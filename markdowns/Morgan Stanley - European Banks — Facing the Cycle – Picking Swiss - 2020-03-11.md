## Facing the Cycle – Picking Swiss 

AT1s are down 7-16 points since February highs and we've not yet seen investors step in in size. With an eye on credit costs rising and an even more challenging rate environment, we recommend tentative first steps into a few of those AT1s at z- spreads wider than Jan 2019 – the Swiss low-triggers. 

**Our economists now expect an ECB rate cut** , with 10bp pencilled in for tomorrow, considering the significant oil price fall, falling inflation expectations and spreading Covid-19. They also perceive downside risks to their already downwardly revised growth forecasts – which put 2020 euro area growth at just 0.4%Y, with a 1H recession. However, the rate cut ought to be accompanied by mitigation measures such as raising the exemption multiplier of the tiering system, extra QE (an additional €20 billion/month) and new TLTROs. 

**Our bank equity colleagues have highlighted which banks are most exposed** to the most challenged sectors and the potential impact on earnings. We include the key exhibits here and also update our simplistic 'stressed cost of risk' (CoR) analysis, which looks at what a bank's CoR would have to increase to, to wipe out its first line of defence, pre-provision operating profits. We note the worst CoRs we've seen over the GFC and sovereign crises to give some context, and also make note of the second-line defences, excess capital above supervisory requirements – a topic crucial for AT1 holders, and one we'll return to. 

**Adding the strongest – if not the cheapest – AT1s at this early stage:** Bad news is still to come, and as discussed in the latest Cross-Asset Brief, our biotechnology analysts believe that Covid-19 cases could peak in Japan, Korea and Italy later this month, and in the US in April. Our new economic forecasts have growth recovering in 2H, but still weak for now. We already cut our AT1 exposures at end-January – still keeping a core group of buys – simply as we had approached historical tights, selling Italian, Spanish and Finnish names. Our credit strategists have cautioned that this is not a "dip" to buy, and they recommend maintaining an up-in-quality stance. In our view, adding (often rare) low-trigger (LT) Swiss AT1s here gives us good-quality underlying credits and absolute best AT1 structures. 

**Around 10% of AT1s are trading cheap to late 2018-early 2019 spread wides** – a necessary condition to improve the broader credit market valuation case, as argued by our strategists. Of this handful, we buy $ LT AT1s – Credit Suisse 7.5% Dec 23s (admittedly tighter than Jan 19 – but 4bp only) and UBS 7% Feb 25s. Switzerland – outside the EU – changed its own AT1 rules years ago, making LT (CET1 of 5.125%) non-compliant as AT1 from the call date. All LT Swiss AT1s have been called at the first call date. Furthermore, these AT1s (unlike EU AT1s) have dividend stoppers, and the MDA level where coupons are cancelled is well below EU banks, at just 7% (both banks are well above 12% CET1, and 10% is in any case the regulatory minimum).

## Credit cycle sensitivity 

The credit cycle is back in focus, with the expected slowdown in economic growth linked to coronavirus and associated containment measures leading to questions over how well placed banks are to weather a slowdown in the economic cycle. While much focus has been linked to a broader-based economic slowdown, the recent sharp drop in oil prices has understandably caused a focus on a sector-specific slowdown in energy.

## Stressing the credit cycle 

At the time of our Outlook in November last year, a eurozone recession was not in our minds and we ran our analysis of stressing the cost of risk (CoR) more as an interesting exercise, simply based on the fact that many banks' CoRs are around historical lows. **Recession is now the base case for Europe** – a 1H recession with 2020 euro area growth at just 0.4%Y. While simplistic, our credit cycle stress analysis provided at least some parameters to what the banks are currently expected to earn and provision, versus what it would take to wipe those earnings out fully, and hence we repeat it here with updated figures, considering the worsened environment. 

## Stressed cost of risk – what it would take to wipe out PPOP 

We begin with a set of unadulterated ModelWare numbers from our equity colleagues – pre-provision operating profit (PPOP, FY20e, 21e) and then a 2021e cost of risk (CoR) calculated from 2021e loan loss provisions (LLP) and estimated average net loans. We then calculate the **stressed CoR** – what the CoR would have to move to in FY21 in order to wipe out the bank's PPOP. The next, simplistic step is to calculate that stressed CoR as a multiple of the current expected CoR. The closer to 1 this is, the more at risk a bank is – all else held equal – for wiping out its PPOP. We have coloured the column showing this from dark red to dark green. Monte dei Paschi is darkest red with a multiple of 1.8x – the current expected CoR in FY21e would need to rise by 80% for the bank's PPOP to be wiped out. At the other end of the scale is UBS, whose expected CoR would have to go up by a factor of over 38 times in order to wipe out PPOP. 

This takes no account of exposures, by type or geography, and no account whatsoever of any other risks the banks are running, for example in trading books or with litigation risk, so is meant as a simple indicator of CoR sensitivities. **Looking at where UBS and CS rank on this measure, we feel comfortable recommending those bonds,** as we face a clear turn in the credit cycle. 

The **worst CoR** in the table denotes the peak of CoR for that bank, looking back over the past 12 years, which includes the sovereign crisis. We take some comfort where we can see the worst CoR is below the stressed CoR. As memo items, we have noted expected RoTEs and the NPL ratios of the banks to give the numbers some further context. 

## Second line of defence – capital 

A bank's PPOP is its first line of defence against losses, but banks have built up a solid second line over the past ten years. We detail the excess banks have above their binding capital requirements (in terms of CET1 excess above SREP) in bp of RWAs and in absolute € millions, again for context. For AT1 investors, this level is also known as the MDA level, where AT1 coupons can be cancelled. The lowest in bp is Lloyds, at 145bp, but this still translates into a required loss of €3.4 billion, to wipe it out – after the PPOP has been wiped out (a further €9.9 billion in 2021e). 

5 

We should also add that this set of data of course does not take into any account that banks' balance sheets have been for the most part thoroughly restructured since the years of the worst CoR, whether during the global financial crisis or the sovereign crises. We also ignore IFRS9, and while it will arguably be pro-cyclical and worsen provisioning requirements in the next recession, again, banks ought to be better provisioned running into it to start with – particularly given supervisory expectations of provisioning set at both a generic and a bank-specific level on both stock and new lending. Furthermore, supervisors appear to be looking at softening provisioning levels from here. Finally, we also ignore that in recessions banks will be allowed/encouraged to reduce capital below buffer levels. What we've already seen is the UK reducing its countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB), but note that the buffers are not part of a bank's MDA – they sit above that level.
