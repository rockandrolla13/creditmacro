The medium term outlook remains skewed towards simultaneous tightening and tapering, despite the dovish turn in recent weeks. But this will now be from very different national starting points as advanced economy central bank policy rates diverged in 2018, and large policy gaps persist. While rate paths look like they may vary from here, we think global central bank balance sheets have peaked. **From QE to QT: For the first time since 2008, global central bank asset purchases are likely to be negative in 2019.** Net asset purchases have fallen to zero after running ~$23bn per month in H2 2018 (down from a peak of $160bn in 2016). Purchases are now likely to turn negative in 2019 for the first time since 2008. Policy remains highly divergent.  Fed  balance sheet reduction began gradually but accelerated over the course of 2018. In contrast, the ECB only stopped net purchases in December, and has promised to initially reinvest fully. 

- **There are still major differences across central banks in both hiking cycles and balance sheet normalization.** While AE central banks have generally turned more dovish at the start of 2019, they have done so in unison - meaning past gaps remain. Policy, in particular, diverged markedly in 2018 with the US leading the hiking cycle, and the ECB and BoJ lagging. The gap between the US and Europe remains particularly marked. 

Though the Fed has moved in a more dovish, data driven, direction in recent weeks, there is little near term prospect of convergence. The ECB has promised not to raise rates until after the summer at least, and recently described the economic risks tilted to the downside (on the back of soft recent data). Diverging policy rate trajectories have been associated with/ reflected in large and growing long term interest rate differentials. **Divergence, alongside asset cross-correlations, increases the risk of turbulent, non-linear asset re-pricing. If the real economic impact of these changes is not limited, expect a reversal.  See:** Central Bank QE: Is Zero A Magic Number?

Central bank purchases affect prices through either _flow_ or _stock_ channels. With flows turning negative, but stocks sticky, understanding the respective impacts of each will be key going forward. We expect the first to drive asset prices among risk assets, and shallower markets, while the second to drive asset prices amongst risk-free assets in deeper markets. See: Don't take stock - mind the flows! 

Central bank research suggests QE works via two channels. These respective effects are influential to different degrees in different markets. 

For risk assets, **the signalling channel/ flow is the more influential** , especially in credit markets. For sovereigns, the flow effect has been less substantial, though this depends somewhat on the perceived credit risk of the specific bond, and depth of the market. Here the stock of assets is often more important, and there are fewer pressing concerns.

## Growth: Moderatin Robust but Financiall Threatened g, ( y) 

Increases in the cost of risk is driving more acute reactions in risk markets to changes in economic data. In recent months this has contributed to a tightening of financial conditions in response to moderating economic data. These reactions often look disproportionate in historical comparison. Unease around fundamental and institutional factors is compounding liquidity issues and contributing to volatility. 

- **Economic growth remains high, but compared to 2017/8 performance is beginning to moderate towards potential.** 

In 2018, global growth ended up around 3.2%, we currently expect growth in 2019 to moderate to around 3.0% - still well into expansionary territory. 

**There are some acute areas of economic concern including trade, Chinese slowdown and European growth. Despite these** 

- **issues, domestic growth appears to be resilient, with more domestically insulated parts of the global economy driving growth.** 

   - This is acutely dependent on investment. Investment is an important source of growth in its own right, but also key to labour productivity growth, essential to match ongoing wage momentum and offset potential inflationary pressures. 

**Markets have proven acutely sensitive to relatively moderate real economic changes/ news. Market turbulence and a tightening of financial conditions could put growth at risk. Market sensitivity reflects:** 

- QT, and a more general re-pricing of risk that has left markets vulnerable. 

- Trade and other economic concerns are also weighing, increasing more general risk aversion and heightening sensitivity. The risk is that such pessimism, and sensitivity, drives a tightening of financial conditions, strangling wider investment. 

**Such an environment can catalyse a reassessment of the fundamentals.** 

   - Fundamental institutional concerns are contributing to investor uncertainty, and subsequent risk aversion and volatility. 

   - Specific events, and an absence of liquidity, drive outsized market reactions. These can also catalyse new fundamental assessments 

   - Volatility can precede fundamental repricing.

## Inflation: Cyclical Dampeners Receding, Structural Uncertainty Remains 

In the post crisis period, changes in inflation have been remarkably subdued.  In the immediate post crisis period, we would have expected to see inflation fall by more than it did. ## WOMEN IN THE ECONOMY II 

In 2015 we published the Citi GPS report Women in the Economy: Global Growth Generators, which made the case that the role of women in the labour force should be incorporated as a mainstream topic within the debate on global growth for both economic as well as social reasons. In our new report, Women in the Economy II, we dig deeper into the economic case for female economic empowerment through some fresh insights on the key issues, as well as through a series of country case studies comparing the U.S. with Canada and Italy with Sweden. Alongside this, we have produced a standalone investigation of the issues facing Japan, where harnessing the potential of women in the economy will be vital to protecting even modest growth prospects in the country. 

We remain wary of the more exaggerated growth estimates driven by the simple assumption that direct female labour force participation and productivity can be raised fully to the current level of males. Instead, we estimate that significant, and in our view achievable, reductions in workplace gender inequalities could perhaps add around 6% to GDP in the advanced economies over the course of one or two decades a very significant number relative to the potential of other structural reforms or to the relatively depressed growth prospects in advanced economies more generally.
