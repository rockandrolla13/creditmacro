**Time is running out:** To assess the likelihood of stabilisation in Europe, we think it is critical to understand that we are in a situation of a ‘run’ on banks and governments. We believe that only decisive action by governments to create a lender of last resort for the latter can stop this run. 

“That it is necessary to revise and amend the Articles of Confederation I entertain _n_ o doubt. But what may be the consequences of such an attempt _is_ doubtful. Yet something must be done, or the fabrick must fall.” 

George Washington (Letter to John Jay) May 18, 1786 

**Europe’s Hamiltonian moment:** December 9 therefore marks the bifurcation point we announced one year ago when Europe has to choose between debt _assumption_ (enhanced federal control of national budgets accompanied by centralised funding of governments) and a debt _jubilee_ (wide-scale debt repudiation), with all the social, economic and political consequences this entails. 

**Only fiscal federalism will do,** in our view: A true lender of last resort means a mechanism that ensures _solvent_ governments will never go _illiquid_ . We demonstrate that it implies _both_ permanent and binding control over the fiscal stance of national governments and a central mechanism to keep them funded at affordable rates. This is fiscal federalism. 

**Balance is the crux of the issue:** We detail in this note a scheme whereby fiscal federalism can operate. This is _not_ a forecast of what will be decided. It is a _benchmark_ against which to assess policy decisions. The key criterion to assess whether those decisions are stabilising or not is whether they balance fiscal control with a permanent funding mechanism for sovereigns. 

**Beginning of the end?** If governments choose to engage on the route to fiscal federalism, we believe that this does not mark the end of the crisis. It could, however, mark the beginning of the end of the crisis, as it would be a decisive first step towards stabilisation and a European federation. The alternative could well be the beginning of the end for the European confederation.

**Europe’s Hamiltonian moment has come:** One year ago, we wrote that Europe was heading fast towards a crossroads where it would have to choose between a debt _jubilee_ (widescale debt repudiation) and debt _assumption_ (a form of fiscal federalism with enhanced control over national budgets accompanied by centralised funding of governments). We think this bifurcation point has now been reached. By December 9, heads of state and government ought to outline how they intend to amend the constitutional arrangements under which Europe – or at the very least the euro area – operates. Should they set out a clear, consistent and workable policy orientation, which includes _both_ fiscal control and a mechanism to keep compliant governments fully funded, this can in our view constitute a decisive first step towards stabilisation. Should they fail to deliver a credible framework encompassing _both_ these dimensions, we would expect that the ongoing ‘run’ on governments and banks will accelerate, and it is seriously to be feared that it can no longer be stopped. The economic, social and political consequences could be unfathomable. The next few weeks are therefore a critical moment in European history, in our view. 

**Our goal is to provide an interpretation grid to assess decisions to come:** The purpose of this note is not to try to forecast the outcome of the EU summit of December 9. It is to provide an interpretation grid for the decisions that will be taken by then. To do this, we outline the minimum conditions that we believe _must_ and in our view _can_ be met for the current run to be stopped and reversed. These conditions can serve as a benchmark against which to assess the outcome of the forthcoming summit and subsequent actions by the governments and the ECB. Most aspects of our demonstration will likely seem familiar to our readers, if only because it builds up on themes and concepts that we have discussed throughout the past 18 months, in this _Sovereign Subjects_ series as well as in numerous publications by Joachim Fels, Elga Bartsch, Huw van Steenis and Laurence Mutkin. 

**It is critical to understand that we are in a situation of a run:** It is impossible to identify a genuinely stabilising solution to the current crisis if it is not acknowledged that the situation that we are experiencing is a run, by which we mean a selffulfilling spiral of destruction of confidence. This run affects both governments and banks. We described in past months how the run was set in motion by the decision to resort to default in Greece – ‘private sector involvement’ is in our view a sovereign default in all but name – without simultaneously 

creating a credible backstop funding facility for solvent governments (see _Sovereign Subjects: The Economic Consequences of Greece_ , August 31, 2011). 

**Breaking the taboo of euro exit intensified the run:** Since then, there has been another development that has added to the fragility of the situation and further contributed to the run. We refer here to the decision by Angela Merkel and Nicolas Sarkozy to break the taboo whereby no country can ever leave the euro, when they responded to George Papandreou’s suggestion to hold a referendum in Greece on the conditions of its bail-out by indicating that if the Greek people were offered a choice, it had to be on whether they chose to be in or out of the euro area. The notion that it is possible (even if improbable) that a country leaves the euro area creates the conditions for an acceleration of the run on banks. The reasoning is as follows: 

**Causing cracks in the fungibility of money:** Money in a fiat money system is a liability of a bank: of the central bank for banknotes and other forms of central bank money; of a commercial bank for commercial money (deposits). 

If one or more countries can leave the euro, or at the extreme if the euro can break up altogether, this raises questions as to whether these different forms of money are fully fungible. Is one euro of deposit in a Greek bank still fully fungible with one euro in banknote form? Is one euro of deposit in a Greek bank still fully fungible with one euro of deposit in an Italian bank, and is the latter still fungible with one euro of deposit in a French bank, or a German bank, etc.? Pursuing this reasoning leads to the inescapable conclusion that the more plausible it is that a country can leave the euro, the greater the incentives for depositors to move their deposits from the banks of almost all countries towards those of the country that remains the safe haven by convention if for no better reason: Germany. We believe that such behaviour, in turn, would (and judging by anecdotal information received from our clients, does) increase funding pressure on all banks, intensify incentives to accelerate the de-leveraging of the banking system and raise further the spectre of a credit crunch. 

Needless to say, the faster the banking system de-levers, the greater the downward pressure on growth, and the less likely that confidence comes back by itself in the ability of governments to stabilise debt metrics. Hence, the run continues to expand, affecting both banks and governments, not just in peripheral European countries but, as events have evidenced, almost everywhere.

**How does one stop a run?** If the run is not stopped, it is to be feared and expected that it will end the way all runs end, meaning badly. More explicitly, it would end as what we have previously labelled a _debt jubilee_ , by which we refer to a wave of public (government) and private (bank) defaults. The economic, social and political consequences of such an outcome are obvious enough not to require elaboration. 

**What exactly is a lender of last resort?** Stopping a run, as we have had the opportunity to argue in the past, requires the creation of a credible lender of last resort. Central banks already assume this function for banks, so what is required is that somebody assumes the same function for governments. While the need for a lender of last resort is now widely accepted by the market, its precise meaning is not always well understood. By lender of last resort, we do not refer to an institution that would pay off or assume the debt of an insolvent government by taking a loss itself. We do not refer either to an institution that would monetise or inflate away the runaway debt of an insolvent government. The phrase ‘lender of last resort’ refers specifically to an institution that can ensure that no _solvent_ government will _ever become illiquid_ . In other words, it means an institution that has the financial capacity to fund governments for any amount and any length of time, under the strict conditions that these governments are and remain solvent. Every aspect of this definition is important, as will become clear below. 

**Who can assume this role?** There are in our view only two options: the first is a ‘monetary’ lender of last resort: the ECB; the second is a ‘fiscal’ lender of last resort: the union of governments. 

**Why the ECB alone cannot bring full stabilisation:** At first glance, it appears that the ECB has taken upon itself to assume this role when it initiated purchases of Italian and Spanish government bonds. In theory, the ECB could provide an unlimited funding backstop for governments as there is no technical limit to the amount of purchases it can conduct, providing it limits its purchases to the secondary market (its statutes prohibit it from buying government bonds in the primary market). 

While many investors believe that the ECB can and eventually will provide that unlimited backstop, we continue to think that if this were to take place in isolation, it would not constitute a stabilising solution. To understand this point, one has to go back to our definition of a lender of last resort, which implies that only solvent governments are sheltered without limitation from illiquidity. 

**On the subjective nature of government solvency in the absence of fiscal constraints:** For a private institution, whose assets and liabilities are creatures of contract, it is possible to provide an objective assessment of whether it is solvent. For a government, where the main assets and liabilities are creatures of policy, it is not possible to do so. Mathematically, a government is solvent if its debt does not exceed the net present value of all future primary surpluses. As an assessment of the sign and size of future primary surpluses is necessarily subjective, so is the assessment of solvency. 

From the above, it derives that the only situation in which the central bank or indeed any other observer can assess with genuine confidence that a government is solvent is a situation where there exist _binding_ and _permanent_ constraints on the course of fiscal policy. 

**The limitations to ECB intervention are not technical, they are political:** The problem with the ECB acting as a lender of last resort in this context is that it has no control over the fiscal stance of European countries, and that the constraints that were embedded in the Maastricht Treaty and the Stability and Growth Pact have proved neither binding nor permanent. If the ECB were to provide unlimited funding backstop to governments in the absence of any strengthened control on fiscal policy, it could not therefore credibly dispel the notion that it is funding the potentially runaway debt of potentially insolvent governments. Doing so would lead eventually to backdoor and unsanctioned fiscal transfers through either: i) mutualising sovereign credit risk on the balance sheet of the central bank; or ii) inflating debt away (which would affect different countries asymmetrically). 

Clearly, if the ECB were to engage on that road, the political backlash it would create in the hard money constituency of Europe (that the market often equates to Germany) would only fuel expectations of an eventual euro break-up, a point many times underlined by Joachim Fels. It would therefore, consistent with the argument we presented earlier, further encourage a run on banks almost everywhere in Europe, which would compound problems instead of alleviating them. For this reason, we believe that intervention by the ECB alone cannot be fully financially or economically stabilising, because it would remain politically destabilising. 

**What is a fiscal lender of last resort?** The only alternative option for a lender of last resort is one where any government that has retained unimpaired access to markets would be willing to lend this access to the market to its peers, without limits, under the same condition we insisted on above, i.e., that these peers are solvent.

**It is fiscal federalism:** What we have described here in one sentence has obviously little to do with expanding the size of EFSF, leveraging its action or augmenting it by drawing on the resources of the IMF or any other sources of funds. What we have described in one sentence is fiscal federalism, meaning: 

- Full and permanent control from the union of governments – the federal level – over the fiscal stance of each member state, combined with 

- A permanent mechanism to ensure that all governments that submit to this federal control are fully funded on fair and equal terms. 

**A sequential decision tree for roadmap:** If the argument presented here is correct, then it provides an interpretation grid to assess the conclusions of the ongoing discussions between European governments. This interpretation grid can be thought of as a sequential decision tree, based on the following questions: 

1. Can fiscal federalism defined as above be achieved in a sufficiently simple and practical way (in particular as regards the constitutional changes it requires, both at the federal and national level) as to be a credible outcome at all? 

2. If so, will governments commit Europe clearly enough to this objective by December 9? 

3. If so, will their choice be ratified by their own people, according to whichever ratification procedure is applicable? 

4. If so, how long will the ratification and implementation process take? This, in turn, informs the question of whether there exist transitional support mechanisms that are sufficient to keep governments and banks funded throughout the ratification and implementation process. 

Evidently, a negative answer to _any_ of these questions ought to lead to the conclusion that the run will resume, accelerate and cannot be stopped, with all the consequences this entails. 

**How fiscal federalism can work:** It is also evident that we cannot answer all questions ourselves. Governments will have to answer the second, and it will be up to the people to answer the third. It is clear that uncertainty as regards answers to the third and fourth questions mean that even in the best case where positive answers are provided to the first two, there will not be full market stabilisation post-December 9. We will come back to these third and fourth questions in another note, but focus here on the first. 

It is important to note that, in our definition of fiscal federalism, we purposefully referred to full federal control over the fiscal _stance_ of each member state, as opposed to over the fiscal _policy_ . What matters to ensure government solvency is not who gets taxed and where public expenditure takes place. These questions relate to the _composition_ of the budget, which can remain a national prerogative. What matters is the _size of the deficit_ , hence the trend in the debt level. 

Whether fiscal federalism can be achieved depends therefore in the first instance on whether it is possible to ensure full federal control over the size of the deficit of member states. 

**Genuine fiscal control has to operate ex ante, not ex post:** A first observation here is that this control will in our view never be sufficient if attempted in the form of backwardlooking measures, such as ex post sanctions (automatic or not). Imposing financial penalties on a government after it has run an excessive deficit might incentivise governments not to go down that route, but cannot ensure that they do not do so. 

True fiscal federalism therefore requires effective ex ante control over the size of a government’s deficit. There are two routes whereby this can be achieved. These are the judicial route and the funding (or functionalist) route. We describe both briefly below: 

**The judicial route:** The judicial route implies that national budgets be submitted in some form to the European Court of Justice for validation of their compliance with either the federal constitution (the treaties), or federal law (the content of Stability Programmes agreed by the European Union Council). The benefit of this approach, which we believe to be consistent with German preferences, is that it would exert a genuine constraint on governments. To the extent that federal law supersedes national law, if a national budget were to be invalidated by a federal court, this would deprive governments of the powers that emanate from the budget, most notably the authorisation to borrow. There exist procedures for how governments operate in circumstances where they do not have a legally valid budget, but these are typically restrictive, and may be made more restrictive if necessary to strengthen the efficacy of this judicial approach. 

**The funding route:** The second route whereby national deficits can be controlled is that which we advocated ourselves one year ago, namely the funding route. Under this approach, governments may borrow from a central pool of resources benefiting from joint and several guarantees from all member states, under two strict conditions:

- First, that the amount so borrowed in any one year never exceed an amount compliant with federal law (the sum of redemptions plus the budget deficit – or minus the surplus – implied by full adherence to their Stability Programme); 

- Second, that the liability so incurred be super senior to all government expenditure, including any outstanding form of debt of course, but also all current expenditure (e.g., salaries) and all capital expenditure. There already exists in the legal framework of various countries the concept that some types of government expenditure have absolute precedence over others. 

**‘Federal’ bonds:** This approach assumes explicitly the issuance of bonds jointly and severally by the union of governments. This refers to what the market is accustomed to label ‘eurobonds’, which the European Commission prefers to call ‘stability bonds’ and we called ourselves ‘common bonds’ when we initially advocated their issuance, but for which the correct epithet would actually be ‘federal’ bonds. 

We do not describe here in detail the workings of a federal bond framework (for this, we refer readers to _Sovereign Subjects: Curing Demotion Sickness_ , December 6, 2010), other than to highlight the following points: 

**Controlling the tap that supplies governments with cash:** Federal funding of governments in the way described above allows effective control of the fiscal stance of member states because it allows controlling the tap that supplies them with cash. While deficit numbers, expressed as a percentage of GDP, are an accounting aggregate that can be fudged, cash cannot be fudged. Control of cash funding is therefore the most secure of all constraints on any government’s fiscal stance. 

To the argument that governments may be able to run excessive deficits by borrowing in the market in addition to federal bonds, we see two counter-arguments: 

- First, if federal bonds are senior claims on government resources, it is very unlikely that governments will find creditors willing to lend to them additional monies in the form of what would be explicitly subordinated debt; 

- Second, combining the funding route with the judicial route fully alleviates this concern, as it would make such extra funding unlawful. 

**Why we see concerns over rising borrowing costs as misguided:** To the argument that federal bonds would raise the cost of funding of the most virtuous governments, one can also oppose the following counter-argument: 

If the debt incurred by any government in the form of federal bonds is, as we describe above, consistent by construction with credible solvency, then the yield on these bonds will reflect their risk-free nature, which means that it will be no higher than ought to be the case for any fiscally sound government. The only penalty that Germany would suffer would be the loss of the scarcity premium that currently means that yields on Bunds are well below the risk-free rate – an unsustainable situation, in any case. 

It does not mean that the debt of all governments would suddenly become risk-free. What it means is that the debt of governments would be split into a risk-free component (federal bonds or new debt) and a subordinated component (legacy debt), which would alone carry credit risk. 

**Why equalisation of borrowing costs is justified:** To the argument that the different fiscal situations of different countries ought to be reflected in different costs of borrowing, we can also oppose the following counter-argument: 

If governments tie their own hands by submitting to a form of federal control over their budget that ensures their solvency, it would be bizarre to request that they pay interest on their debt above and beyond the risk-free rate. Countries whose sovereign debt is higher than their peers would already be ‘penalised’ under such a system (in a virtuous manner) by the fact that they are compelled to achieve a higher primary balance than countries with low initial levels of debt. 

**What changes at a minimum are required in the treaties:** We believe therefore that fiscal federalism as we described it above is achievable in a simple, practical and robust manner that meets the interests of all parties. Further, we believe that it would require only ‘minor’ changes to the treaties, namely the following: 

- The removal of the prohibition that member states can issue joint and several liabilities; 

- The affirmation that any money borrowed by a government from or through the European Union and any of its institutions becomes a super senior claim on its resources, senior to all other expenditure; 

- The introduction of a process whereby a national budget can (and in practice will) be submitted to a federal court, such as the European Court of Justice, to validate its compliance with federal law; It may be further specified whether federal law, in that case, refers to static provisions included in the treaties (such as that the deficit cannot exceed 3% of GDP) or to dynamic provisions unanimously agreed by member states (such as the content of the

stability programmes – arguably a better reference as it would allow the imposition of more stringent requirements on countries that start from a high level of debt than on countries that start from a lower level of debt). 

**The critical issue is to balance fiscal control with funding:** Again, we underline that what we have described here is not a forecast as to what governments will decide on December 9. Rather, it should be seen as a _benchmark_ against which to assess whichever decisions will be announced by then. Governments may announce many decisions such as automatic sanctions for governments contravening their constitutional obligations, the possibility for the European Commission to interfere with the national budgetary process of member states, partial pooling of tax revenue, or a multitude of other initiatives. The important criterion, in our view, to determine whether any government plan can stabilise and reverse the run or not is whether it achieves a balance between fiscal control and funding conforming closely to the logic (not the details) of the scheme we detailed above. 

Funding without control would be – as we highlighted above – politically destabilising and is not, in our view, an option. 

**Control without funding is fundamentally unstable...** Symmetrically, the notion that control without funding (fiscal discipline without the prospect of federal bonds) can achieve stabilisation would reflect, in our view, a fundamental misapprehension of the situation in which governments now operate. It would stem from what we see as the misguided perception that if governments commit to fiscal discipline, they will instantly regain access to the market on fair and equitable terms. That is _not_ the case in a situation where government debt has lost its risk-free character. 

If it was necessary to provide evidence for this point at all, the Irish example ought to suffice. Despite being widely praised by its peers for compliance with its adjustment programme, the Irish government has clearly at the moment no access to the market on anything approaching affordable terms: the yield on 5-year Irish government bonds stands as we write at 9.40%, 825bp above the equivalent German yield. The implication is that if governments deliver an effective mechanism for fiscal control without delivering at the same time a credible mechanism for funding those governments on affordable terms, then there is no guarantee whatsoever and very little likelihood that the run will stop, in our view. 

**…or it would require systematic ECB intervention:** Should governments reject the option of achieving funding in the form of federal bonds, then the only other way to guarantee funding on affordable terms would be to fall back on the ECB as a lender of last resort. In this case, control of the fiscal stance would still be achieved in the form of treaty changes constraining governments to fiscal soundness. Affordable funding would be secured by requesting explicitly or implicitly the ECB to clear the secondary market for government bonds (it would likely still be prohibited from purchases in the primary market – indeed, we see no need for change of the ECB’s statutes or mandate) at a necessarily discretionary price. There are many ways in practice to implement this approach. It can be done either directly (à la SMP) or indirectly, by granting ECB counterparty status to a funding vehicle (à la ESM), allowing it to benefit from indirect central bank financing. It is not our purpose to elaborate here on the multiple aspects that such a scheme might take. We only underline that it is feasible, but we think it would clearly be a distant second-best solution to the federal bond model we described earlier. 

**Are governments heading in the right direction?** What we have done here is to answer the first question of our sequential decision tree. Stabilisation is possible. It is now up to governments to answer the second: will they choose this course of action, or something closely resembling it? One can only observe that at the time of writing the full necessity of balancing fiscal control by a funding mechanism does not seem agreed yet by all. We believe, however, that providing the proper sequence is respected (the establishment of a funding mechanism follows from the acceptance by all governments of genuine control over their fiscal stance, as opposed to the other way round), there is time for governments to reach a balanced agreement. 

**Beginning of the end?** Again, we believe that this alone would not be the end of the crisis. Political risk during the transition phase will remain a factor of volatility, to which we will return by addressing the third and fourth questions of our decision tree. We deem it however important to bring to the attention of our clients the key issue (the balance of control and funding) that may leave December 9 remembered as the beginning of the end of the crisis (and a decisive first step towards stabilisation and a European Federation) or the beginning of the end for the European Confederation.
