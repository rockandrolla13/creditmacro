## What Also Comes Before Recessions? Rate Cuts 

MORGAN STANLEY & CO. LLC Matthew Hornbach STRATEGIST Matthew.Hornbach@morganstanley.com +1 212 761-1837 

Focus on the yield curve as a predictor of US recessions is commonplace. While the yield curve has a proven track record, another indicator has recently been as good at preceding US recessions: Fed rate cuts. Ever since it began targeting rates, the Fed has cut them ahead of recessions. 

- On Friday, March 22, 2019, the yield spread between the 3m T-bill and the on-the-run 10y Treasury note inverted for the first time since August 27, 2007. We studied the 3m/10y yield curve before each of the last seven NBER recessions and discovered that it was not actually inverted immediately preceding the last three (beginning in 1990, 2001, and 2007). However, the 3m/10y curve was inverted immediately prior to the first four recessions in our sample (those beginning 1969, 1973, 1980, and 1981). 

- How can we explain these differences in curve inversions ahead of the last three recessions and the first four? The answer lies in what Fed policy was targeting at the time and how the Fed changed its target before each recession. Beginning in October 1982, according to research from the FRB St. Louis, the Fed "switched to a funds rate targeting procedure but never formally announced the change". 

- Since the Fed began targeting the federal funds rate, it has either cut the rate itself ahead of recessions or cut the range it was targeting. On average, the Fed first cut rates 169 business days (about eight months) after the 3m/10y curve first inverted with the target rate at its peak for the cycle. A rate cut in line with this average would arrive in December 2019. 

- While our economists believe that the Fed's next move will be a 25bp hike in December, not a cut, we see room for markets to put a higher probability on a rate cut in 2019 if economic data struggle over the coming months. We continue to suggest notional-neutral UST 2s5s flatteners to get exposure to a good pre-cut flattener and duration at the 5y point. We also continue to suggest being long 5y TIPS on a real yield basis, and long duration in the US and Canada versus Germany and the UK.

## An important curve just inverted 

On Friday, March 22, 2019, the yield spread between the 3m T-bill and the on-the-run 10y Treasury note inverted for the first time since August 27, 2007. The constantmaturity yield spread also inverted at the same time. As we discussed in Full Bull in Effect, economists at the FRB San Francisco concluded that, “The yield curve has been a reliable predictor of recessions, and the best summary measure is the spread between the ten-year and three-month yields.” Exhibit 1 shows that the curve inverted ahead of every NBER recession back to the late 1960s.

We went several steps further with the data and display our findings in Exhibit 2. For our analysis, we used the 3m T-bill versus on-the-run 10y yield spread instead of the constant-maturity series. First, we note that the 3m/10y curve was not inverted in at least the ten business days before the last three recessions began. However, the 3m/10y curve was inverted immediately prior to the first four recessions in our sample. We will explain this fact in a bit. 

For each recession, we counted the number of business days from the first day on which the 3m/10y curve inverted to the start of the month in which the recession began. On average, it was 439 business days (about 1 ¾ years). Then we counted how long the first curve inversion episode lasted. We marked the end of the episode if the curve stopped inverting for at least ten business days. The length of the first curve inversion lasted much longer, in general, for the first four recessions in our analysis than the last three recessions.

Curve inversion episodes occurred more frequently ahead of the last three recessions in our analysis (3-4 episodes before each recession) than in three of the first four recessions. For the last three recessions, curve inversion episodes lasted between 38 and 63 days on average. The magnitude of the curve inversion was also much smaller ahead of the last three recessions than the first four in our analysis.

## The Fed cuts rates before recessions begin now 

How can we explain these differences between curve inversions ahead of the last three recessions and the first four? The answer lies in what Fed policy was targeting at the time and how the Fed changed its target before each recession. Before October 1982, the Fed placed emphasis on M1 money supply to affect monetary conditions. In terms of short-term rates, while the Fed didn't target the discount rate, it moved it around to help to achieve its money supply goals. Our analysis showed that, ahead of the first four recessions in our analysis, the Fed did not lower the discount rate from the prevailing peak at the time. 

However, after October 1982, according to research from the FRB St. Louis, the Fed "switched to a funds rate targeting procedure but never formally announced the change". And before the next recession began in July 1990, the Fed had lowered the range in which it wanted the federal funds rate to trade by 200bp – from a range of 8- 12% to a range of 6-10% – in two steps over a six-month period (see Record of Policy Actions from July 1989 and December 1989). 

Exhibit 3 summarizes Fed action ahead of each of the seven NBER recessions in our analysis. Since the Fed began targeting the federal funds rate, it has either cut the rate itself ahead of recessions (2001, 2007) or cut the range it was targeting ahead of a recession (1990). On average (sample size: three), the Fed first cut rates 169 business days (about eight months) after the 3m/10y curve first inverted with the target rate at its peak for the cycle. A rate cut in line with this average would arrive in December 2019.

While our economists believe that the Fed's next move will be a 25bp hike in December, not a cut, we see room for markets to put a higher probability on a rate cut in 2019 if economic data struggle over the coming months. The idea of a pre-recession rate cut from the Fed would be in line with our framework for this Fed – one that wants to "keep the party going". In addition, downbeat economic data in the coming months would likely help the 3m/10y yield curve stay inverted and possibly invert further. As we see in Exhibit 2, the average episode of 3m/10y inversion lasts 47 business days or just over two months with a median level of inversion of 11bp. 

As the same time, the history of pre-recession curve inversions suggests that, while the recent 3m/10y inversion should be unwelcome, it should not unnerve the Fed just yet. The Fed will probably want to see the 3m/10y curve inversion continue for a period before thinking more seriously about its implications for recession. If the inversion persists until the June FOMC meeting, then the Fed may feel more uncomfortable. If it continues beyond that, the Fed may begin to contemplate actions necessary to keep the party going. 

For now, we continue to suggest notional-neutral UST 2s5s flatteners to get exposure to a good pre-cut flattener and duration at the 5y point. We also continue to suggest being long 5y TIPS on a real yield basis, and long duration in the US and Canada versus Germany and the UK.
