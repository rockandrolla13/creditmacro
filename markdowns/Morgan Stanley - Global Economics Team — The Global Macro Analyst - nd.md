## **Bottom Line** 

Isn’t it ironic? **Just as Japan, thanks to aggressive monetary and fiscal policies, is about to exit deflation at long last, the euro area risks falling into a similar deflationary trap as Japan experienced in the 1990s and 2000s.** Back then, Japan suffered from a prolonged postbubble balance sheet recession, a late and overly cautious monetary policy response, periodic strong currency appreciation, a failure to enforce a rapid clean-up of bank balance sheets, a premature fiscal tightening in 1997 that pushed the economy back into recession and a general institutional reform sclerosis. Sounds familiar? 

Of course, the euro area is not Japan and history doesn’t repeat itself. Yet it rhymes, and given the many parallels, a Japanification of the euro area is a serious risk. **I believe that Japanification can still be avoided if euro area policymakers heed three lessons from Japan:** i) Monetary policy should move early and aggressively _before_ deflation manifests itself; ii) Regulators should enforce a clean-up of bank balance sheets including a realistic assessment of bad assets and a swift recapitalisation where needed; and iii) Governments should avoid overly tight fiscal policies that could plunge economies back into recession **. I think there is a better-than-even chance that these lessons will be heeded and the euro area’s Japanification can be avoided.** But I’m nervous. 

## **The Japan Narrative** 

The story of Japan’s two lost decades has been told many times, so it suffices to recap the key elements briefly. 

**The prelude to the lost decades was the three Bs: a boom that begot a bubble that ended in a bust.** Easy monetary policy and strong growth in the second half of the 1980s fuelled a debt-financed equity and land price bubble, which burst in the early 1990s after the Bank of Japan had tightened policy sharply. 

**What followed can be summarised as the three Ds: Deleveraging, deflation and debt.** The collapse in equity and land prices pushed many firms and households into negative equity and induced them to pay down debt. 

- **Deleveraging** depressed demand, resulting in what Richard Koo has called a **balance sheet recession.** A series of fiscal stimulus packages by the government helped to mitigate the impact of private sector balance sheet contraction on demand, making the recession more shallow and even supporting occasional recoveries. 

- **Deflation** set in only several years after the bubble burst. In my book, it largely resulted from a combination of delayed balance sheet clean-up in the banking sector and a slow response of the Bank of Japan to the balance sheet recession in the first half of the 1990s, which was partly explained by the fear of pumping up yet another bubble. By the time the Bank of Japan finally cut official rates to the zero bound (1998), deflation had already set in so that real interest rates never went negative. 

- Public sector **debt** rose sharply, partly as a consequence of the many fiscal stimulus packages, and partly as a consequence of deflation, which resulted in falling nominal incomes and tax receipts. 

Finally, another important element of the Japan narrative is **the CRIC cycle – the cycle of Crisis, Response,** 

**Improvement and Complacency** – which my colleague Robert Feldman coined and described as early as 2001 to explain Japan’s institutional reform sclerosis. The cycle arises from the interaction of two natural tendencies: i) The economy responds slowly to policy reforms; and ii) Policy-makers tend to slow the reform process as the economy improves. Each economic crisis during the lost decades sparked a policy response, but the following economic and market improvements led to complacency by policy-makers, which in turn paved the way for the next crisis. 

## **How Japanese Is the Euro Area?** 

The Japan narrative is certainly not a blueprint for the euro area as there are some important differences, but there are also many parallels. Here’s my list of the main similarities and differences: 

- Like in Japan, a **cycle of boom, bubble and bust was at the origin of the euro area’s current problems.** A sharp decline in real interest rates and strong growth in the peripheral countries following the introduction of the euro led to debt-financed asset price booms and bubbles, which eventually burst in the late 2000s.

- Yet, much of the booms and bubbles in the periphery was financed by lenders from the core, who quickly withdrew their capital when the bubble burst. Thus, the bursting asset bubble and the resulting banking sector problems turned into an **intra-euro area balance of payments crisis,** which led to doubts about the viability of the single currency. In this sense, the euro area crisis was much worse than the Japanese crisis. 

- In addition, markets soon realised that the **euro area member states were not true sovereigns as they were indebted in a currency they could not print themselves.** Thus, markets refused to fund governments at reasonable interest rates and a sovereign debt crisis resulted. Therefore, **contrary to Japan, the euro area countries most affected by the crisis lost their ability to pursue countercyclical fiscal policies** and instead had to tighten policy into the downturn, thus aggravating the recession. By contrast, Japan was able to mitigate the consequences of the balance sheet recession in the private sector through expansionary fiscal policy. And when the government decided to raise the consumption tax in 1997 after an economic recovery in 1995-96, it soon turned out to be a mistake as it pushed the economy back into recession. 

- **Another important parallel between Japan back then and the euro area today is the slow progress made in cleaning up the bank balance sheets and recapitalising financial institutions.** As a consequence, both Japan and the euro area witnessed (and the latter is still witnessing) a contraction of credit volumes and weak broad money growth. The ECB’s master plan is to achieve the balance sheet clean-up and recapitalisation in the run-up to banking union over the next 12 months or so. If successful, this could be an important catalyst for repairing the credit mechanism. Yet, much work remains to be done, and **in the meantime, bank deleveraging with its potentially deflationary consequences will likely continue.** 

- Until recently, the ECB has been able to prevent deflation and keep inflation expectations anchored around its definition of price stability (below but close to 2%). Arguably, this has been due to the fairly aggressive early response since the onset of the crisis in 2007-08, when the ECB started to engage in various forms of unconventional easing. However, note that deflation only took hold in Japan about eight years into the balance sheet recession – thus it is too early to rejoice, in my view. As I see it, **the risk of deflation in the euro area has increased recently.** Why? First, credit keeps contracting as bank deleveraging continues or even accelerates ahead of next year’s asset quality review and stress test. Second, wages in several crisis countries such as Spain are now starting to fall as a lagged consequence of high unemployment and past labour market reforms. Third, the euro has appreciated further in response to the Fed’s change-of-mind on tapering. Meanwhile, current inflation is already running significantly below target and looks set to drop further this month. **But so far, the ECB has** 

**stubbornly refused to acknowledge deflationary risks, much like the Bank of Japan before the onset of Japan’s deflation.** 

## **Japanification Can Still Be Avoided** 

Taken together, a Japanification of the euro area is a serious risk. **But I believe that Japanification can still be avoided if euro area policy-makers heed three lessons from Japan:** i) Monetary policy should move early and aggressively _before_ deflation manifests itself; ii) Regulators should enforce a clean-up of bank balance sheets including a realistic assessment of bad assets and a swift recapitalisation where needed; and iii) Governments should avoid overly tight fiscal policies that could plunge economies back into recession **. I think there is a better-than-even chance that these lessons will be heeded and the euro area’s Japanification can be avoided.** But I’m nervous.

## **Spotlight: US: Looking Through the Fed’s Window on the World** 

Vincent Reinhart (1 212) 761 5387 Ellen Zentner (1 212) 296 4882 

Decisions taken by the Fed will shape the financial landscape for some time. Will it be taper or taper talk? Is more serial disappointment in store as Fed forecasts are marked lower and lower? Will incoming data touch the Evans thresholds that are the key mechanism of forward rate guidance? 

A good case in point is the announcement after the September FOMC meeting. We learned that “tightening financial conditions, if sustained, could slow the pace of improvement”. Much head-scratching in markets ensued because charts of various financial conditions indices (FCIs) nearly uniformly showed an easing on net over the inter-meeting period. The seeming disconnect between the policy statement and market perception of financial conditions suggests that policy-makers and financial markets look at conditions through different lenses. We believe that the fundamental difference in the way the FOMC sees financial conditions compared with financial markets lies in the treatment of mortgage rates. 

Since taper talk began in early May, the yield for the Freddie Mac 30-year fixed-rate mortgage shot higher by some 120bp by the time policy-makers met to discuss the prospect of trimming longer-term asset purchases, with little sign of abatement. In the press conference that followed, Chairman Bernanke described the decision not to taper as a “precautionary step” because the Committee would like to “see the effects of higher interest rates on the economy, **particularly mortgage rates on housing”.** [emphasis added] 

Macro models of the US economy capture financial conditions differently compared with composite indices. To be sure, both methods generally include measures that capture the effects of interest rates, equities and FX markets on financial conditions. However, the composite indices appear to include either a mortgage spread (30-year mortgage rate less 10-year Treasury yield) or no mortgage measure at all, whereas macro models include the level of mortgage rates. Because long-term interest rates tend to move in tandem, using a spread may inadvertently place less emphasis on the effect of mortgage rates on financial conditions, particularly as they apply to the US housing market. In macro models, the weight given to the level of mortgage rates is significant. 

FOMC chair nominee Janet Yellen is not only model-centric, viewing financial markets through the lens of a macro model, she is also mortgage-centric and risk-averse to boot. **FCIs appear to be underweighting the importance of mortgage rates in general in a Yellen-led FOMC world.** 

**The impact of an increase in mortgage rates:** The FOMC is right to be concerned about the impact on housing activity, and therefore the broader economy, from higher mortgages rates. Housing is an extremely interest rate-sensitive sector and activity is affected with a lag. The housing recovery is also an essential pillar of the health of the overall US recovery. 

Shocking a macro model with a sharp and sustained increase in mortgage rates will result in a downgrade of the outlook (primarily via the GDP component of direct residential investment). One can even imagine this scenario may have played out in the Federal Reserve staff presentation on the economic outlook at the September FOMC meeting. The key word in this exercise is “sustained”, and its inclusion in the policy statement was very deliberate. 

Since the September FOMC meeting, mortgage rates have declined by nearly 30bp to 4.28% in the latest Freddie Mac survey. Regardless, mortgage rates remain roughly 90bp higher compared with pre-taper talk levels (early May) and roughly 90bp higher compared with one year ago. 

Our own model simulation suggests that a sustained 100bp increase in mortgage rates from the very low levels that prevailed through 2Q lowers real GDP growth by an annualised 0.3pp, raises the unemployment rate by roughly 0.2pp and lowers the level of housing starts by about 115,000 four quarters hence. At the September meeting, the FOMC lowered its central tendency projection of real GDP growth in 2014 by 0.25pp. No doubt higher mortgage rates played an important role in the downward revision to growth. 

Policy-makers are well aware of the damage a sustained increase in interest rates can do to the economy, so they acted to be sure the rise in rates was not, indeed, sustained. Nevertheless, in the past few months there has been a softening in housing activity in response to the back-up in rates. 

Higher mortgage rates will provide a headwind to the housing market recovery, but the fundamental tailwinds make it more likely that the housing recovery has several years more to run. For example, single-family home sales would need to double over the next few years to catch up to levels suggested by these fundamentals. 

Perhaps more importantly, the FOMC has shown its hand in regards to financial conditions. The message: mortgage rates matter more than you think, and particularly with Janet Yellen at the helm. 

For full details, see _US Economics: Looking Through the Fed’s Window on the World_ , October 22, 2013.

## **The Morgan Stanley Global Economics View** 

## **Our Core Global Views** 

**Acceleration, stabilisation and accommodation:** Our global narrative for the next 6- 12 months rests on three pillars: An acceleration of growth in DM, stabilisation in EM economies (with downside risks) and continued accommodation by central banks. 

**A deeper divide between EM and DM:** We continue to expect the DM economies to _accelerate_ from twilight to daylight in 2H13 and 2014, taking leadership of the global economic cycle. EM growth should _stabilise_ at a lower level, although we remain concerned about the structural and cyclical fragilities.

## **November 17, 2013** 

**Eurozone not out of the woods yet:** We think that resolution of the euro area sovereign and banking crisis requires both a fiscal union and a banking union coupled with the ECB being willing and able to be the lender of last resort to governments. While the ECB has taken a decisive step towards fulfilling this role, progress on fiscal and banking union remains painfully slow and full of setbacks. 

**Fiscal dominance:** Don’t expect DM central banks to tighten soon – they are locked into a regime of fiscal dominance, where increases in the real interest rate worsen government debt sustainability, inflation targeting becomes unfeasible and monetary policy is forced to remain super-accommodative. 

**Financial repression and inflation:** Part of the solution to high government debt levels can be imposing artificially low, or even negative, real returns on captive investor groups – financial repression. Inflation – allowed by central banks constrained by fiscal dominance into a passive monetary stance – could be part of this solution, too. 

**EM growth model broken – needs structural reform:** EM economies face external and internal challenges that render the old, export-led model of growth defunct. Weak DM consumers, onshoring of DM manufacturing and risks to external funding all work against EMs externally. Internally, the focus on export-led growth has meant that important sources of domestic demand have been neglected. Aggressive policy stimulus will probably make imbalances worse. For potential output growth to rise, policy stimulus needs to go to the ‘right’ sources of domestic demand.

## **Spain: Inching Back to Growth** 

Daniele Antonucci (44 20) 7425 8943 

**Exiting recession…** The national accounts of the first large eurozone country to report 3Q numbers are a further reminder of how fragile and sub-par the recovery is – at least for now. That the Spanish economy started to expand again, with GDP printing a 0.1%Q _gain_ (-1.2%Y), is of course encouraging, and in line with our long-standing call of a cyclical trough, along with the Bank of Spain’s estimate from last week (and also with the consensus, which tends to base its forecast on such estimate). This follows a nine-quarter economic contraction, the longest since records began in 1960. 

**…and starting a slow recovery…** Yet the pick-up in the pace of economic activity is also quite slow by historical standards and compared to other countries, even though, together with Portugal, Spain is so far the only southern European country that’s inching back to growth. We expect Spanish GDP to expand by around 0.1%Q in 4Q too, and see above-consensus growth of close to 1%Y on average in 201415 (next year’s median expectation is 0.5%Y). This is good news, but it’s also only a quarter of the pre-crisis growth pace. 

**…characterised by weak domestic demand:** This is an important point because national consumption and, to a lesser degree, investment are likely to remain Spain’s weak spots for quite some time. This is because, historically, the economy has only started to create jobs when it was growing twice as fast as we project for next year (even though recent labour market reforms might have lowered the threshold, or escape velocity, for employment growth). So, our worry is not that unemployment will go much higher (we think it has peaked). It’s that it will not come down quickly. A process of ‘good deflation’ (CPI was a weaker than expected -0.1%Y) might give household disposable income some respite. 

**How the ‘New Spain’ will look like:** Whether a sub-par recovery turns into a more sustained upswing hangs on where Spain is in the process of deleveraging, rebalancing and reforming (see _The New Spain_ , September 9, 2013). There’s more to do, but we’re more positive than market expectations on Spain’s ability to set itself apart from the rest of the periphery in the medium term. 

**Export-led recovery and structural reforms:** Spain has indentified an engine of growth in its export sector, which seems structurally strong. Exports are growing, also as a share of GDP, as competitiveness is being recouped courtesy of wage moderation – in part due to labour market reforms. However, these medium-term dynamics might be overshadowed, in the near term, by the strength of the euro.

## **UK: Preliminary 3Q GDP Strong as Expected**

**Impact on our views:** At +0.8%Q, the preliminary estimate of 3Q GDP was in line with consensus expectations (although one-tenth below our expectations). **Broad-based sectoral gains:** We won’t get chance to judge if the recovery has become more balanced between consumer spending and investment/net exports until the end of next month. ## **Russia: Where’s the Rebound?** 

Jacob Nell (7 495) 287 2134 Alina Slyusarchuk (44 20) 7677 6869 

**Stagnant Russia:** Following disappointing preliminary 3Q BoP and September IP and investment data, Deputy Economic Minister Klepach recently claimed that 3Q growth continued at the low level of 2Q (1.2%Y), which he described as stagnation, and Prime Minister Medvedev said that 2013 growth would not reach 2%. 

**Little sign of the rebound yet…** We forecast a stronger 2H, with growth running at 2.8%Y, to give 2.2%Y for 2013 as a whole. The data are not yet supportive of the rebound thesis. 

**…but we still think it’s coming:** We see downside risks to our growth numbers (2.2%Y in 2013, 3.1%Y in 2014), from the faster-than-expected deterioration in trade and the slower easing of policy. But we have four main reasons for sticking to the rebound thesis: 

- **Easier policy:** Many have focused on the structural reasons for slower growth – the falling current account surplus, lack of spare capacity and unsustainable wage growth. But the slowdown has also been cyclical. Russia had a political business cycle – and now we see easier policy, with the fading of the post-election fiscal drag and incremental monetary easing. 

- **Robust consumption:** The resilient consumer has not gone away. Real wages, the most reliable measure of income, jumped to over 8%Y in September, and will remain strong through 2014, we think, given the structurally tight labour market. Resilient domestic demand points to a pickup in consumption and a turning in the inventory cycle, in our view. 

- **Investment recovery:** The investment slowdown has been concentrated in state-dominated industries, which had a ‘political investment cycle’, and extractive industries, where we now see O&G capex picking up. 

- **Harvest:** Agricultural output fell in September by 1.4%Y, as a result of poor weather. But with a good harvest, up 22%Y, we expect some payback.

## **India: RBI Reduces Short-Term Rates Again; Negative Real Rates Persist** 

Chetan Ahya (852) 2239 7812 Upasana Chachra (91 22) 6118 2246 

**RBI policy action in line with expectations:** The Reserve Bank of India (RBI), in its quarterly review of monetary policy on October 29, announced the following policy changes: i) Reduced the marginal standing facility (MSF) rate by 25bp to 8.75% from 9% with immediate effect; ii) Increased the policy repo rate under the liquidity adjustment facility (LAF) by 25bp to 7.75% from 7.5%; and iii) Increased the liquidity provided under the seven- and 14-day term repo window to 0.5% of net demand and time liabilities (NDTL) from 0.25% of NDTL earlier (equivalent to approx Rs380 billion/US$6.3 billion). We believe that the timing for cutting short-term rates further via transitioning of effective short market rates to repo rates from the MSF rate (currently) would likely be dependent on continued FX stability.

environment warrants the RBI to hold short-term rates at least at the current levels (if not higher). In our view, a reduction in policy rates without an improvement in the underlying liquidity situation (credit-deposit ratio) will mean that transmission of rate cuts to lower borrowing cost will be less effective. Hence, for a sustainable reduction in short-term rates, we believe that moderation in CPI inflation will be critical.

## **India and Indonesia: Which Is Facing More Challenges?** 

Deyi Tan (65) 6834 6703 Chetan Ahya (852) 2239 7812 Derrick Kam (852) 2239 7826 Zhixiang Su (65) 6834 6739 Upasana Chachra (91 22) 6118 2246 

**Is India worse off than Indonesia?** Much economic comparison has been drawn between India and Indonesia. Many believe that India is the worse of the two. However, we think that the reality is less clear-cut. Each is vulnerable in different ways. Concluding that India is economically worse off may be oversimplifying matters. We explain why by examining the following: 

**1. What is the genesis of the macro problems? Which economy has the bigger issues to deal with, near term and medium term?** India’s macro malaise stems from poor domestic policy choices and a bad growth mix, in our view. Indonesia’s stems from an external reversal of the commodity supercycle. As India’s policy choices have reduced productivity and its current account deficit is more stretched, we think that it has the bigger _near-term_ challenge of reining in loose policies and spending productively within its means. However, Indonesia has the bigger _medium-term_ challenge of developing alternative growth sectors amid poorer commodity terms-of-trade. 

**2. What are the risk factors? How do they affect the macro outcome?** Two global factors are important: i) The pace of rising real rates; and ii) China’s growth story and its collateral impact on commodity prices. A sharp spike in the former has non-linear growth implications for India; a fall in commodity prices asymmetrically benefits India but hurts Indonesia. 

## **3. What policy adjustments are required, and who is** 

**further along the macro rebalancing process?** For the near term, Indonesia needs to see higher real rates and a weaker real exchange rate – we believe that IDR is still overvalued. India also needs higher real rates but INR has already adjusted to fair value, in our view. For the medium term, India needs to commit to sustainable policies, correct price distortions and improve the business climate. Meanwhile, Indonesia needs structural reform 2.0 to improve the competitiveness of non-commodity sectors. 

_–_ For full details, see _Asia Pacific Economics: Asia Insight India and Indonesia: Which is Facing More Challenges?_ October 29, 2013. 

## **Korea: The New Safe Haven** 

Sharon Lam (852) 2848 8927 Jason Liu (852) 2848 6882 

**From crisis-prone to increasingly resilient:** Korea has been in the eye of every economic storm we have seen in Asia over the last two decades. However, 2008 proved to be a watershed year, as regulations were implemented to foster stability in its economy. Whether compared with its own history or with that of other countries in AXJ, Korea now stands out as a safe haven for investors _,_ especially amid global macro uncertainty. 

**Disciplined investment and healthy corporate balance sheet:** Korean corporates have turned more disciplined on capex since the Asian financial crisis in 1997. Lack of overinvestment means that there should not be any overcapacity issues in the economy. 

**No longer externally vulnerable:** Korea is running a recordhigh current account surplus, supported by its strong export competitiveness. It has more than sufficient foreign reserves to cushion it against external liquidity shocks. It has also received the least amount of ‘hot money’ inflows in the region since 2009. 

**No need to worry about asset prices in Korea:** Unlike the rest of the region, Korea is not seeing any risk of a property bubble. It is probably one of the very few economies in AXJ that could even see upside in its property prices in the next three years rather than the possible correction we may expect in other countries. 

**There should be no policy dilemma this time:** We believe that the Bank of Korea will not be forced to hike interest rates this time as it did in 1997 and 2008. Its accommodative stance can remain in place for longer, in our view. Meanwhile, Korea’s fiscal position remains solid, and local government debt is not yet an issue. 

**We are not ignoring the risks:** 1) Household leverage is high, but we think it is overstated – Korean household assets are also much stronger than before, and they did not participate in the QE liquidity party. 2) Korea has seen the largest amount of bond investment inflow in AXJ since 2008, but we are not worried about the risk of massive unwinding, because foreign ownership is still low and we believe that the holders are mainly long-term investors, not speculators.
