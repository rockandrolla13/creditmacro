## Meet in the Middle 

Short duration IG is now rich in outright and relative terms as credit curves have bull steepened this year. We recommend that investors extend out the curve into 7-10Y bonds and reduce notionals. The potential for weaker technicals and rising late-cycle concerns imply poor front-end risk/reward. 

**Credit curves back to steep levels:** Credit curves in the cash market have steepened meaningfully this year as short duration IG has benefited from attractive all-in yields, low net supply, strong inflows, and a market rally that was initially "defensive" in nature. Similarly, curves in the synthetic market are also steep in percentage terms, with front-end flatteners offering positive carry and roll-down. At these levels, we think the front end is no longer attractive. Simply extending out the curve and reducing notionals (i.e., increasing cash) offers better risk/reward, in our view. 

**Short duration technicals may weaken:** We see a few technical headwinds for the front end going forward. With the Fed pausing and rate cuts now in the dialogue, inflows into short duration IG funds could slow down. More importantly, short duration IG now offers almost no yield on a currency-hedged basis for non-US investors, which could pressure foreign flows going forward. Finally, while repatriation was more thematic in 2018, we continue to view lower demand from Corporate Treasuries as a minor long-term headwind for the front end of the market, especially at rich valuation levels. 

**The front-end and the cycle:** Beyond the technical headwinds, the risk of flatter credit curves also rises late in an economic cycle and especially towards the end of a rate hiking cycle. We find that credit curves tend to bear flatten meaningfully from the last rate hike to the first rate cut, as the market brings forward default expectations and refinancing risks rise. 

**Trade recommendations:** In the IG cash market, we recommend that investors switch out of front-end bonds and extend out the curve into 7-10Y bonds. We like pairing low dollar bonds in the long end with short-dated CDS as a convex trade that monetizes steep curves and the cash-synthetic basis and should do well in either tail scenario. We also like 3s-5s CDS flatteners in certain IG single names as a positive carry, convex trade against a sharp widening in spreads. Finally, our top bearish recommendation within credit remains to short the front end of the CDX HY indices (S25 or S27).

## Introduction 

We are switching our investment grade maturity preference from the front end of the market to intermediate maturities (7-10Y), given the steepening in credit curves this year. In other words, while we remain cautious on credit, we think investors are better off moving out the curve into 7-10Y bonds and reducing notionals, rather than remaining overweight the front end. Our view is based on a combination of valuations, where we are in the economic and Fed hiking cycle and how technicals could evolve for the different maturity sectors going forward. We dig deeper into all these factors through the rest of the report. 

## How steep are credit curves today? 

We start by showing performance across the maturity spectrum this year and the steepness of credit curves relative to history. YTD 3-5Y and 5-7Y IG spreads have tightened by 46bp and 50bp, respectively. Shorter maturities in general have outperformed longer tenors, with the 7-10Y and 10Y+ sector spreads tightening by 41bp and 31bp, respectively. In absolute spread terms, the front end (1-5Y) is now within 6bp of its 1Y tights. In contrast, the 7-10Y and 10Y+ sectors are still 15bp+ off their 1Y tights. 

Below we show the shape of credit curves over time. While curves have steepened across the board, vs. history, we find that the steepest part of the credit curve is the 7- 10Y relative to the 3-7Y. The curve steepness between these sectors at 36bp is not far off the most extreme level of 43bp over the past 9 years. The 10s-30s curve (which we approximate using index level buckets) has also steepened this year, but is still a way away from the all-time steepest levels last hit in 2016.

## Why has the front end outperformed this year? 

Next, we dig into the specific drivers of the front-end outperformance so far in 2019. 

**a) Cheap valuations to start the year** 

3 

The front end cheapened substantially in 2018, driven by a combination of macro and technical factors. At the beginning of last year, repatriation-related selling (and subsequently lack of buying) on top of elevated market volatility drove a bearish flattening in the credit curve. On top of that, rising currency-hedging costs meant that the front end periodically saw selling pressure from non-US investors (either outright or to deploy capital at longer maturities). These headwinds, on top of concerns around a Fed policy mistake and weaker US growth in 4Q18, drove front-end (1-5Y) spreads all the way to ~120bp in December. At these levels, valuations had cheapened to the point where short duration IG looked very attractive as a defensive trade both outright and vs. long duration IG, especially once technicals stabilized and volatility normalized. 

On a related note, the outperformance of the front end this year also aligns with the very defensive nature of the rally in credit and equities. While sentiment certainly bounced post the dovish Fed pivot in early January, macro fundamental concerns have remained. As a result, defensive sectors have outperformed in equities and lower-quality credit (i.e., CCCs) has lagged the broader indices. Along the same lines, the front end has served as a low-beta trade for IG credit investors who view default risk as remote in the very near term, but are concerned about risks a few years down the line. In contrast, falling yields and defensive positioning have arguably hurt the long end.

## **b) A flat Treasury curve has driven short duration inflows** 

Rising front-end rates were initially a headwind for short duration IG as “cash” became a competitive investment alternative, when the Fed started hiking nearly once a quarter at the end of 2016. Not surprisingly, flows for short duration IG funds have risen sharply over the past two years.

## **c) Focus on liability management from IG companies** 

A unique technical supporting the front end this year has been increased liabilitymanagement operations at IG companies. ## Why are we turning more cautious on the front end 

## **a) Technicals may become less supportive going forward** 

Thinking about short duration flows going forward, we note three points. First, with the Fed’s dovish pivot this year our economists expect no more rate hikes in 2019. While this isn’t an outright negative for the front end, at the very least we think it could cause inflows into short duration funds to slow down, especially as pockets of value emerge elsewhere. 

Second, and more importantly, in our view, is the fact that non-US investor flows could 

6 

become a headwind with the repricing in bond yields relative to currency-hedging costs. In Exhibit 11, we show yields for 1-3Y IG for Japanese and European investors after accounting for currency-hedging costs. As the Exhibit shows, short duration IG now has a negative yield for EU investors and a very slight positive yield for Japanese investors. As we have flagged in several reports, non-US investors were the marginal buyers of IG credit from 2014-17, and any selling pressure from this investor base can cause bouts of volatility, as we initially saw with the front-end selling just over a year ago in March 2018. If volatility remains subdued, we imagine that non-US investors will continue to prefer the additional yield in the intermediate or long duration parts of the IG market.

Without going into too much detail, the rationale for the above curve dynamics is intuitive. When the Fed is hiking rates, the Treasury curve tends to flatten, and all else equal, this often leads to a steeper credit curve. Once the rate-hiking cycle has ended and default risks start to tick higher, credit spreads widen and credit curves flatten for two key reasons. 1) The market starts to bring default risks forward as growth concerns rise, and 2) At this stage the Treasury curve tends to bull steepen, as the market begins to expect the start of a rate-cutting cycle. Note, while we don’t expect an actual rate cut in our forecast horizon, our interest rates strategists believe the Treasury market will remain priced for one for a period of time (see Global Interest Rate Strategist: Crossing the Rubicon, 2019.04.05).

Second, we think it is important to remember that conservative corporate behavior is good, up until a point. Or said another way, when one company starts paying down debt it is a good thing, but if a large percentage of the market is retrenching by paying down debt, cutting costs, and the like, that is exactly how the credit cycle can negatively feed into the business cycle. And when that happens, as noted above, credit curves tend to flatten. 

Also, as a sidenote, while the LM operations we have seen this year reduce refinancing risk for corporates, which is a good thing at the margin, most have not been significantly deleveraging and, as such, have not resulted in an improved fundamental picture. In fact, we have argued that leverage for the market is going to go up in 2019 because the handful of attempts to pay down debt will likely be offset by negative earnings growth for the market in aggregate. 

## Why do we prefer 7-10Y and not the 10Y+ sector? 

While we have focused much of this report on the front end, it is worth looking into value at longer tenors (i.e., 10Y+ relative to the 7-10Y). One somewhat confusing dynamic this year in the credit market has been the continued steepening in the 10s-30s spread curve considering that the 10s-30s Treasury curve has also steepened (the two curves are often negatively correlated). 

While there are many explanations for this trend, which we will save for another note, the short (and simple) answer is that while the Treasury curve is an important consideration for the long end, rate levels are just as important. For example, the long end in IG saw healthy LDI flows in 2018 as all-in yields hit eight-year highs, and corporate pension funded status also hit a cycle high of 94%, supported by a rally in stocks (prior to 4Q). 

While the 10s-30s credit curve has steepened, 30Y yields are more than 50bp off their highs from late October and corporate pension funded levels are now lower. In addition, while long-end demand was supported by the September 15 tax carve-out for pensions 

9 

last year (which incentivized new contributions), that is no longer the case today. 

In Exhibit 15, we compare the actual 10s-30s curve in IG versus what is implied by the level of rates and the 10s-30s rate curve (using a simple linear regression). Our analysis shows that for the current level of rates/curve shape, the 10s-30s credit curve is fair. Also, our Morgan Stanley interest rates strategy colleagues continue to expect lower long-end Treasury yields by the end of 2019. Accordingly, we don’t view the risk/reward of extending beyond the 10Y point on the curve as overly attractive. However, pockets of long duration IG, such as low dollar-price bonds, do offer good value, a theme we mention in the next section.

## **b) Convexity trades – buy low dollar bonds vs. short-dated CDS** 

Another trade we like to express our view on the curve is to own long-dated low-dollarpriced bonds vs. 5Y CDS. This is essentially a trade that works well in either tail scenario. In a sharp sell-off in credit, the curve could flatten meaningfully and discount $ bonds will outperform, given that they have lower loss in default. In a rallying market, the upside on front-end CDS is capped at these tight absolute levels. In contrast, there is more room for the long end to tighten, given that curves are very steep. The risk to this trade is that credit spreads grind wider slowly, in which case the curve could steepen modestly and the roll-down on short-dated CDS can be meaningful.
