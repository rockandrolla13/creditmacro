## **A Tou h Year For The Asset Class g** 

- Corporate hybrids have faced a number of challenges this year that have made their performance disappointing on both an absolute and relative basis. 

- • Misconceptions related to rates sensitivity and call risks as well as robust supply raised volatility and lowered returns in the first half of the year. 

- Since then, broader macro concerns alongside idiosyncratic company events have provided little relief. Confidence in the asset class was not helped by S&P’s notice at the end of October revising the equity content of certain hybrids (further details on next slide). 

- While recent developments have hurt investor confidence, we do not expect them to prompt a strategic reallocation away from the asset class. In fact, our conversations with investors suggest that some HY funds have increased exposure to hybrids post the recent correction.

## **Still Constructive, but More Selective** 

- Looking through the recent disappointment, we remain hopeful. In terms of relative value, yield differentials to IG bank subs for the iBoxx non-fin subordinated index remain near a record high of 1.4%. Yield buyers can therefore choose from a number of IG-rated hybrids that offer in excess of 4% YTC. 

- An IG asset class yielding more than 4% in the current environment will continue to appeal to a variety of investors, in our view. However, we no longer see hybrids as a directional play and emphasize the need to be selective both from a credit standpoint and in terms of returns convexity in the individual instruments. 

- We also see a number of names (away from Volkswagen), where the hybrid spreads are at record wides versus seniors. For the weaker credits, this is a reflection of deteriorating call economics. But for the more stable names, this presents a case for spread compression.

## **Dealing with S&P’s Surprise** 

- **What happened?** On October 27, 2015, S&P revised the equity content it assigns to some hybrid issues from “intermediate” to “minimal”. In all, 29 hybrid issues from 14 issuers were affected. As a result, these hybrids are now fully treated as debt and lose all equity benefit received from S&P. At the heart of the issue is the “ratings event call” language that is not uniform across issuers, which we discuss in our recent note. 

- **How have issuers responded?** In recent days, some companies have issued undertakings partially waiving their call rights. This option potentially allows them to regain equity benefit at S&P while at the same time avoiding payment of a consent fee to investors. For these names, S&P has now started to reinstate “intermediate” equity content. Others have stated their intention to regain “intermediate” equity content by working with S&P. We indicate below which action, if any, has been carried out so far. 

- **Implications for investors:** Price action in the names where issuers have opted for waiving call rights has been negative. Consent fees would have clearly been a source of some upside for investors. However, given that the call option has always been with the issuer, it is not obvious to us if investors had a strong case to push back on the modifications anyway. In our view, further downside associated with any future waivers should be limited.

## **TERMS & STRUCTURE – SPECIAL EVENT CALLS What Are S ecial Event Calls? p** 

Special Event Calls give issuers the option to redeem a hybrid early, usually when the perp no longer functions as intended at issuance. These include: 

## **Rating Event Call/ Capital Event Call** 

- These give issuers the option to redeem a hybrid early **,** _**if the equity benefit of the instrument allowed by rating agencies deteriorates (subject to conditions)**_ 

- Calls are dependent on equity content, _NOT_ hybrid or issuer rating (at least not directly) 

## **Accounting Event Calls** 

- Accounting Event call gives issuers the option to redeem if a hybrid is no longer classified as “equity” or “financial liability” under IFRS, or the applicable accounting standards. The majority of hybrids with this type of call are redeemable at 101. 

## **Change-of-Control Calls** 

- If not called after a CoC Event, the coupon would step up immediately by 500bp. 

- What investors should be aware of is what constitutes a CoC Event. As the name suggests, a CoC Event generally comprises: 

   - some sort of change in ownership – the exact definition of which would typically be with reference to takeover laws of the issuers’ domestic jurisdiction, _**AND**_ 

   - a ratings downgrade or withdrawal, within a certain window from the CoC, that is the result of the CoC; if a deterioration in rating is unconnected to the CoC, then the CoC call would not be triggered. 

## **Tax Events Calls (Gross Up/ Tax Event)** 

- Gross-Up Event call (or Withholding Tax Event) gives issuers the option to redeem early if they have to pay additional amounts to hybrid holders to make up for any taxes withheld or deducted due to changes in tax legislation. 

- Tax Event call gives issuers the option to call if cost on hybrids are no longer tax-deductible, due to changes in tax legislation/ 

## **Clean-Up Calls** 

- These allow issuers the option to redeem when the amount outstanding of a hybrid drops below a certain level, typically about 10-25% of the amount originally issued.

## **HYBRID CALLS – DRIVERS, FEATURES & CASE STUDIES Which Factors Drive an Issuer’s Decision to Call or Not Call at the First Call Date?** 

The financial ability of a company to retire debt trumps all, but hybrid structure and language can also play significant roles in influencing an issuer’s decision to call or not to call at the first call date. 

**Loss of equity content** 

**What?** Rating agencies treat portion of hybrid as equity. But this benefit may fall away after a period of time, creating incentive for issuers to call. 

**Useful for valuating:** Hybrids issued post-2012. 

**Hybrids more likely to get called if:** Equity benefit falls away at or before first call date. This is the case for nearly all European hybrids issued since 2012. European hybrids could see their equity content intact after the first call date. 

## **Coupon resets and step-ups** 

**What?** If not called at the first call date, the coupon typically resets to a Libor or swaps-linked coupon, usually L + margin where the margin equals the spread at issuance. For nearly a third of the market, specifically hybrids issued from 2010 to 2012, this margin remains elevated compared to where these issuers’ senior unsecured and perps are trading now, i.e., where issuers can likely refinance. 

**Useful for valuating:** All vintages of hybrids. 

**Hybrids more likely to get called if:** Spread at which issuer can refinance is lower than the step-up + spread of hybrid at issuance. 

**Replacement language/ replacement capital covenant (RCC)** 

**What?** Replacement language generally requires issuers to redeem the existing hybrid only with funds from newly raised hybrid or common stock. 

**Useful for valuating:** All hybrids, although style of replacement language varies by vintage. 

**Hybrids more likely to get called if:** Legally enforceable RCC exists, and takes effect after the first call date; _or_ to a lesser extent, replacement language/intention exists, and takes effect after the first call date.

## **HYBRID VALUATIONS How Does Ne ative Convexit Relate to H brids? g y y** 

**Potential early redemption means a hybrid’s upside can be capped, while risk of maturity extension can see duration increase as yields rise** 

- A hybrid’s callability means that some of the upside may be capped – duration basically falls as yield falls to ‘extreme’ lows. 

- On the other hand, as yields rise, the ‘worst’ yield for a hybrid will shift from its YTC to its YTM. Since the latter represents a much longer effective date, it means duration rises as yields rise to ‘extremes’. 

- On the right, both Hybrid A and B trade to call, but Hybrid A’s YTC is considerably lower than its YTM, while Hybrid B trades at a lower price, with YTC much closer to its YTM. 

- This one difference gives rise to the very different convexity profile for the two hybrids. Note the ‘kink’ in the relationship that occurs when yields rise above ~5.75% for both of these securities. Below this level, the bond prices to a shorter-duration call, giving the price/yield relationship a gentle slope. Above this yield level, the bond would price to maturity, with much larger associated duration, and much more to lose with each incremental rise in yields  this increasingly severe downside illustrates negative convexity.

## **HYBRID VALUATIONS** 

## **How Can Investors Measure Ne ative Convexit ? g y** 

## **Upside/Downside Ratio** 

- The ratio of hybrid returns between a 100bp decrease and increase in yield. 

- The higher this ‘upside/downside ratio’, the better the convexity profile. 

- For trading to maturity, the price fall with a 100bp rise in yields would be much more severe than any potential gains from a 100bp fall in its yield; the upside/downside ratio would be less than 100%. 

## **Estimated Change in Duration** 

- For most perps trading to call, the change in duration should be minimal. For perps trading to maturity, however, or those with YTC close to YTW, the new duration could increase dramatically. 

## **YTC ∆ to Non-Call Kink** 

• Estimates the spread increases needed to bring the hybrid to that kink. The lower this spread, the closer a hybrid is to duration extension. 

- Let’s look at the choice that faces an issuer when its hybrid spread increases dramatically. As spread increases up to the ‘kink’, YTC is still lower than YTM, and it makes economic sense to call. As hybrid spreads continue to increase beyond the ‘kink’, YTW is lower than YTC, and it becomes uneconomical for the issuer to call. Hybrid starts trading to the second call, and duration extends.
