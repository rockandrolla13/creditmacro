## Cross-Asset Quant 

## Momentum for Diversification 

Cross-asset trend and momentum strategies are less compelling than carry but can provide offset and diversification with low cost. 

**"Buy high, sell low" did you say?** Momentum-based investing is, in some form, buying assets which have already risen, and selling assets which have already declined. We divide our analysis into two types of strategies: i) 'Trend' (i.e., long all assets with positive 12m return, short all assets with negative 12m return within an asset class); and ii) 'Momentum' (i.e., long/short best vs. worst quartile on the rank of current 12m returns within an asset class). 

**It shouldn't really work, but somehow it does:** Momentum-based strategies run against the grain of fundamental/value-based investing and it is difficult for traditionalists to stomach why they could possibly work. Overall, momentum and trend produced modest returns and respectable, but not exceptional, risk/reward. However, returns for individual asset classes tend to be clustered in certain years, which is a strong argument for using a broad variety of assets to improve consistency of returns. 

**Momentum's real appeal lies in diversification:** Momentum's differentiating characteristic is being able to position for both lower and higher valuation dispersion in different environments, unlike many factors which are inherently positioned for lower dispersion or meanreversion on some dimension (e.g., value). This lets momentum strategies stick with winners for longer and avoid buying too soon in times 

of stress. We think this is why momentum strategies can be a good diversifier, doing better when stocks are down than up, and is an important argument for inclusion of momentum in traditional portfolios. 

**Momentum as a low-cost hedge:** We think it is best to view momentum through the lens of a low-cost portfolio hedge as opposed to (high) income/yield that is modestly diversifying. One would ordinarily expect to pay for the hedging characteristics of such an asset, and positive returns that momentum produces in acting as such a 'hedge' are effectively the icing on the cake. The low level of expected returns in traditional assets makes this a less expensive proposition today. 

**How we view momentum today:** Our macro view of slow growth, easy monetary policy and low returns is not supportive of both momentum and trend as effective factors over the short term. However, expensive valuations in traditional assets can result in big structural repricings, which are inherently difficult to time. We think that momentum-based portfolios have the ability to capture such a structural repricing. The price to be paid in terms of sub-par returns in our base case scenario (low returns, range-bound markets) while we wait for such a repricing may be worth it as the effective cost of such a hedging/diversification tool.

## Executive summary 

## "Buy high, sell low" 

## Diversification at low cost 

Momentum-based strategies involve buying assets which have already risen and selling assets which have already fallen. We create 'cross-section momentum' portfolios ('momentum') by getting long the quartile of assets with the best 12m returns rank against the quartile of assets with the worst 12m returns rank. We create a 'time-series momentum' portfolio ('trend') based on the stand-alone momentum (long positive absolute 12m momentum, short negative absolute 12m momentum) of each asset class and then weight the various longs and shorts by volatility. In both cases, we use 12m return as a measure of momentum and use a one-month holding period before rebalancing again. 

Bonds have served an exceptional dual role in portfolios, providing both income and hedging offset over the last three decades (see The New Diversifiers). Finding alternatives which can do both is tough and most factors fall somewhere on the spectrum of providing either yield or diversification. While cross-asset carry – which we discussed in a previous note (see Why We Like Carry) – primarily falls into the first category (income which is modestly diversifying), momentum in our view ought to fall primarily into the second category (hedging/ diversification which is low-cost or even manages to produce modestly positive returns). 

## How is momentum different? 

## It shouldn't really work 

Momentum-based strategies arguably are the most controversial of the various factor strategies. They run against the grain of fundamental/value-based investing that many investors would categorise their style as. The justifications for momentum and trend to work often involve behavioural reasons which bring into question their predictability and sustainability over long periods of time. Nevertheless, momentum strategies seem to have produced respectable, even if not exceptional, risk/reward over the long run. 

Many factor strategies inherently position for reduced dispersion on some dimension, be it value, carry, yield differentials, etc. For example, by buying the value factor, we are effectively positioning for cheap assets to get less cheap and rich assets to get less rich. Momentum in contrast can position for lower as well as higher valuation dispersion depending on the environment (i.e., it can position for rich assets to become richer and cheap assets to become even cheaper). We think this ability is what distinguishes momentum from other factors and gives it the correlation characteristics that are very valuable in a portfolio context.

## How does momentum perform in different environments? 

An overall cross-asset momentum portfolio comprising an equalweighted portfolio of the individual momentum factors does better when overall stocks are lower than when stocks are higher. Momentum also does better when rates are lower and curves are flattening than when rates are rising and curves are steepening (see **Exhibit 1** ). Cross-asset momentum does fine in both extreme dollar swings, whether USD rallies or declines. 

## Better together 

Returns to momentum strategies for individual asset classes tend to be clustered in specific years, which means that using a broad-based universe of assets/asset classes can improve the consistency of returns. Combining momentum and trend across different asset classes results in a material improvement of Sharpe ratios compared 

to any individual factor on a stand-alone basis. We will explore momentum and trend portfolios' interaction with other factors in future as their differentiated characteristics make them promising diversifiers when used in conjunction with traditional assets as well as factor investments. 

## Portfolio implications 

Our macro view of slow growth, easy monetary policy and low returns is not supportive of both momentum and trend as effective factors. However, expensive valuations and exceptionally low returns in traditional assets mean the threat of big structural repricing will be persistent. The challenge of course is timing such a repricing. Systematically buying momentum and trend is one way of capturing that move. There will of course be a price to be paid – in terms of sub-par returns in our base case scenario characterised by low returns and range-bound markets that may be trendless. But this price may be worth it as the effective cost of such diversification.

## Defining 'momentum' and 'trend' portfolios 

We define cross-section momentum-based portfolios using a simple 12-month look-back and 1-month holding period and then getting long the best momentum quartile against the worst momentum quartile. We also create a time-series momentum-based portfolio, getting long assets with positive 12m returns on a stand-alone basis and short assets with negative 12m returns, with all assets having the same volatility exposure. 

Momentum-based investing is, in some form, buying assets which have already risen, and selling assets which have already declined. The momentum and trend portfolios do this in different ways: 

**The cross-section momentum portfolio ('CS momentum' or 'momentum')** ranks the assets on trailing 12m returns within each asset class and then goes long the best vs. the worst quartile assets, e.g., the FX momentum portfolio today would be long currencies like BRL and IDR which have the highest 12m returns and short currencies with the lowest 12m returns like GBP and MXN irrespective of whether they have positive or negative 12m returns (see **Exhibit 3** ). If all assets have negative 12m returns, we would still have the same number of longs and shorts. The assets are equally weighted within the long and the short quartile. 

We use a different weighting – scaling weights so that each asset has the same volatility contribution – in **the time-series momentum portfolio ('TS momentum' or 'trend') portfolio** . The trend portfolio goes long all assets with positive 12m momentum regardless of the ranking or the momentum relative to other asset classes. Using the same universe, for example, the FX trend portfolio would in effect be long all currencies including INR and KRW and short all currencies including EUR. 

The weighting for all the assets in the trend portfolio is scaled to have the same unit volatility exposure. If BRL is twice as volatile as SEK and INR, the weighting for BRL will be half that for both INR (the weight will be in the same direction as BRL in line with the absolute direction of its momentum) and SEK (but the SEK weight will be the opposite sign of BRL in line with the absolute direction 

of its momentum). The relative difference between momentum and trend portfolio weighting for the FX universe can be seen in **Exhibit 4** . If all currencies have positive momentum, we would be long all currencies, such that the number of asset longs and the level of market exposure may change from month to month. 

In theory, there are different look-back periods to measure the momentum signal, with 12m and 6m returns being most popular, and holding periods from 1m to 3m to measure signal performance can be used. For simplicity and consistency, we have stuck to using 12m momentum even though momentum may work better on different combinations of look-back and holding periods. We will examine this issue of look-back in future work. 

In all the asset classes, we have tried to stick to the same universe as our carry portfolio (especially in FX, equities and commodities) but we have made a change relative to the rates and credit carry portfolios. For rates trend we focus only on the regional dispersion by picking just the 10yr swap rate in key DM currencies, and excluding the 2yr, 5yr and 30yr, in effect removing the curve components which we found to be dominating performance. Similarly in credit, we only consider a smaller list of indices: 5yr and 10yr indices in iTraxx main and CDX IG, and 5yr indices for CDX HY and iTraxx XOver. 

Finally, we take the product of the asset weights generated above and the individual asset carry level, momentum, volatility and valuation level to calculate the portfolio-level metrics for trend and momentum portfolios for each asset class.

## A profile of momentum and trend 

'Cross-asset' portfolios based on trend and momentum offered respectable (low single-digit returns) but not exceptional returns over the last two decades. The diversification characteristics rather than the return potential are the appeal of momentum. 

Momentum-based investing has a long history and a wealth of literature (particularly in single stocks and futures-based strategies), most prominently highlighted in recent years in _Time Series Momentum_ , Moskowitz, Hua Ooi and Pedersen, and _Value and Momentum Everywhere_ , Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen. 

Momentum-based strategies are often viewed with scepticism by long-term investors, as they seem removed from any notions of value. They go against the grain of value-based investing which many investors would categorise their investing style under. There is also the difficulty in explaining why they work, which is often rooted in sentiment and behavioural factors. But there are a number of reasons that could support the performance of momentum strategies: 

- l Different market participants have a different pace of absorbing information and thresholds for acting on it. Markets underreact initially, perhaps as they look for more information and try to catch up after initial scepticism. 

- l Conversely, in times of stress, risk-averse investors overreact to negative price momentum, pushing prices well past what would be considered fair value on longer-term valuation measures. 

- l Risk-management practices of letting the winning trades run and cutting out losses could also support momentum-based portfolios. 

- l Cycle-based arguments can also be made for momentum to work in some asset classes. In rates, central bank policy often follows steady cyclical paths, which would imply that recent direction could be a reasonable predictor for future policy, hence arguing for trend-based strategies to be effective in rates. In stocks, steady earnings cycles can support performance of momentum strategies. 

## Do momentum and trend work? 

Over the last two decades, 'cross-asset momentum', an equally weighted aggregate momentum portfolio of FX, equity, commodities and rates, has produced 5.5% return with 8.6% volatility, in line with equity market risk/reward. 'Cross-asset trend', an equally weighted aggregate trend portfolio of FX, equity, commodities and rates, has produced slightly better risk/reward with about 4.1% return against 5.2% volatility. Momentum and trend risk/reward across asset classes was largely similar (see **Exhibit 7** and **Exhibit 10** ). 

Momentum has had a challenging time since 2009, which was one of the worst years in two decades for both momentum and trend portfolios. As with carry, post-crisis performance of momentum factors has not been as steady year to year relative to the 2000s.

## How momentum and trend perform in different environments 

Momentum's differentiating characteristic is being able to position for both lower and higher dispersion in different environments, unlike many factors which are inherently positioned for lower dispersion on some dimension. We think this helps momentum and trend avoid buying too early in stressed markets, allowing the cheap to get cheaper and the rich to get richer. We think this is why momentumand trend-based portfolios can be good diversifiers, doing better when stocks are down than up. 

**Equity drawdowns:** Both cross-asset momentum and trend portfolios show an ability to do relatively better in equity market drawdowns relative to equity up-markets. Trend portfolios in particular seem to show some amount of convexity, i.e., making positive returns, or at least not losing a lot of money in either extreme. 

**Rates moves:** Momentum and trend both seem to do better in falling rate and curve flattening environments relative to rising yield or curve steepening environments. This is consistent with the theme that momentum and trend can outperform in risk-off environments. 

**Dollar moves:** Momentum and trend seem to take a volatile dollar (DXY) environment in their stride with lower-than-average – but modest – positive returns in both extremes. Trend seems to fare slightly better than momentum. 

**Volatility levels:** Individual factor performance varies but overall momentum and trend seem to do better if initiated when VIX is in the lowest quartile vs. highest quartile. Arguably, if VIX is already very elevated, then we are closer to a momentum reversal which might explain this performance. 

**Growth and inflation:** Most of the individual trend and momentum factors show a strong bias for higher growth environments. FX and commodity momentum do better in high inflation environments but trend shows more mixed performance.

## Momentum, trend and diversification 

All the momentum portfolios show average low/negative correlations to equity portfolios and, as highlighted above, resilient performance in different scenarios. Correlations of the various 

momentum and trend portfolios with each other are positive but not exceptionally high. That said, the correlations vary a lot over time, with periods when correlations are as high as +60% and as low as -60%. 

Returns to momentum strategies for individual asset classes tend to be clustered in specific years. This means using a broad-based universe of assets/asset classes can improve the consistency of returns as different asset classes perform at different points in time. Combining momentum and trend across different asset classes results in a material improvement of Sharpe ratios compared to any individual factor on a stand-alone basis. 

We will explore momentum and trend portfolios' interaction with other factors in future as their differentiated characteristics make them promising diversifiers when used in conjunction with traditional assets as well as factor investments.

## How we view momentum and trend today

We have to acknowledge that this is a tough environment for momentum-based strategies in the short term.

The rates market is an obvious place where positioning for an unwind from extreme valuations has been unfruitful so far. ## FX: Prefer momentum to trend 

FX momentum and trend returns in the long run are better described as modest rather than exceptional. The improved carry and valuation level of the FX momentum portfolio – even as stronger momentum signals are materialising – suggests better prospects relative to recent years. Trend, on the other hand, has less carry support and expensive valuations. 

Over the last 20 years, FX momentum has produced a modest 3.1% return with about 8.7% volatility. FX trend has produced similar riskadjusted returns, 1.9% return with 4.5% volatility. While these returns are roughly only half of what our FX carry portfolio produced, both have produced these returns with lower correlation than carry (see **Exhibit 22** ). Both FX momentum and trend have struggled post-crisis barring a couple of good years recently. 

**Momentum signal:** The level of momentum signal for both portfolios has improved modestly vs. 2011-13, suggesting stronger currency trends now, which arguably improve prospects for momentumbased portfolios (see **Exhibit 25** ). The cyclical EMFX rally is driving positive momentum and idiosyncratic political factors are driving negative momentum (GBP and MXN). 

**Carry support:** Both FX momentum and trend have had limited carry support through history (see **Exhibit 23** ), in contrast to a pure 'FX carry' strategy, which we find from our previous work has the potential to deliver more attractive returns. The current carry level of 5.3% for FX momentum is above average, and the highest level since 2010, making FX momentum more attractive today. Carry support for trend is lower than for FX momentum in absolute terms and below average vs. history. 

**Volatility exposure:** On a related point, both portfolios have higherthan-usual volatility exposure (i.e., longs more volatile than shorts). Through history, the average net vol exposure for both has varied a lot but, on average, it was close to zero. The net long vol exposure for FX trend in particular makes it vulnerable to a sharp USD rebound. 

**Valuation bias:** Finally, FX momentum historically has had an unfavourable relative valuation (i.e., long expensive currencies on a REER basis, and short cheaper currencies on a REER basis). It is encouraging to see that the net valuation gap is much closer to neutral right now, unlike a year ago. 

This is in contrast to FX trend, which historically has a net valuation score that varies (a lot) around zero in different environments but currently faces a significant valuation disadvantage. We think this is being driven by long exposure to some of the expensive AXJ low yielders. 

**Our view:** Overall the higher carry exposure, neutral valuations and stronger currency trends should be supportive for FX momentum portfolios over the coming quarters, despite the higher volatility exposure we are taking on than usual. We are more circumspect on trend portfolios, due to the lack of carry support and valuation disadvantage. 

FX momentum is currently long a mix of EM high yielders, JPY and NZD against a somewhat idiosyncratic mix of GBP and MXN alongside European low yielders. On balance, the long momentum portfolio looks more promising, with a significant overlap with the FX carry portfolio. On the short side, we see more mixed prospects. On our FX strategists' bottom-up 12m forecasts for the various currencies, this momentum portfolio is set to produce a ~3.2% return in a year, in line with the long-term average for FX momentum. 

FX trend is currently long most currencies (barring the few idiosyncratic shorts) against USD and has lower carry support than FX momentum, and a bigger valuation challenge. We see vulnerability to a USD rebound against DM and low-yielding EM currencies.

## Equity: Subdued signals 

Historical returns for equity momentum and trend have been modest like for FX momentum and risk/reward is similar as well. Current subdued momentum, lack of strong carry support and fair valuations make the prospects for equity momentum harder to gauge. Our muted earnings and returns expectations are not supportive of momentum and trend, but an equity rebound, especially in EM equities, can help trend. 

Over the last 20 years, equity momentum has produced low singledigit returns with equity-like volatility and drawdowns (6.1% annual return with 16% volatility), which is not particularly exceptional on a stand-alone basis (see **Exhibit 34** ). Equity trend delivered similar risk/reward – about 5.2% return with 12.6% volatility. However, favourable diversification characteristics justify accepting a lower return to include equity momentum in traditional portfolios. 

**Momentum signal:** The level of equity momentum signal for both momentum and trend portfolios has been subdued since 2011 and has fallen back to the lows after rising in late 2015/early 2016 (see **Exhibit 37** ). Current 12m momentum for both portfolios is well below the long-term average, suggesting a lack of large sustained trends. 

**Carry support:** Equity momentum and trend carry exposure through history has averaged close to zero. which is also where the current carry level lies. Unlike FX momentum which has above-average carry today, equity momentum lacks any carry support (see **Exhibit 35** ). The overlap of equity momentum/trend and equity carry portfolios is minimal, unlike in FX. 

**Valuation bias:** Equity momentum hasn’t had a pronounced valuation bias through history, i.e., the valuation percentile of the long portfolio is largely similar to the valuation percentile of the short portfolio. The current valuation bias is also close to neutral (see 

**Exhibit 38** ). On the other hand, equity trend often has a net long valuation bias which is worse than usual today, i.e., trend is long the equity market at above-average valuations. 

**Volatility exposure:** Similarly, equity momentum doesn’t have a consistent volatility bias but is currently net short the more volatile indices, i.e., equity momentum should do better in a correlated selloff than a correlated equity rally (see **Exhibit 36** ). This is in contrast to equity trend, which has a net long volatility exposure today and is more vulnerable to an equity sell-off. 

**Our view:** Overall, the lack of a strong carry support or valuation bias and subdued momentum does not suggest high expected returns from equity momentum. Our macro outlook for modest earnings growth and low returns also reinforces this view. 

The equity momentum portfolio today seems to be largely marketneutral: Long Germany and UK vs. France and Spain, Brazil vs. India, China and Taiwan vs. Singapore and Japan. We have relatively mixed views on this portfolio. 

Trend is currently chasing bullish equity momentum across key EM markets. While our equity strategists see upside in the US, they are less optimistic on other regions. The continuation of the cyclical rebound in EM equities is a strong upside risk for the equity trend portfolio.

## Commodity: Chasing a rally 

Risk/reward for commodity momentum and trend is largely in line with that for equity and FX momentum portfolios in the long run. Both momentum and trend are net long commodity beta and vol exposure with a net valuation profile that is expensive today even as the level of carry is well below average. However, commodity momentum is improving, suggesting scope for monetisable moves. 

Over the last 20 years, commodity momentum and trend have produced mid-to-high single-digit returns, albeit with much higher volatility than equities. On a volatility-adjusted basis, returns are largely in line with the performance of equity and FX momentum portfolios. 

**Valuation bias:** Both commodity momentum and trend have a significant valuation challenge now and are positioned for the already expensive commodities, which are at the high end of their long-term price ranges, to outperform even further (see **Exhibit 50** ). 

**Momentum signal:** After a period of subdued momentum between 2011-14, the level of 12m commodity momentum has been rising this year towards long-term averages and is at the high end of the 5yr range (see **Exhibit 49** ), suggesting opportunities for monetising potential trends. 

**Carry support:** Commodity momentum has generally benefitted from positive carry, commodity trend less so. However, both have no carry support currently. From a carry perspective, commodity momentum is unfavourable relative to FX momentum. 

**Volatility exposure:** Both commodity momentum and trend have a net positive vol exposure today, i.e., the portfolio is long the riskier assets and effectively net long commodity beta, which can be an advantage in a continued commodity recovery (see **Exhibit 48** ). 

**Our view:** The commodity momentum portfolio is a idiosyncratic mix with longs in zinc vs. copper, sugar and coffee vs. wheat and corn, and lumber vs. hogs and cattle. The commodity trend portfolio is long most commodities, with metals (gold, aluminum and silver dominate the long-side) featuring with higher weights on account of their low volatility. The few commodities with negative momentum are mostly the agri/softs and copper. 

Overall, commodity momentum and trend today are sensitive to a continuation of the commodity recovery and dependent on the strong upside momentum in several commodities sustaining for longer. However, the valuation richness and below-average carry are a challenge.

## Rates: Near-term challenges 

Rates momentum risk/reward is in the same context as momentum in other asset classes, with attractive diversifying characteristics for equity portfolios. However, the rates momentum and trend portfolios are still chasing last year's arguably exceptional bond rally, which can be a headwind in the short term. Longer term, the lack of valuation risk premium means rates momentum can be one way to capture a large repricing higher in yields. 

Rates momentum and trend risk/reward is similarly modest as other asset class portfolios. Returns to trend and momentum have been clustered in some years in contrast to the performance of traditional stock/bond portfolios (see **Exhibit 58** and **Exhibit 64** ). 

**Momentum signal:** The level of rates momentum has been subdued in recent years and below the long-term average, although it has improved modestly. Longer term, the expensive valuations make it likely that we will have sustained monetisable trends, although this is difficult to time. 

**Carry support:** The level of carry support for both rates momentum or trend is relatively modest, although it has improved off the recent lows. We think the carry level is minimal and not a key driver of returns for either factor today. 

**Volatility exposure:** Both rates momentum and trend have net positive volatility exposure, i.e., receiving rates in more volatile instruments. Rates are chasing the exceptional bond rally and hence vulnerable to a continued reversal in rates. As a note, for cross-section 

momentum we have included 2yr, 5yr, 10yr and 30yr maturities across G10 currencies. However, for trend we have used just the 10yr rate across DM rates. 

**Valuation bias:** Rates momentum currently has a long duration and curve flattening bias (long the 30yr sector against the 2yr sector), which can be a concern, given the focus on rising inflation and lack of term premium. The challenge is further exacerbated by the regional exposure – receiving rates in the UK long end driven to historically low yields post-Brexit against paying front-end rates in negative rate currencies which may struggle to move materially. 

**Our view:** Rates trend is net long all bond markets as 12m momentum across all markets remains positive, despite recent reversals. As such, rates trend is vulnerable to a continuation of the rate rise off the post-Brexit lows. Given the sharp rally, we would need a material move in yields for trend to reverse its bias. In the short term this is a key risk but, longer term, we see potential for these portfolios as a way to position for the rate reversal that many expect.

## CDS indices: Not a favourable environment 

CDS momentum and trend risk/reward have been lower than other asset classes as both got whipsawed by the credit crisis. Our macro views don't favour 'CDS momentum and trend' as spreads are likely to remain range-bound and returns dominated by carry and momentum signals. But we think CDS trend can capture a cycle turn. 

CDS momentum and trend portfolio risk/reward has been poorer relative to other asset classes, but this might be a consequence of being whipsawed by a particularly severe credit crisis. We think these portfolios have the potential to capture a credit cycle turn. The drag while we wait for such a turn is unlikely to be severe, given the limited room for spreads to tighten from current levels. 

**Momentum signal:** Today, the level of momentum signal for both portfolios is subdued and the lowest in years, reflecting the rangebound environment we have been in. We expect a continuation of this theme, which implies risk of frequent positioning reversals but low risk of major drawdowns unless the cycle turns and kicks off a new wider spread regime. 

**Carry support:** Carry support for both portfolios have varied a lot around zero. Currently the momentum portfolio has small positive carry while trend currently has a negative carry today, by virtue of buying protection on several of the key indices in the portfolio. 

**Volatility exposure:** Both portfolios have muted volatility exposure currently and are (small) net short the market, positioning them well for a continued market widening. 

**Valuation bias:** Both portfolios have had a net valuation disadvantage through history, i.e., selling protection on richer indices or buying protection on cheaper indices. Currently the net valuation exposure is relatively modest for both portfolios today. 

**Our view:** CDS index momentum is the least convincing of the various momentum and trend portfolios based on recent years' history in terms of risk/reward and correlation characteristics. In the short term our macro view suggests subdued CDS momentum and a good likelihood of limited volatility of momentum and trend portfolios. However, we think that both look more promising into the future and will be able to do a better job of capturing a gradual cycle turn.
