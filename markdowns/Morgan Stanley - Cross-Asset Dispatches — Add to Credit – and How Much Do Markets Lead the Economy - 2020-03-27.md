## Add to Credit – and How Much Do Markets Lead the Economy? 

We raise global credit to OW via adds to US corporate and securitized, and examine how markets bottom in recessions. 

**Our checklist:** We've been concerned by: 1) Rich starting valuations; 2) Insufficient central bank action; 3) How volatility often needs to peak before markets trough; 4) How markets were already showing late-cycle dynamics _before_ recent events; and 5) Uncertainty over the future path of US and European COVID-19 cases. 

**Important progress:** 1) Valuations have improved; 2) Unprecedented central bank action easing USD funding stress; 3) Signs that volatility and negative market technical pressure may have peaked, and 4) The severe US recession our economists forecast will shift our cycle model out of 'downturn' and into earlycycle 'repair', which is historically better for risk assets, especially credit. 

**A key risk remains:** Our colleague Matthew Harrison believes that we could get significantly more clarity on the trajectory of US and European COVID-19 cases over the next week. The probability is rising that the US will start tracking worse than our base case. Incoming economic and earnings data will be bad. 

**How do markets trough in recessions?** 2Q20 may be the worst quarter for growth in US history, but markets usually lead the economy. Using historical lead times, it would be typical for a market bottom to be forming 'now'. This is not an attempt to 'call the bottom', but rather an important factor long-term investors should keep in mind. 

**Strategy implications:** After a modest addition to exposure on March 13, further progress on our checklist leads us to raise exposure more. Along with our credit strategy colleagues upgrading credit, we see better risk/reward in global credit than global equities and raise our overall credit weight from +0 to +5 by adding to US and securitized, funding via reductions to cash and commodities. We favour cash over CDS indices in credit, given skew and basis.

## The checklist – add to credit 

The world faces a public health crisis and a severe economic shock. These are human and economic tragedies on a large-scale, with the worst likely lying ahead. But markets usually _lead_ conditions on the ground. The highest levels of prices occur when conditions are their best; price lows occur when conditions seem at their bleakest. Indeed, this current bear market started well before the full scale of the human and economic toll was apparent. 

If we want to buy low, we must be prepared to act before having complete clarity, before the tide of fund outflows has peaked or the economy has begun to turn a corner. In doing so, there is the natural risk that any optimism is seen as either naïve or callous, given the serious risks to the economy and public health. We're aware of this dilemma. 

When faced with a shock, especially one as volatile and disorienting as this one, we think that investors should focus on three axioms: 

**1. Identify a checklist** of the most pressing problems of the _current_ sell-off, and the progress made in addressing them. 

**2. Identify what past recessions** say about how much markets lead the data, and what they say about shifts to sectors and styles. 

**3. Identify what 'bad' events one is fine moving ahead of:** Price lows will occur long before everything is fine; in 2009, it was important to identify future developments (i.e., higher default rates) that one thought were already in the price. 

This note will cover our thinking on all three. **We think that enough progress has been made to increase exposure further, focusing on credit.** We are not trying to 'call a bottom' and note that further downside is possible. We'd see such downside as an opportunity to raise weights further. 

## Our story so far – the checklist 

This has been a disorienting market of unprecedented volatility. To address it, we've been using a checklist of five challenges: 

**1. Valuations:** Many markets, especially in the US, were unusually expensive going into this sell-off, meaning that prices had to fall further to become historically cheap (see Cross-Asset Dispatches: Be Patient, Look for Asymmetry, February 28, 2020). 

**2. Policy action:** The problems were different than in 2008/09 or 2011/12, and harder to fix with policy action. Rate cuts were unlikely to have much impact (see CrossAsset Brief: A Policy Fix Isn’t Easy, March 4, 2020). 

**3. Volatility and deleveraging:** Realised and implied volatility went _beyond_ levels seen 

2 

in the GFC. Many factors were likely at play here, included deleveraging of systematic and risk parity strategies. Past cycles have suggested that volatility needs to peak before broader markets finally bottom (see Cross-Asset Dispatches: Volatility Peaks Before Markets Trough, March 19, 2020). 

**4. Late-cycle dynamics:** Similar to 2001 or 2007, an unexpected shock has collided with a market already showing late-cycle dynamics. Our cycle models were, and still are, in a 'downturn' phase (see Cross-Asset Dispatches: Was Our Cycle Model Wrong? February 18, 2020). **This was the main reason why our allocations were relatively cautious to start the year.** 

**5. Quantifying COVID-19:** There remains major uncertainty over the path of infections in the world's largest economy, in part due to a slow uptake in testing. In 2007-09, uncertainty around the scale of credit losses caused the market to apply large, ultimately excessive risk premiums. We think that a similar dynamic is at work today. 

As time has passed, progress is being made. On March 13, following an improvement to valuations (#1) and expectations that the Fed would do large-scale QE (#2), we made a modest upward adjustment to exposure.

## How do markets trough in recessions? 

Historically, **equity markets tend to bottom ~4-5 months before the economy troughs,** ending before the recession finishes. 

**Current performance has been unusual compared to that of past recessions:** Stocks have fallen much farther and faster, credit spreads have widened faster and bond yields have declined significantly more. Valuations across asset classes are now reasonably cheap compared to some of the worst valuations in past recessions. 

Typical sequencing between market and economic lows over the last five US recessions suggests a market low, now, would be 'normal'. The yield curve flattens first, then equities reach their lows and credit spreads their wides, before manufacturing PMI and industrial production trough. While there is historical variation, **this would be consistent with an ultimate market low in March/April,** given our economic forecasts. 

In Exhibit 13, we show every US recession (per the NBER) since 1926. Next to it, we show whether it had an associated bear market, and the lead/lag between the starts and ends of economic and market cycles. **Note that, more often than not, the bear market begins** _**before**_ **the economic downturn, often** _**considerably**_ **before. And the equity bear market ends** _**before**_ **the recession finishes (with some exception). With our economists calling for recession,** this sequencing, and the idea that the market usually leads the economy (not the other way around) is all the more relevant.

## Which markets are most priced for recession risk? 

In addition to comparing current performance versus past recessions, current valuations also give us an insight into how different markets are pricing the risk of a recession. We compare current valuations across asset classes with the _worst_ levels of valuations in each of the past recession periods. Given the limited amount of data for certain assets, we focus on the past two recessions (2001 and 2008-09) and recent growth scares, namely in 2011 and 2015. 

In Exhibit 21, we highlight in green periods where current valuations are _cheaper_ than past recession/growth scares, and in red periods where current valuations are _richer_ than past recession/growth scares. The more green numbers you see, the cheaper the current level of valuations are compared to the worst of the past recession/growth scares, and the more assets are priced for recession risk. We note that: 

- **Equities:** While current valuations across global equity markets are cheaper than the worst valuations during the 2001 recession and 2015 growth scares, they are more expensive than valuations during the GFC or the 2011 growth scare. Equity risk premium ('ERP'), on the other hand, is much higher now compared to history, given the level of rates. The S&P 500 and MSCI Europe are exceptions, where current ERPs are lower than during the GFC and 2011 growth scare.

## Timing the lows 

As we attempt to navigate this sell-off, the key question remains how far markets can fall before they begin to recover. While timing this trough may well be a near-impossible task, history provides insight into the typical sequencing between markets and the economic fundamentals during past recessions. 

Having seen moves last experienced during the GFC, we take the last major growth decline as a guide for how markets might respond from here. Exhibit 22 shows the US growth trajectory from 2Q08 against what our economists are forecasting for 4Q19 onwards. 

10 

**Exhibit 22:** This recession – similar drawdown to the GFC, but a faster recovery 

Source: Haver Analytics, Morgan Stanley Research forecasts 

We think that there are broadly three ways of thinking about the sequencing between the trough in risk markets and the trough in growth. For each, we use the growth trajectory from 2Q08 onwards and the risk asset low in 1Q09 as guidance for what that pattern would imply today. 

**1. Duration of the growth decline:** Risk assets reached their low in 1Q09, i.e., three quarters after our 2Q08 reference point. This would imply a trough in 3Q20. 

**2. Magnitude of the growth decline:** In 2009, risk assets troughed when growth was about halfway through its full decline. This would imply a trough in 2Q20. 

**3. Time to growth recovery:** If we assume that markets are forward-looking, it makes sense to expect markets to trough in anticipation of the growth recovery. In 2009, risk troughed nine quarters before GDP recovered to pre-crisis levels. This would imply that the lows in risk markets are already in. 

Exhibit 22 suggests that the expected growth trajectory for 2020 looks more comparable to that of the GFC in terms of magnitude than duration. This renders option 1 less likely, and leaves us thinking that the low for risk is near. 

We look at sequencing between markets and key economic variables, establishing the lead/lag times that have been typical during the past five recessions. We find that: 

- Equities reach their lows and credit their wides ~1 month before US manufacturing PMI troughs, and ~3-4 months before US industrial production YoY troughs. 

- Unemployment tends to trough at the start of the recession, and reach a new high towards the end. Thus, risk asset lows lag the unemployment trough, and lead its peak. 

- The US 2s10s curve tends to be flattest at the start of the recession, leading both other assets and economic metrics in their lows.

## What 'bad' events do we need to move ahead of 

Buying opportunities have an important quality – there need to be factors the market is _deeply_ concerned about (which allow prices to be below fair value) that _you_ believe are manageable and already priced in. All good rallies need a wall of worry to climb. The following are things we expect to get worse, but ultimately be manageable. 

**1. Worsening consumer conditions:** This pressure will put significant strain on the consumer. But again, we think that a long span of history suggests that it is when consumer confidence is high, and unemployment is low, that forward-looking returns are most at risk. Markets consistently trough before unemployment peaks, and we think that the unemployment peak will be very rapid in the current downturn, given the nature of the challenges. 

**2. Higher default rates:** Default rates will rise, as they always do in a recession, and will be amplified by high corporate leverage. But we are strong believers that the time of danger for credit is when spreads and default rates are low; by the time both peak, the worst is generally behind the market, and in 2009, 2016 and 2018, credit spreads peaked well ahead of the peak of defaults. 

**3. Earnings downgrades:** Severe earnings downgrades have started, and more will follow. We think that credit markets have moved ahead of earnings changes, and the rapid declines we've seen recently are consistent with that.
