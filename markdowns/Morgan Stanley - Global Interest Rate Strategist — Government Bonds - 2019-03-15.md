The coming week will witness the most important Fed event since the "Powell pivot" in early January: the March 2019 FOMC meeting. We detailed our expectations for the meeting in FOMC Preview: Lowered Expectations. We concluded:

We think the end to balance sheet normalization will keep downward pressure on term premiums. In order for term premiums to trend higher, we think the Fed would have to start cutting rates, and we don't see this happening in the near term. As a result, we like owning US duration outright and on a cross-market basis against Europe. 

In what follows, we take a step back from the details of the upcoming meeting to see the forest from the trees. We stress that it's not our place to make a call on what the Fed will do in the future. ## A framework for this Fed: It's my party and I'll cut if I want to 

What mantra defines the behavior of the current FOMC? Let's put aside the Fed's wellknown dual mandate (which is actually a triple mandate: maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates). **What is the current FOMC really about? Keeping the party going.** What does that mean? Chairman Powell explained it plainly at the January 2019 FOMC press conference: 

_My colleagues and I have one overarching goal: to sustain the economic expansion with a strong job market and stable prices, for the benefit of the American people._ 

Even before that, Chairman Powell made the Fed's intentions clear. In his conversation with Dallas Fed President Kaplan on November 14, 2018, he said: 

_So the best thing we can do it to try to sustain this expansion for as long as possible, and that’s really what our policy is designed to do._ 

3 

In our view, **the desire of the current FOMC to keep the party going as long as possible is different than Committee of the noughties (2000s)** , and possibly more similar to the FOMC of the 1990s. In the noughties, the FOMC turned down the music (by hiking rates) with the intent of bringing the party to an end before the authorities arrived. However, once the music was turned off, the silence led to boredom and the party ended (recession). In response, and to brighten the mood, the Fed endeavored to host another party (by cutting rates). In short, the FOMC of the noughties waited for recession before cutting rates, while the FOMC of the 1990s tweaked policy to avoid recession (see Exhibit 1).

The current FOMC, however, has no desire to end the party (see a recession hit). And it knows that the party can end in 1 of 2 ways: the volume of the music is too high (rates are too low) and the authorities end it (financial instability leads to recession), or the volume is too low (rates are too high) and the party ends because people are bored and leave (restrictive policy leads to recession). Let's review what the FOMC has done over the past few years and translate it into the party analogy: 

In December 2015, the FOMC raised rates for the first time in over a decade, even though core PCE inflation was at 1.3% Y/Y. It began to turn down the volume of the music before attendees got too rowdy and the authorities showed up. 

By March 2016, guests of the party started departing, and the FOMC decided to hold off raising rates - it refrained from turning down the volume further. 

In December 2016, after seeing that most guests had stayed and some had even returned, the FOMC continued raising rates gradually, i.e., it turned down the music volume further, but very gently so as to keep the attendance up. 

By January 2019, the FOMC realized that more guests had left the party and that the crowd was thinning. If more guests left, the party would likely wind down. So, the FOMC decided to stop turning down the music volume (hiking rates) in hopes that guests would stop leaving and previous guests would return. 

4 

## What does the framework imply for the Treasury market? 

In practical terms today, the Fed is waiting, patiently, to see if current guests stay and previous guests return (growth to rebound back above potential, the unemployment rate to fall further, and inflation to move above target for a period). 

In practical terms tomorrow, if current party guests start leaving and previous guests don't return, then the FOMC may turn up the music volume (cut rates) even though the party is still going (the economy is not yet in recession). By turning up the music volume, the Fed will (1) encourage people to return to the party and (2) prevent people already at the party from getting bored and leaving. 

Simply put, the hint of rate cuts we see priced into 2020 - displayed in Exhibit 2 - are very likely to stay unless real GDP growth rebounds to a rate above the economy's potential and inflation starts moving higher again. In addition, if the March 2019 dot-plot comes on the more dovish side of our economists' expectations, we think an even larger degree of rate cuts will get priced in (see Exhibit 3).

## The fiscal impulse faded fast 

One element of our economist's below-consensus forecast for US real GDP growth in 2019 is the fading fiscal impulse. They thought that fiscal policy added 0.8pp to real GDP growth in 2018 and was set to add 0.2pp in 2019. That downshift is supposed to subtract 0.6pp from real GDP growth in 2019 relative to 2018. One popular measure of fiscal policy on the real economy comes from the Brookings Hutchins Center: the Fiscal Impact Measure (FIM). 

5 

The FIM suggests that the fiscal impulse started to fade in late 2018. The impact of federal spending only added 0.1pp to real GDP growth in 4Q18, down from its contribution in the 3Q of 0.23pp and in the 2Q of 0.24pp. The largest contributor to growth from fiscal policy has come from taxes and benefit programs for consumers which added 0.18pp in 4Q18. 

On the consumer tax front, we continue to monitor the uninspiring tax refund season. The latest IRS release showed $160.874bn of tax refunds paid through March 8, 2019. That is down 3.2% from last year at $166.184bn. The lower level of refunds is in line with 3.3% fewer number of returns processed. The average refund in 2019 at $3,008 was very similar to the average refund in 2018 at $3,004. Our economists also track stats from the Treasury Department suggesting that, through March 14, refunds are running 5% below the equivalent day in 2018. For consumers that were hoping for a higher tax refund this year, disappointment must be setting in.

## Talking TLTRO...it's all about Italy and Spain 

At the March Governing Council meeting, the ECB announced the initial framework for a new round of TLTROs. Specifically, “a new series of quarterly targeted longer-term refinancing operations (TLTRO-III), will be launched, starting in September 2019 and ending March 2021, each with a maturity of two years…at a rate indexed to the interest rate on the main refinancing operations over the life of each operation.” Further details on the precise terms would be communicated at a later date. 

6 

Importantly, the lending terms and shorter maturity are broadly viewed as being less favorable to discourage too much additional usage; however, we anticipate that the ECB is maintaining some degree of optionality, by controlling the timing of the announcement and details of the precise terms. For the ECB, this may ensure the program can be more powerful in the event that further incentivized lending to the real economy is needed, particularly if Europe starts to miss on growth estimates. 

## **Italy and Spain** 

Mechanically, the funding rounds in TLTRO-III will overlap with the maturity of large allotments from TLTRO-II. However, the first three TLTRO-III operations in September 2019, December 2019 and March 2020 will all occur prior to the significant TLTRO-II.1 maturity occurring in June 2020. 

We largely anticipate that TLTRO-III will act as a stopgap for Italian and Spanish banks, allowing them to avoid a major maturity waterfall, while simultaneously easing market concerns that both banking sectors would need to become sellers of BTPs and Bonos, respectively, absent any renewal of funding. 

As shown in Exhibit 5 and Exhibit 6, Italian and Spanish banks have historically been the largest users of TLTROs, both in terms of absolute euros and when viewed as a % of total banking sector assets. Currently, total outstanding from TLTRO-II between the two countries is north of 400bn euros (243 Italy and 167 Spain), representing more than 57% of the total outstanding from TLTRO-II in aggregate.

## Which TLTROs will most likely be used the most? 

Initial estimates of interest and allotment size at TLTRO-III.1 in September 2019 will evolve in line with macroeconomic conditions in Europe, the ability of the banking sector to issue successfully into the credit markets (as has been the case so far in 2019) and, perhaps most importantly, as the ECB lays out the borrowing terms at upcoming Governing Council meetings. 

That said, we note in Exhibit 9, that TLTRO-II.1 and TLTRO-II.4 will represent the two largest maturities, in June 2020 and March 2021, respectively. While it’s possible that some banks may prefer to gradually space out any anticipated rollover prior to the larger maturities given the quarterly nature of the upcoming TLTRO-III and/or theoretically be in a situation to roll that exposure into 2023, we expect that the largest take-ups for TLTRO III will most likely occur coincident with the largest maturities of TLTRO-II. 

Still, there is some risk that market participants assign a greater probability of a larger take-up in September 2019 due to concerns about Net Stable Funding Ratios (NSFRs). However, we again highlight that our banking sector teams have noted that, even though funds with less than 1 year to maturity lose 50% of their Available Stable Funding, HQLA posted as collateral to facilitate TLTRO drawings also lose 50% of their Required Stable Funding value when they are encumbered for less than one year. This should act as a natural offset, lessening some concerns about TLTRO-II.1 falling below 1 year in June 2019.

## Have periphery spreads reacted historically around allotment dates? 

While the sample size is small and there will be other factors that have influenced spreads during this period, we found limited evidence in both the 10y BTP/Bund spread and the 10y Bono/Bund spread that certain consistent price behaviors existed into and out of the 30-day windows prior to allotment announcements. In most instances, outright levels of 2y yields in Italy, Spain and Portugal were marginally lower in the 30day window following the allotment; however, we found no evidence that this same price behavior extended in any of the three countries further out the curve. 

As previously noted, other market factors will have also weighed on spreads and yields during this period, but it’s possible that most of the price behavior happened during the signaling prior to the announcement date, like in February 2016, in anticipation of a market impact rather than when the actual allotments occurred. ## What will happen to the Eurosystem balance sheet? 

If our view is correct that less favorable terms will lead to less take-up of TLTRO-III and therefore less rollover of TLTRO-II, then it’s likely that the total Eurosystem balance sheet will begin to shrink after rising briefly following the first three TLTRO-III allotments. 

Currently, as shown in Exhibit 13, LTRO and TLTROs represent about 15.5% of the total Eurosystem balance sheet while the PSPP and legacy SMP holdings represent more than 45%. As the TLTROs wind off, we would expect that the total balance sheet will begin to shrink, all else equal, but only gradually. For EGBs, we anticipate that this will only matter to the extent banking sector issuance accelerates coincident with both a reduction in TLTRO usage and a ramp-up in front of expected bail-in regulations during the coming years.

## EGB and spread implications going forward 

In our view, the announcement of an additional TLTRO-III means that banks in Italy and Spain will have a needed backstop to ensure they are not hit by a significant maturity waterfall. We believe this should give markets comfort that banking systems in both countries will not need to involuntarily sell government bonds to repay the TLTROs in the near term, particularly at a time that there are already increased concerns about net and gross supply in some countries now that the PSPP net purchases have concluded. 

We believe that markets believe the ECB will be looking to disincentive stronger banks that do not need the funding from continuing to participate. However, we do not anticipate material spread widening as a result, given we find little evidence around allotment times of meaningful market moves. Additionally, the announcement of the new TLTRO’s has already passed. 

If anything, less participation at TLTROs may be viewed as a positive by the markets, particularly if noteworthy reductions are observed in Italy and Spain, given that markets may see this as a signal about the health of the broader banking sector and its views about its own liquidity situation. 

That said, a significant sweetening of the terms relative to market expectations that could lead to incredibly favorable funding similar to TLTRO-II.4 may see greater take-up by banks, but we do not see how this would be a compelling story for front-end carry trades given that first, the sweeteners are tied to real economy lending ,and second, the Refi rate, at 0%, is already above most 2y government bond yields outside of Italy, which is just below 0.20%.

## What could cause much lower than anticipated take-up?

## **Stigma of Usage** 

Banks banks across Europe may believe that any participation in future TLTROs will be punished by markets, given the view that it is primarily weaker banks in the periphery that are the heaviest participants. Across a range of countries, this may result in a greater desire to avoid the program even though it may unequivocally still be the cheapest source of funding for banks in aggregate. 

Indeed, there has been evidence in both the United States, via the discount window, and the United Kingdom, post Northern Rock, of "stigma" in the past. The US Federal Reserve has even written on the topic, publishing a December 2017 note titled, “Stigma and the discount window”, where it concluded that, over time, banks were willing to obtain more expensive sources of funding to avoid being associated with the public perception (rightly or wrongly) that they were participating in any type of “bailout”. We wonder whether one of the risks to the upcoming TLTROs is that banks would rather eat potentially higher funding costs to no longer be associated with its usage.

New issues in the credit market for European banks appear to be going well in aggregate so far in 2019, consistent with the theme we’ve written about for EGB syndications to date. ## **Powell's speech and the message for breakevens** 

We finally heard from Fed Chair Powell on the current debate about whether the Fed should be looking to change its inflation targeting framework. On March 8, Powell's speech laid out the issues due to which the Fed did not adopt such policies in the last crisis. We note that those issues exist today as well: 

**1. The "time consistency" problem:** Powell mentioned the time consistency problem as a reason why adopting an average inflation targeting framework may be difficult. The time consistency problem arises from the fact that if the Fed misses its inflation target from below, then it may be unpopular to communicate to the public that the Fed is seeking higher inflation. 

_**Powell speech, March 8, 2019:** Part of the problem is that when the time comes to deliver the inflationary stimulus, that policy is likely to be unpopular--what is known as the time consistency problem in economics._

## The Hype 

SOFR is a pun-friendly acronym, with "So-Far" and "Sofa" being the most common wordplays; their overuse in reports has truly been inSOFRable. ## **In this piece we discuss why, under our base case expectation, the Fed will neither announce the introduction of a repo facility nor a switch to targeting SOFR any time soon.**

## **Now we move on to defending the importance of Fed Funds market:** 

(3) Could the repo facility be necessary if the Fed switches to targeting SOFR? 

No, for some of the reasons discussed above relating to the fed funds rate. 

But more importantly, we do not expect the Fed will ever switch to targeting SOFR for these reasons: 

**1.** SOFR in increasingly becoming a **hedge fund borrowing rate** given the introduction of the sponsored repo model. We will discuss this in more detail in a future publication, but to get an idea what counterparties underlie the Bilateral Cleared Repo Volume in Exhibit 35, you can consider the list of 1700+ Sponsored Members and Netting Members on the DTCC's website. 

34 

**2.** Contrary to common wisdom, **fed funds is an actual bank borrowing rate.** Fed funds have had a reputation as being simply a market where foreign banks borrow cash to park it at the Fed. However, this arbitrage trade has actually not been active for years and the fed funds market is used by banks (even if not all types of banks) to borrow overnight and fund some of their assets. The fact that FHLBs (as opposed to other banks) are the primary lenders in that market is sometimes seen as a negative. But this is simply a reality of bank funding post-crisis. Banks don't borrow from one another as much as they used to and their primary source of borrowing is unsecured borrowing from FHLBs and Prime Money Market Funds (the latter being more relevant for Foreign Banking Organizations). In fact, the survey summary in Exhibit 38 suggests that short-term unsecured borrowing is a more likely borrowing channel for banks than secured repo borrowing (compare green and grey lines in the Exhibit). 

**3.** We think the FOMC would be reluctant to adopt SOFR as a target rate given its relatively **elevated volatility.** The dislike of volatility in the benchmark rate was actually one reason the Fed ended up adopting a floor rather than a corridor system for managing EFFR as this excerpt from the December FOMC minutes (our emphasis): 

However, reducing reserves to a point very close to the level at which the reserve demand curve begins to **slope upward could lead to a significant increase in the volatility in short-term interest rates and require frequent sizable open market operations or new ceiling facilities to maintain effective interest rate control** . These considerations suggested that it might be appropriate to instead provide a buffer of reserves sufficient to ensure that the Federal Reserve operates consistently on the flat portion of the reserve demand curve so as to promote the efficient and effective implementation of monetary policy. 

We notice again in this excerpt that the repo ceiling facility seems to have been only considered if we were to move to a corridor system, but as discussed earlier, the Fed already confirmed it is adopting a floor system. 

The importance of avoiding a volatile benchmark was also highlighted in the November 2016 FOMC meeting, when the idea of changing the target rate was first raised (our emphasis): 

…Policymakers noted the merits of relying on a policy rate that would be robust to shifts in financial market structure, practices, and regulations as well as to changes in premiums for credit risk. **Other important considerations for the choice of policy rate included the volatility of the rate, the breadth of the set of Federal Reserve counterparties that would be required to ensure adequate control of short-term interest rates,** and the role of the policy rate in FOMC communications.

## Why GC repo rate surged recently? 

GC (general collateral) repo rates have finally started showing signs of settling down over the past few days, with short-end JGBs also beginning to be bought up as a result (see Exhibit 39). Three-month TDB yields fell markedly once again from late January through early February despite a tight level of the 3m USD/JPY basis (consistent with 

36 

the new calendar year having got under way) that would have made 3m TDBs much less attractive to foreign investors (via an erosion of the so-called "hedge premium") (seeExhibit 40,Exhibit 41), and MoF International Transactions in Securities data also point to foreigners having been purchasing fewer Japan short-term debt securities over that period (see Exhibit 42). 

That leaves city banks as the most likely source of demand for TDBs. One possibility is that, with TDBs issued from that time set to mature beyond this year's unusually long Golden Week vacation period (when Japanese markets will be closed through May 6), banks looked to meet their collateral needs as early as possible. This may have left banks with some bonds to lend out in the GC repo market in the interim, thereby putting a certain amount of upward pressure on GC repo rates. 

It is also possible that – with foreign demand having dropped away (due to TDB yields remaining low despite a tightening of the USD/JPY basis) and city banks also now comfortably positioned – dealers have ended up adding to their inventories at subsequent auctions. TDB auction results were indeed generally on the weak side from February into early March, suggesting that investor demand may have been limited. It therefore seems possible that dealers also started to lend out TDBs in the GC repo market. SC (special collateral) repo rates cheapened across the board over the same period, suggesting that SC repo transactions may also have been viewed as a means of eking out higher yields. 

The T/N (tomorrow/next day) GC repo rate climbed towards 0% at one point, but the BoJ then responded by supplying both two-week and T/N funding at Funds-Supplying Operations against Pooled Collateral and hiking its TDB purchase amount to ¥500 billion. 

Moreover, foreigners appeared to start buying once again as TDB yields improved to their highest levels since September 2018, thereby giving dealers an opportunity to reduce their inventory levels. Repo rates could face further downward pressure in the near term as the next BoJ reserve maintenance period gets under way and demand for funds starts to die down once again.

## G4 Smarter (beta) Trading Strategy 

Enhancements to a G4 10y government bond futures momentum strategy have produced higher Sharpe ratios and stronger returns, relative to total return government bond indices for the G4, US, Germany, Japan, and the UK since 2000. ## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts" 

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25 business day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25 business day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25 business day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, do nothing. In short, an investor ONLY trades long signals initiated by the SMAX given their historical precedent to outperform
