## Executive summary 

## **We've just seen a tough 12m for Europe** 

In early 2018, the euro area was enjoying c.2.5% GDP growth, Bund yields were at 70bp, MSCI Europe's N12M PE was close to 15x, and we had seen persistent inflows into EU equities and credit. A year later, GDP growth is c.1%, Bund yields are 5bp, equities have materially de-rated and both equity and credit investors have seen persistent, large outflows. 

## **Euro area macro newsflow to get better from here** 

Despite the recent growth downgrade from the ECB, we think the macro newsflow for Europe is about to get better: the idiosyncratic drags on growth in 2H18 are now fading; a rebound in China should feed in to Europe; wage growth is accelerating; and fiscal stimulus is picking up. In addition, if Brexit uncertainty abates, this could provide a tailwind. We think the euro area composite PMI troughed in January. 

## **European equities looking better – macro upturn/China upturn/better Brexit newsflow** 

Despite the negative backdrop cited above, MSCI Europe has not underperformed MSCI World over the last 6m, reflecting the fact that investor sentiment, positioning and relative valuations were already low. History suggests the risk-reward profile for MSCI Europe improves materially after a PMI trough, while the region also correlates positively to China equities and should benefit from any forthcoming reduction in Brexit uncertainty. 

## **Buying China & UK exposure, 'cyclical cyclicals' and upping Real Estate to overweight** 

Within Europe we continue to recommend buying China exposure, UK exposure and 'cyclical cyclicals' (where we think valuations are much too low). Real Estate is geared to a better EU macro backdrop and has badly lagged the rally in bonds – we upgrade to Overweight. 

## **Neutral Banks for now but looking for an entry point** 

As we can see from last week's market reaction, ECB policy appears likely to remain a significant headwind to the performance of Bank equity for some time to come. However, it's not all doom and gloom, and most other factors we look at are more encouraging: relative valuations are very low, the sector tends to outperform post PMI troughs, our bond strategists see higher yields over time and any drop in Brexit uncertainty should help the wider European sector too. European Banks are also positively correlated to China newsflow and have correlated more closely with China bond yields than European yields over the last 5Y. 

## **Fixed income strategy** 

With investor sentiment and positioning already low, we think that EUR is nearing a bottom and any upturn in European macro data should allow it to appreciate over time. We use the 1.12 level as a buying opportunity in EURUSD, targeting 1.18 by the summer. We reiterate our underweight view on European government bonds relative to North America bonds while also laying out a potentially positive scenario for BTPs. Within EUR credit, we add China exposure via Autos and Industrials and are neutral Financials after their rally.

## We've just seen a tough 12 months for Europe 

The last year has not been kind to European macro watchers or investors. It's hard to believe that just over 12m ago, Eurozone GDP growth was running north of 2.5%, MSCI Europe traded on a N12M PE of close to 15x, and European equity and credit fund managers were enjoying a persistent run of inflows. As is all too common in Europe in this cycle, such periods of hope have usually been followed by disappointment and this has again been true this time. In the next section, we argue that there are grounds for optimism looking forward, but first we quantify the extent of the disappointment of the last 12 months. 

## **Economics** 

• By Feb-18, the Eurozone economic surprise index had enjoyed its longest ever run of positive readings; since then, the same index has been negative for 51 of the last 52 weeks, which only previously occurred in the year to Apr-09. More depressingly, the Eurozone has had the lowest surprise index reading across all the major regions for 47 weeks in the last year - a level far above the previous high of 33 weeks seen in 2009. 

• In Jan-18, the Eurozone composite PMI reached 58.8, its highest absolute level since 2006 and close to its highest ever level versus the equivalent global index. In Jan-19, the Eurozone index fell close to 50 and significantly below its global peer.

This shock was compounded by a number of structural features of the monetary union, e.g., its higher export openness than its peer group, and an inherent fragility in its institutional set-up. With growth already slowing gently, as the policy stimulus was diminishing and 

capacity constraints in the core of the region were emerging, a number of idiosyncratic factors, such as the cutbacks in German car production, compounded the slowdown. 

**Export orders next to trough:** So, as these shocks clear and the impact of the transitory factors peters out, growth should stabilise and then bounce back. To be clear, the _level_ of the soft data we track remains pretty low. But their _rate_ of change, or in some cases the change of the change (the momentum), is beginning to improve. What's more, _euro area_ export orders, unlike China's, are still falling. But, with some leading indicators possibly picking up already, we think they're close to starting to rise too. 

**The European consumer is and has remained resilient throughout:** Job creation is 

ongoing, albeit more slowly. Wage growth is accelerating while inflation is slowing. The consumer, the backbone of the euro area economy, is well underpinned, with the forward-looking components of the consumer confidence indicator still pointing to high levels for the metrics measuring households' perception of their future financial situation and their willingness to make major purchases.

## What we like in Europe 

Our highest conviction recommendations in Europe this year so far have been and remain: 

• **Buy China exposure** - European companies with high China exposure should benefit from our positive house view on the country; however, they currently trade at relative valuations close to 10Y lows. For more details, see pages 32-33 of this earlier report. 

• **Buy UK exposure** - Domestically focused stocks are attractively valued and should benefit from any reduction in Brexit-related uncertainty. As we discussed here, UK Banks, Real Estate, Insurers and Airlines are likely to be most sensitive to Brexit-related newsflow. 

• **Buy 'Cyclical Cyclicals'** - at the headline index level the relative valuation of European cyclicals versus defensives is in line with its long-run average after the former's outperformance year-to-date. However, we think opportunities exist within the cyclical space where the performance and valuation spread between high beta cyclicals and low beta cyclicals is extended. We upgraded Autos and Transport to overweight on 1st February as part of this view - see How big an earnings downgrade do markets price in? 

## **Upgrading Real Estate to overweight** 

If we start to see a recovery in the European macro backdrop going forward, this would open the door to increasing our exposure to the region too. In Exhibit 44 we rank sectors by their revenue exposure to Europe, and it highlights the perennial issue that most of the sectors at the top of this list are not especially cyclical. Banks is the main exception, but this is arguably something of a special case and we consider this below. On this theme, we are upgrading Real Estate to overweight, given: 1) it has the highest exposure to the region of any sector; 2) a lower for longer rate/yield profile should be supportive for sector sentiment; 3) valuations look attractive as per Exhibit 46.

Another common element of mature currency trends are extremes in opinion.

**...and using EUR as a funding currency:** Ultimately, central banks face a challenge when stimulus is running near its limits. If nominal yields are already low and the curve is already flat, a marginal dovish tilt is less likely to have as much of an FX-negative impact compared to scenarios when the curve is steep and yields are high. As noted above, attractive hedging costs have meant that foreign investors holding EURissued securities have increased their hedges. Meanwhile, EUR funding outflows have increased in recent years. Exhibit 82 shows that the EMU gross financial liability position has failed to increase since 2014 as foreign investors' liquidation of fixed income balanced out growth in equity flows (in turn a function of the valuation impact of the bull market in global equities). It is no surprise that we see net outflows picking up markedly after the ECB turned more dovish (Exhibit 87).

**A rebound in European data...** Recent economic weakness has clearly caught European policy-makers by surprise, as illustrated by the noticeably dovish response at the most recent ECB meeting. However, with monetary accommodation becoming less and less impactful at the margin, the focus in Europe may increasingly switch to fiscal policy. Germany's budget surplus of 1.9% of GDP offers some flexibility to boost investment into infrastructure and other projects. Public sector pay has increased by 3.2%Y, reaching the highest level in more than a decade. 

**...would catch the market off guard:** Export-driven economies tend to have weak currencies – Japan being a classic example. Domestic demand strength is what drives the currency higher by increasing demand for imports and also increasingly consuming domestic production at home, rather than shipping it abroad. Last year's USD rally is a good example here – while US GDP growth was strong, the US trade deficit widened out considerably as imports and consumption were both strong. 

**Domestic demand matters:** Europe may surprise investors this year as European households have strong balance sheets, providing scope to increase leverage. Any indication that Europe is nearing this inflection point wherein domestic demand picks up would be a EUR-positive signal. Importantly, it seems that the market is unprepared for such an event, given positioning and past fund flows. 

**A rising Chinese tide…** Investors should also not forget about the USD and the RMB side of the equation. Ultimately, RMB strength may help EUR. A rebound in the Chinese economy is supportive of global demand, and thus also supportive of European exports. It is no surprise that 2018 has seen a slowdown in global trade volumes alongside a slowdown in Chinese economic data. China needs to import capital to grow as its high degree of domestic leverage limits further expansion. Capital imports in turn generate RMB demand, pushing RMB higher and also likely USD lower. The rebound in RMB and Chinese equities renders EURUSD less exposed to downside risks.
