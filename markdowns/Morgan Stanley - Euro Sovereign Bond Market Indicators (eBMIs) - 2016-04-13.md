## **Introducing our euro sovereign BMIs** 

## **Overview** 

Our suite of tactical **bond market indicators** ('BMIs') grows larger. Following the release of our G4 BMIs in March 2015, we extended the framework to the **ACGB, NZGB and CAN** government bond markets, in what was essentially a like-for-like implementation of the original models. Now, we turn our attention to euro sovereign spreads, i.e., the credit spread component of bonds issued by the sovereignties of France, Italy, and Spain. In this note, we introduce long/short tactical indicators for these spreads at the 10-year maturity point. 

We constructed our euro sovereign BMIs ('eBMIs') to form a view on 'blocks' of spreads, i.e., long/short periphery vs. core, semi-core vs. core, and periphery vs. semi-core. While the construction, factors, and systematic nature of the models resemble those of our original BMIs, the eBMIs are different in several respects. With the eBMIs, we are looking to predict returns from credit spreads rather than the return from trading 'riskfree' government bonds vs. cash, i.e., duration returns. Credit spread returns are different to duration in two respects: 

1. Credit spreads benefit (tighten) due to an improvement in economic conditions and risk sentiment, which is opposite to the dynamic when trading government bonds outright (i.e., trading duration risk). 

2. Credit returns are less normally distributed than duration returns. Credit returns exhibit short periods of extreme volatility followed by long periods with less volatility. This affects how we construct our indicators, e.g., we look at ways to avoid extreme losses during 'blow-ups'. It also affects our confidence in our back-tests, which can be dominated by one or two stress events. 

To construct the eBMIs, we included factors similar to those in our original BMIs, but the rationale for including them often differs. We included the following factors: 

- **Bond market volatility-adjusted carry:** The key reason investors by lower rated issuers is for yield/carry pick-up. The attraction in doing so depends both on the yield pick-up and the volatility of the pick-up, which we look to capture through the recent realized spread volatility. 

- **Spread momentum:** We find evidence that recent spread performance is useful for predicting (short-term) future spread performance. We hypothesize that this is not only due to the momentum seen in most financial prices, but also because of the feedback loop between credit risk and funding conditions, i.e., an issuer's default risk increases if it is being forced to issue debt at higher yields and faces an increased probability of being denied market access, and vice versa. 

- **Government bond supply:** Several studies suggest a V-shaped return pattern around euro sovereign supply events, with the market pricing in a concession ahead of issuance and higher returns afterward. Our eBMIs suggest taking advantage of this pattern by overweighting sovereigns that have recently completed their issuance. 

- **Risky asset performance (equities and credit):** We find that the relative performance of risky assets is useful for predicting future credit spread changes. We postulate that this is because these markets reflect more rapidly the shifts in domestic growth and credit conditions, which impact the sovereign's relative credit risk. Note that, in contrast to our original BMIs, where positive risk asset performance is 'bad news' for government bonds, it is supportive for sovereign spread tightening.

- **Business cycle surprises:** Surprisingly strong growth data help to reduce sovereign credit risk and help to tighten credit spreads, and vice versa. Once again, in our original BMIs, we find upside growth surprises predict lower returns from the duration component of government bonds. But upside surprises are positive for the credit/spread component. 

Following any of these factors on their own would have helped to generate positive excess returns trading euro sovereign spreads over the last eight years. However, following the combination of factors would have done significantly better. This is because of an information portfolio effect, i.e., one can predict future spread returns with higher conviction if all the indicators are pointing in the same direction. Following the aggregate signal historically would have generated higher and more consistently positive returns than relying on any single indicator. 

## **The thinking and the fabric** 

The eBMIs give us an output in the form of a spread 'block' recommendation. They would suggest going long/short (1) the periphery vs. the semi-core, e.g., Spain/Italy vs. France/Belgium, (2) the periphery vs. core, e.g., Germany, and/or (3) semi-core vs. core. Our reason for applying such a framework is based on the fact that common factors, rather than country-specific ones, tend to account for the lion’s share of the variance in sovereign spreads (see **Exhibit 1** and **Exhibit 2** ).

We believe this stems from the fact that economies and financial markets in the eurozone are tightly linked. Specifically, the outlook for larger individual sovereigns tends to affect others, especially in times of stress. For example, increasing default/redenomination risks typically cause all peripheral spreads to widen in tandem. In contrast to corporate credit markets, sovereign credit risk within the currency union is usually systemic by nature and tends to affect all other sovereigns to varying degrees. 

We approximate these 'blocks' of spreads by modeling our framework on Italian and French spreads, representing the periphery and semi-core, respectively. This is partly because, in developing our eBMI framework, we have found it exceedingly difficult to model country-specific spreads such as Italy vs. Spain in a systematically successful way.

It also makes sense to focus on these sovereigns as liquidity in the euro sovereign bond markets is increasingly concentrated in the Bund, OAT, and BTP futures contracts. These contracts have become bellwethers for smaller, similarly rated sovereigns, and the futures contracts are increasingly the preferred instruments for hedging activities and tactical risk-taking. 

However, even if one were to construct a signal that would successfully predict such intra-peripheral or semicore spreads, e.g., Italy/Spain or Belgium/France, the variance in these spreads has been low over time, meaning that absolute returns would naturally be low. Of course, one could scale positioning accordingly, but this would by necessity involve the use of large notionals in markets that are, in some cases, entirely cash-based and illiquid to varying degrees. 

Our methodology also benefits from greater availability of data than country-specific models. In particular, the series we use for our business cycle surprise factor can be drawn from a larger pool of indicators, as we are not bound to only French or Italian data series. Instead, for example, we use Spanish as well as Italian economic series to constitute our peripheral business cycle surprise index. For all other factors, i.e., the market data-based ones, we use data that are specific to Italy and France. 

## **The output** 

A further benefit of molding our output into periphery/semi-core/core blocks is the breadth of possible implementation. For benchmarked euro government bond investors, the eBMIs can be used as an input for broad overweight/underweight decisions. While the eBMIs do not provide guidance on how to weight intraperipheral or semi-core names, forming a view on these blocks has accounted for a very high share of the variance in all sovereign spreads historically. At the other end of the spectrum, the eBMI output can be used by investors who prefer to express short-term long/short views on the peripheral and semi-core blocks of spreads through futures contracts. 

We intend to complement the eBMI output with more qualitative assessments of what specific countries to implement the signals in. Our judgments will be based on relative developments in the political, regulatory, and institutional spheres, as well as security-specific relative value analysis. Naturally, we view some of these issues that affect sovereign spreads as too specific, 'qualitative', and time-varying to incorporate in a framework like ours. Among these, recent developments in single-name Portuguese banks, Spanish politics, and relatively more severe illiquidity in certain euro government bond markets serve as examples.

## **Aggregating the signals** 

The output of the eBMIs resembles the original BMIs in many ways. We calculate scores for individual factors, then aggregate them into an overall score using a simple average of the individual factors. Scaling positioning with signal strength allows us to form views between all 'blocks' within the model. We construct signals for all individual factors for periphery vs. core and semi-core vs. core, and let the spread between the two determine our view on periphery vs. semi-core in order to achieve consistency between our three 'blocks'. Had we instead constructed individual factors for periphery vs. semi-core, the overall signal generated by these individual factors might have been inconsistent with positioning in the periphery vs. core and semi-core vs. core. 

Using the spread between the periphery vs. core and semi-core vs. core models achieves consistency with regards to the three different positions generated by the models. However, the set-up means that the spread between the periphery vs. core and semi-core vs. core factors are not bounded within the -10 to 10 range. Furthermore, the spread between the individual factors within the periphery vs. core and semi-core vs. core models will tend to be significantly more volatile than the spread between the two models' overall signals, which is ultimately what will decide positioning in periphery vs. semi-core spreads. 

In order to make our output as intuitive as possible, we therefore divide the spread between the individual factors in the periphery vs. core and semi-core vs. core by 2, which also safeguards the -10 to 10 range. However, for the overall signal, where breaking out of the -10 to 10 range is merely a theoretical issue (it hasn't been close to doing so over our full back-test), we use the simple spread between the periphery vs. core and semi-core vs. core models. This achieves comparability between the overall signals' magnitudes. 

## **Back-tests** 

Similar to our duration BMIs, we initiate hypothetical trades on a weekly basis, with signals reset every Thursday at close of day. In back-testing the eBMIs, we enter spread trades duration-neutral, with a maximum long of 1 notional of Bunds (and maximum short of -1 notional). 

We back-tested the framework over a period spanning 2008 to today, using 7-10y total return baskets. Total returns are converted into excess returns by subtracting Germany’s return from Italy and France respectively (after adjusting for duration). Whether one starts in 1999 or 2008 has very small implications for the back-test, given the low variance of sovereign spreads pre-2008. So what caused the relatively poor performance over the past year or so? Looking at the individual factors in 2015 and 2016, the economic surprise and supply signals performed worse than in previous years. In part, some of this may reflect a disconnect between economic indicators and financial markets, which could have been caused by the ECB’s QE programme. However, we think it is more likely related to the choppy and trendless behaviour of sovereign spreads during the period in question, an environment that typically sees tactical models underperform. Also, in this sense, our eBMIs resemble the duration BMIs, i.e., the model tends to thrive in a strongly trending environment.

Indeed, we have observed significant volatility within a trending environment, which can lead to large week-to-week losses. ## **What are the alternatives?** 

There are many other ways one could use these or similar factors and signals to form views. For instance, One could rank countries using specific factors, e.g., carry is highest in Spain and lowest in Germany; hence, go long Spain/Germany. Another option would be to construct signals based on absolute yields and let the spread between this signal and our original BMIs guide spread positioning. 

For the former, data-related issues in constructing models beyond France and Italy would apply, and the model output would not necessarily be consistent with our duration BMI output. The latter would allow us to back-test the strategy using a longer history and, if successful, show that the models can adapt to different spread regimes, i.e., pre- and post-crisis. However, we have found this approach very difficult to implement, given the dynamism it requires in ex ante assessing to what extent yields move as a result of moves in risk-free rates visà-vis credit.

However, the return/risk distribution of carry strategy varies greatly depending on the underlying asset, with credit being one of the riskier strategies for the reward offered. In this context, we reiterate the importance of distinguishing between term premium and credit spread-driven carry trades within the government bond space. 

Credit spread carry trades are different from term premium carry trades as the former face potentially large and sharp drawdowns as a result of increases in perceived default risk and default itself. Term premium carry trades are vulnerable to risks around the central bank policy cycle, but this tends to happen more gradually. As a result, the return distribution of term premium carry trades is more normally distributed, while credit investors face 'fat tail risk'. 

While protecting against downside risk in either case is important, it is more essential in the case of credit carry trades where the drawdowns can be large and devastating. This applies even more so to sovereign credit than corporate credit, given that sovereign default risk tends to be systemic and is less easily minimized through portfolio diversification. 

The simplest technique for reducing the risk of carry strategies is to moderate the risk one takes when volatility increases. In **Exhibit 10** , we have adjusted the level of carry with spread volatility as a means of 'protecting' against significant drawdowns. In other words, when the market is relatively more volatile, we decrease conviction and vice versa. However, this adjustment alone is not enough to mitigate potential losses. 

In **Exhibit 11** , we show the results of our carry strategy in Greek government bonds. The results highlight the fact that if stress becomes too severe and ends in default, drawdowns become so large that the carry strategy never recovers. Alas, we have not been able to find a metric that is forward-looking enough to insulate the carry strategy from losses. Instead, we rely on the benefit of diversification provided by the pooling of signals, which is highlighted by the performance of the overall strategy.

## **Signal construction** 

We approximate the level of carry in sovereign spreads using 10y credit spreads, i.e., the relevant sovereign’s 10y yield less the German 10y yield. We divide this spread by the 18-week rolling volatility of the spread itself, and normalize using an expanding z-score. Compared to our duration BMIs, where the volatility look-back is one year, the eBMI look-back is significantly shorter. We wanted to make the eBMI more adaptive to changing spread dynamics. 

Another difference compared to our duration BMIs is that the eBMIs use only one point on the curve as a proxy for carry, as opposed to the BMIs, where we use the 2s10s yield curve. The dynamics of credit curves mean that using curve slope is difficult, given its level-dependent behaviour. When credit spreads widen out aggressively, they tend to bear-flatten/bull-steepen; whereas they bear-steepen/bull-flatten in more benign environments. We show the results of back-testing the carry strategy in **Exhibit 12** and **Exhibit 13** .

## **Signal construction** 

We find that offsetting excess returns alone is not enough to capture the short-term mean-reversion of spreads. For example, using 12-month spread returns offset by one-month yields unimpressive results. Furthermore, we find that mean reversion occurs in very short timespans, i.e., on a weekly basis. Therefore, we construct the signal as an average of (1) nine-month spread excess returns and (2) sign-flipped 1-week spread excess returns. Sign-flipped means if spreads have tightened over the past week, they tend to move in the opposite direction the week after. A strong signal is therefore generated if (1) there is a clear trend over the past nine months and 

11 

**Global Interest Rate Strategy** | April 13, 2016 MORGAN STANLEY RESEARCH 

(2) the spread has moved in the opposite direction of that trend over the past week. 

As is the case in our duration BMIs, we do not z-score this variable to allow for potential excess return drift over time. Instead, we scale the variable in order for its terminal volatility to be equivalent to that of the other signals. This means that it is as important as the other four signals in the overall average. 

The construct of this variable too is a deviation from the duration BMIs, where we use four-month exponentially weighted vol-adjusted excess returns. We find that applying this signal to sovereigns spreads (1) fails to capture the longer-term trend and (2) doesn’t account for the short-term reversion behaviour of spreads with respect to trend. 

Needless to say, the somewhat arbitrary look-back periods applied introduce risks of data mining. On the other hand, the relatively weak theoretical arguments for the existence of momentum and exactly at what horizon this plays out means there is no definitive way of constructing momentum signals.

## **Sovereign bond supply** 

## **Trading half the inverted V** 

Academic research (see for example **Price Effects of Sovereign Debt Auctions in the Euro-zone: the role of the crisis** , Beetsma et al., 2013) and our own analysis of auction cycles suggest that there is persistency in the price action around supply events, as yields exhibit an inverted V shape around auctions (see **European Interest Strategist - Goldilocks Escapes the Bears** , January 10, 2014). The theoretical case for the existence of these patterns is often cited as dealers charging a premium for warehousing the additional risk stemming from new supply. 

Regardless of potential drivers, there are quite substantial timing issues with implementing supply strategies. Any systematic strategy before/after supply would have to address a number of questions. Do bonds cheapen on the back of supply announcements or closer to the actual supply event? How predictable is the size, timing, and impact of supply, and does it change over time? 

We argue for a conservative approach to avoid some of these issues. We focus on the second leg of the inverted V, i.e., going long out of auctions. Doing so circumvents some of the previously discussed timing issues, especially from a time-series perspective. The empirical argument to include a supply variable is relatively strong, with all three block pairs showing stronger performance on the back of large supply and vice versa (see **Exhibit 18** ).

## **Risky asset performance** 

## **Cross-asset correlations** 

Our duration BMIs incorporate risky assets as a guide for future term premia expansion/compression. We assume that positive local equity market performance is associated with positive wealth effects and less riskaversion. By assuming this, our duration BMIs postulate a negative equity-government bond return correlation. 

With sovereign spreads, we argue that the opposite is true, i.e., a positive local equity market should be associated with credit spread compression and vice versa. This is because tightening credit spreads and positive equity returns are associated with higher growth expectations and lower credit risks in the country more generally, which should reduce sovereign risk. As such, we would expect the CAC outperforming the DAX to coincide with OAT/Bund compression, for example. 

14 

**Global Interest Rate Strategy** | April 13, 2016 MORGAN STANLEY RESEARCH 

In addition to equities, we incorporate information from credit spreads to form our view on sovereign spreads in this signal. Euro corporate credit spreads tend to correlate with peripheral sovereign spreads relatively well over time (see **Exhibit 21** ). Furthermore, the two are often traded against each other, while both are sometimes used as a 'valuation' anchor in each market respectively. Our preferred way of exploiting this correlation is weighing local equity market performance against how sovereign spreads have performed versus credit in the short term. 

One of the reasons why we think equity and credit markets have provided useful leading information for sovereign credit spreads in recent years is the contingent liability the financial sector poses to the sovereign. In several cases like in Spain and Ireland, problems in the banking sector, which first became evident in bank equity and credit prices, became the main driver of the sovereign debt market. Government bond investors who were focused on these markets became aware of a deterioration in the fiscal position before it became evident in the more traditional sovereign fundamental indicators.

## **Flipping the sign**
