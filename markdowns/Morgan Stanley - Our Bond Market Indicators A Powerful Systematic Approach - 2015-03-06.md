## **Bond Market Indicators** 

## Introducing Our Bond Market Indicators 

## **What is a Bond Market Indicator?** 

We introduce the Morgan Stanley Bond Market Indicators for the US, German, UK and Japanese interest rate markets. Our indicators are used for predicting if excess bond returns over cash are more likely to be positive or negative in the short term. In brief, are yields more likely to rise or fall? In our case, the short term is the following 1-2 months, with the average holding period for recommended trades being 6-10 weeks. Our indicators are simple, systematic tactical models. They represent a simple way of taking a set of information from various sources and distilling it into a single message and recommendation. In particular, we include the following factors in our indicators: 

- Bond market volatility-adjusted carry 

- Bond market momentum 

- Equity market performance 

- Business cycle surprises 

We chose these variables because we find, in most government bond markets and over long periods, excess bond returns over cash are higher when: 

- The yield curve is steep and carry is unusually high 

- Prior bond market returns have been positive 

- Prior equity market returns have been low, negative 

- Business activity has surprised to the downside. 

We calculate scores for these individual factors and then aggregate them together into a combined score. Rather than tying the information together in an econometric model or a qualitatively based weighting system, as some have done previously, we present a simplified framework in which we equally weight the individual factors. We do this to keep the Indicators simple and transparent, and because we are not convinced that any weighting system one would have come up with _a priori_ would have done better than an equal-weighting system[1] . 

Each of these factors by itself may have only limited ability to predict future bond returns, but we found that when combined they had significantly greater predictive power in our testing. This would fit with the idea that bond market returns are driven by multiple factors, so if you can construct variables that proxy several of these factors, you end up with a far more useful model than if it were based on just one or two of the factors. 

Astute observers will note that we did not include a valuation factor – i.e., are bonds rich or cheap versus some notion of fair value? We tested several conventional measures of valuation – e.g., term premiums, fair value factor model residuals – but did not find that valuation predicted future excess bond returns very well. We discuss later in the section other factors we chose not to include. 

**Exhibit 1** shows the current indicator output for 10-year benchmark government bonds. The same methodology could be applied to other rates products like interest rate swaps and other maturities on the yield curve. The indicators output range on a scale from negative 10 to positive 10. A negative 10 indicates the largest short (underweight) duration position and a positive 10 indicates the largest long (overweight) duration position. A “0.0” indicates a neutral (equal-weight) position. 

**Currently, the Morgan Stanley Bond Market Indicator suggests investors should be neutral duration across the four major sovereign markets.** As shown in **Exhibit 2** , the indicators have had a long-duration bias over the past three years across G4 rates, and were until recently long USTs and UKTs in significant size before turning neutral. However, as described on page 7, neutral signals are important for enhancing long-term performance in times of low conviction.

In the sections that follow, we describe in detail how we constructed the individual factors and our justification for choosing them. Just as importantly, we needed a way to combine the factors’ output to create a single signal for investors to use in helping them decide how to position their portfolios. The **combined signal** is a simple average of each factor’s output for each sovereign market. We average volatilityadjusted carry, momentum, equity market performance, and business cycle surprises. While we could have combined each factor in a more complex way, we decided to keep our indicators straightforward, which helps us (1) explain the model to our readers, and (2) avoid the pitfalls of data mining/overfitting. We made two adjustments in our calculation of the combined signal: 

- When the combined signal is close to zero – i.e., shows little conviction – we make it zero. 

- We cap the individual signals that we use to calculate the average to avoid one signal overwhelming the others (see page 7 for more details). 

We found both these adjustments improved the indicators’ performance, the first because the success rate is lower when the indicator has low conviction; the second (we hypothesize) because achieving a ‘balanced portfolio of information’ turns out to be very important for the strategy to perform consistently through different regimes. 

We use the combined signal for each sovereign market as an input into the decision about how to position cross-market – i.e., whether to be long 10-year Treasuries versus 10-year Bunds. But being long Treasuries against Bunds does not imply that investors should be overweight Treasury duration necessarily. What happens if Bund yields rise by more than Treasury yields rise? Being long Treasury duration outright may have cost the investor dearly. The duration call necessitates a third adjustment to capture the impact of cross-market correlations: 

- We impose a global restriction for each sovereign’s **overall signal** such that it can only be long (or short) duration if, at least, one other major market has the same combined signal (otherwise, the overall signal is neutral, or 0.0). 

Once again, this constraint improves the backtest results we get from the model, suggesting **the conviction investors have in their domestic duration call should be influenced by their views on other G4 rates markets** . We find that most of the performance enhancement emanating from the cross-market restriction is attributable to the closing out of signals when conviction is low – i.e., simply magnifying signals due to crossmarket alignment does not do the job. 

## **The Power of Bond Market Indicators** 

Each of the four factors we use in our indicators shows reasonable short-term predictive power on its own and, hence, could be its own strategy. However, the power of the indicator approach is that, by combining the information each factor conveys into a single strategy, we get a more skillful strategy that results in a higher Sharpe ratio. The insight is formalized in the “The Fundamental Law of Active Management”, which states that the Sharpe ratio of a strategy (or its value added) is a function of the skill of the strategy, squared, multiplied by its breadth (or how often the strategy is used). 

_Sharpe Ratio = Breadth × (Skill)[2 ]_ 

Designing more skillful strategies is the best way to increase the Sharpe ratio, but it is generally difficult to enhance a single strategy beyond a certain level. The easier way to boost performance is by adding breadth – i.e., adding strategies that have low correlation to existing ones. In order to create an overall strategy with more skill than its individual strategy components, the correlation between the individual strategies should be low[2] . Indeed, the correlation between the performance of each of the individual factors/strategies is quite low over time.

Admittedly, we are not blazing an original trail with our Bond Market Indicators. Much of the academic work identifying the predictive power of the factors and approach we use dates back more than 20 years[3] . Many investors will already be aware of the approach and some may adhere to it religiously. So why launch Bond Market Indicators ourselves? We think using the model as an input into our duration view will improve the success of our calls considerably over time. However, we do not intend to follow the indicator model at all times. The factors that contribute to the overall signal will not be able to foresee event risks like monetary policy meetings and will not understand what bond market yields are pricing in for those events. In addition, the factors do not include a measure of value which, from time to time, can affect future bond market returns. 

In reality, making the right call on the direction of bond yields is both difficult and important for fixed income investors. It is difficult because there are various approaches that work at certain times and not at other. Calling duration from a fundamental perspective requires an understanding of the central bank response function, its connection to the bond market, and the ability to forecast the macroeconomic data that will be input into that central bank response function. The duration call is important because the main way real money fixed income managers can take risk against their benchmarks is via duration and/or risks which are highly correlated with duration (like yield curve risk). At the same time, fund performance data show most managers fail to beat their benchmarks[4] , especially in long-maturity funds. As a result, we believe most investors will welcome an additional tool to assist in their duration call, even if those investors decide not to follow the tool in a systematic way. 

Looking further into the results shows the strategy generally does well during periods of crisis. But we also recognize that, while we think our backtests suggest the indicator approach is robust, it is fallible. In addition to producing negative returns in about one out of every five years (on average), our indicator has also had long periods of positive, but modest, returns. These lackluster returns occur generally because the signal has lacked conviction. On average, our indicator has suggested being neutral duration ~40% of the time. ## **Signal Construction** 

A critical step we take in constructing our indicators involves aggregating the four factors into a combined signal. Again, the four factors are: 

- Bond market volatility-adjusted carry 

- Bond market momentum 

- Equity market performance 

- Business cycle surprises 

For each factor, we normalize the individual signal by using a cumulative distribution function to cap the signal strength when it reaches high values. This means that the signals take on values from negative 10 to positive 10 as shown in **Exhibit 10** . The normalization mitigates the impact on the overall average in the event of an individual signal growing ‘too strong’. We use a simple average of the normalized signals to create our combined signal. Given that the magnitude of future excess returns is correlated to signal strength in our data set, we neutralize the combined signal when its value is close to zero. Specifically, we define a span around zero (-1.5 to +1.5 on our -10 to 10 scale) where the combined signal takes on the value 0.0. 

**Exhibit 11** shows the distribution of strategy returns, along with risk-adjusted excess returns (Sharpe ratios), for varying signal strengths. The exhibit shows why we choose to construct our signal in this way. Although the risk-adjusted return distribution is not completely smooth within and across countries, there is a clear relationship between performance and signal strength.

## **Why These Variables, and Why Not Others?** 

In the following sections, we review the data that show each of these variables has predictive power in its own right, substantiated in academic literature. But, first, what variables did we exclude? We chose to exclude variables for three different reasons: 

- **The variables have little predictive power in their own right.** This includes valuation metrics, mean reversion strategies, and many economic data releases such as GDP, industrial production and inflation prints, which are frequently released with such time delays to be of limited use to the market. It is possible that we have just not found the correct way to construct variables from these data sources to turn them into useful tactical indicators, but we fear too much manipulation of the data may amount to data mining.[5] 

- **The variables are very similar to existing ones within the model.** For example, we have found changes in credit spreads, implied and realized volatility, and changes in commodity prices can be used to predict short-term bond returns. However, they are also highly correlated with variables already included in the model, and hence tend not to improve the overall performance. 

- **Data limitations prevent us from using the variables.** For example, inflation surprises, as calculated from the difference in reported consensus expectations and realized prints, sound like a promising variable to include, given the importance of inflation to the central bank response function and the bond market. However, the available time series is short and the lag with which some figures are updated means the estimated ‘surprise’ can be a poor proxy for actual market expectations.

The term premium-carry and roll trade involves buying bonds/receiving swaps that look cheap relative to their forward yields. If the realized yield is lower than the initial forward yield, one makes a profit. However, if bonds tend to sell off more when carry is high, thus cancelling out the gains from carry, the strategy will not work. In addition, if the gains from carry are insignificant relative to the general volatility of bond returns, then being long just for carry’s sake is unlikely to be an attractive strategy. The credit spread trade involves being long higher-yielding, but also higher-credit-risk paper. One is compensated for taking on the additional credit risk, and the trade works if the realized default rate is lower than initially priced and credit spreads widen less than the forward credit spread. 

Our focus here is term premium carry trades. The Expectations hypothesis, which postulates that long-term interest rates reflect the unbiased expected evolution of short-term interest rates, suggests being long carry should not be a successful strategy. Given forward yields are a function of the term structure, they should be the market’s best and unbiased estimate of what yields will be in the future. 

## **Expectations Theory Does Not Hold in Practice** 

In reality, this is known not to be the case. In some seminal work done nearly 40 years ago, Eugene Fama showed that forward rates are poor predictors of future spot rates[6] . What this means is that rates are unlikely to rise more just because the forward rate is further away – i.e., the carry is more positive.

## Bond Market Momentum 

## **Is the Trend Really Your Friend?** 

Trend-following investment strategies are widely employed by Commodity Trading Advisors (CTAs) within equity, commodity, and FX markets. Some believe trend-following works in bond markets as well. The success of the approach is impressive, given its simplicity (‘if it is going up, keep on buying it; if it is going down, keep on selling it’). But it also lacks fundamental justification, and the nature of the strategy means one will inevitably be slow to adjust to changes in market direction. 

## **A Powerful Signal to Go Long G4 Bond Markets** 

The academic literature suggests momentum strategies work in government bond markets, and we indeed find evidence of this. In **Exhibit 16** , we show the weekly excess returns data for 10-year Bunds, USTs, gilts and JGBs. We have divided the table in two parts: (1) In the top half of the table, we report the return figures for months when the previous three months’ returns were positive, and (2) In the bottom half, we report the returns for when they were negative. There is a significant difference: subsequent returns are significantly higher if the prior returns were positive. For example, 10-year Bund excess returns averaged +77bp if prior Bund returns were positive, but only 4bp if they were negative. In addition, they were positive more often (i.e., 58% of the time versus 52% when returns had been negative). 

## **A Useful Tool for Setting the Duration View** 

These observations, along with previous academic findings, form our basis for including a long-short momentum factor in our Bond Market Indicators. In creating our momentum strategy, we define the signal as the excess return index level at t-1 less as an exponential moving average of the prior index level spanning a period of four months (effectively a decaying moving average). This differential is divided by volatility, as measured by an exponential moving average of absolute changes in the excess return index, also reaching four months into the past[8] .

## **But Are We Data Mining?** 

A key risk we would flag about a momentum trading approach is event/policy risk. Bond market trends have often been ignited by unexpected changes in policy stance – e.g., the taper tantrum of 2013, or various QE announcements. Similarly, geopolitics also has the potential to abruptly change the direction of yields. A momentum approach, by its nature, responds slowly to such changes and the strategy could overshoot whenever there is a big change in direction. From a practical perspective, this makes defining the appropriate period over which one estimates the momentum trend critical. Rather than optimizing for the look-back period, we applied only the four-month period in our backtesting. This could, of course, be optimized – but at the risk of data mining. 

## **Are Bond Investors Really Slow to React?** 

It may sound strange that government bond market investors can be slow to incorporate new information. Bond markets are dominated by professional investors who have ready access to the macro data that should fundamentally determine changes in bond yields. It sounds far more likely to be the case for investors in, say, small cap equities, where less money is spent on researching more obscure companies and the dissemination of information can be slower. 

However, our experience of working with large institutional investors suggests many need time to implement material changes to their portfolios. It can take time to effect changes in investment policy (boards have to meet to discuss changes, mandates need to be drawn up or amended, risk departments may demand changes) and the large size of some institutions’ portfolios may force them to be net buyers or sellers for extended periods (in spite of the size and liquidity of government bond markets). Further, some government bond investors (reserve managers, pension funds, insurers and banks that face changing regulatory requirements) may need to buy or sell for reasons other than expected investment returns – e.g., accounting/regulatory rule changes – and may cause a persistent trend in the bond market. 

In **Exhibit 18** , we show the historical risk-adjusted returns, given varying signal strengths. Much like our carry strategy, the momentum strategy tends to work better in bull markets when yields are falling compared to bear markets when yields are rising. In contrast to the carry strategy, there is a clear long bias throughout our sample period, which is perfectly understandable given the issues outlined above. 

The momentum strategy has exhibited notably few years with negative return in our sample (see **Exhibit 19** ). This is unsurprising, given the consistent and gradual decline in G4 rates. There have been large drawdowns, notably in 2009 when the signal failed to anticipate the rapid rise in rates across the G4 following the dramatic falls in yields experienced in 2008. ## **Forecasting Term Premium Fluctuations** 

In addition to changes in central bank rate expectations, shifts in the interest rate term premium are a major driver of government bond excess returns over cash. Academic literature suggests that the level of (wealth-dependent) risk-aversion is also an important determinant of the observed risk premiums[9] . The theoretical argument for this relationship is that investors will require a higher premium for holding risky assets if their net wealth declines, and also demand a lower rate of expected return from safer assets in a bid to protect their wealth against further losses. Hence, if risk-aversion rises due to a fall in stock markets, for example, investors are likely to switch into less risky government bonds and accept a lower yield from them. 

Another potential explanation is that the performance of risky assets and risk appetite indicators provides relevant information for bond investors, not only about the appropriate interest rate term premium but also about the path of central bank interest rates, and that the bond market initially underreacts to this information. While it seems surprising that wellanalyzed government bond markets may react slowly to such widely available information, we noted in the previous section that the momentum in bond market returns suggests that investors can be slow to adjust their positioning. 

## **Negative Equity Returns = Higher Bond Returns** 

Looking across G4 rates markets, negative equity market performance over the previous three months has consistently coincided with higher bond market returns since 1992 (see **Exhibit 20** ). Conversely, positive three-month equity returns correlate with lower future bond returns, but less so. We can explain the rationale for bonds to outperform on the back of negative equity market performance by an increase in riskaversion and _vice versa_ . But why bonds still manage to generate positive returns when equity market performance has been positive is not immediately evident. We think this is to some extent a sampling issue: bonds have been in a bull market for the past 20 years due to the declining inflation environment, which has also been supportive for equities. 

When we look at bond performance over other periods – e.g., the US from 1976 to 1986 – excess returns were on average negative when prior 3-month equity returns were positive, but still

positive following periods when the S&P index had gone down. There is still a skew to the return distribution – i.e., losses following equity rallies are smaller than gains following equity sell-offs. However, this makes sense, given that one would expect bonds to generate higher returns than cash on average.

We do this to account for the reason that, if risky assets perform well outright, EM equities generally outperform versus DM. The converse also holds. Our signal is a simple average of the local equity market return and the EM-DM differential z-scores.

## **Bond Returns and Business Conditions** 

In one of the most influential papers of modern finance, Fama & French[10] in 1989 showed that the bond market term premium, or the slope of the yield curve, has had significant predictive power on bond returns historically. Furthermore, the authors found that the variation of the term spread is closely related to business cycle changes, as the yield curve typically reaches its steepest shape around business cycle troughs and vice versa. By extension, this also means that bond returns tend to be high as business conditions deteriorate, while fixed income generally underperforms in a stronger macro environment. 

The links between bond returns and the development in the broader economy have since been well established, and we find that most studies focus on either (1) the short-term price action around the release of economic data[11] , (2) outright changes in the business cycle[12] , or (3) how good a predictor the shape of the yield curve is for future recessions[13] . 

Armed with this evidence of the relationship between bond returns and business cycle changes, we explore the relationship between bond yields and business cycles further by analyzing how weekly excess returns correlate with surprises in the outcome of business condition indicators. We use initial outcome and consensus time series dating back roughly 10 years for what we gauge as the most closely followed business condition indicators across US, European and UK rates markets. We believe that there is virtue to this approach as opposed to using outright levels or changes, as financial markets are inherently forward-looking and hence react strongly to surprisingly good or bad economic data.

## **Long/Short Signal** 

We construct a long-short signal to test which of the abovementioned indicators are useful for predicting bond returns. Specifically, we calculate deviations of outcomes versus (median) consensus expectations to signal positioning, using a range of activity indicators to calculate a composite surprise index for each of the four individual markets. Hence, if a given business condition indicator surprises positively, we go short an amount of bonds equivalent to the magnitude of the deviation from consensus (on our -10 to 10 scale). Similar to the momentum strategy, we do not apply fullsample z-scores to the business cycle indicators, as this would effectively incorporate the average historical surprises and hence possibly systematic forecast errors. Instead, we allow for trends in the surprises over time to guide positioning. These would otherwise have to be over- or undershot in order for the indicator to recommend positions. 

As shown in **Exhibit 25** , the strategy has generated positive excess returns over the full sample period – which is significantly shorter than the other strategies given the lack of consensus data.
