## One Size Doesn't Fit All 

A softer euro area outlook implies a more dovish ECB. This will affect the 'ECB periphery', but we feel investors continue to underestimate significant differences across the region. 

**Economics | One size doesn't fit all. The 'ECB periphery' is very diverse:** Our euro area colleagues now expect the ECB to keep rates on hold well into 2020, and to raise rates only once, in June 2020. This has an impact on growth and interest rates in the 'ECB periphery' (CEE, Scandi, Switzerland). However, investors often underestimate how diverse the 'ECB periphery' is. We look at seven central banks and identify three groups: i) Those 'on a journey' to normalisation (CNB, NBH, NBR); ii) Two 'hesitant hikers' (Norges Bank, Riksbank); and iii) Those 'holding back' (NBP, SNB). 

**Rates and FX strategy | What's EUR's relationship status? It's complicated:** Should EUR yields rise, we expect CHF, SEK and NOK yields to see larger upside than yields in CEE due to closer ECB dependency. On the FX side, people often consider EURUSD as a proxy for currencies in the ‘periphery’, but we show that this isn't always the case. It is important to distinguish whether it is USD or EUR driving the performance of the EURUSD pair. Given our call for broad USD weakness in 2019 and EUR strength, we argue that the effect on the ‘periphery’ currencies is ambiguous. CZK is our top pick in CEE, due to rising rates. Also, we see Scandi FX appreciating ahead, while CHF is one of our preferred shorts.

## Executive summary 

**Economics | What is the ECB periphery, and why does it matter?** When investors think of European macro, inevitably EUR and the ECB outlook come to mind. What about the countries that don't use EUR? How do we think about them? In this note, we look at CEE economies, Scandinavia and Switzerland, which we label the 'ECB periphery'. These are small, open economies which have mostly flexible exchange rates and trade mostly with the euro area. Together, their GDP is just over 20% of the euro area and their population is close to 30%. Inevitably, they are all affected by the EUR and ECB outlook. However, their central banks differ substantially. 

**We identify three groups:** i) Those central banks who are **'on a journey'** to normalising rates (Czech National Bank, National Bank of Hungary, National Bank of Romania); ii) Those who are **'hesitant hikers'** (Riksbank and Norges bank). They also want to normalise rates but higher leverage and housing concerns represent more serious obstacles; iii) Those who are **'holding back',** sometimes out of choice (National Bank of Poland), sometimes out of fear of FX gains (Swiss National Bank). Next, we use our analysis to sketch out the rate outlook for the coming two years, and draw investment conclusions. 

## **Rates strategy | Scandi and CHF yields would be more affected by higher EUR** 

**rates:** We looked at how much ECB yields explain local rates in the ‘periphery’ and found the relationship to be stronger in Scandi and CHF. This could be explained by the closer dependency of the central banks to the ECB through the monetary policy channel. This suggests that should EUR yields rise as the ECB starts signalling rate hikes, CHF, SEK and NOK yields should see the largest upside potential due to spillover effects. CEE yields might receive less of a boost but we note that the relationship with Poland is strengthening. 

**FX | EURUSD is** _**not**_ **a good proxy of the periphery:** Contrary to popular belief, the assumption that when EURUSD appreciates currencies in the ‘periphery’ perform positively is not a robust one. Unlike for yields, EUR is slightly more important in CEE than Scandi while CHF is an outlier, likely due to its ‘safe haven’ status. Given that we expect both EUR appreciation and broad USD weakness, we argue that the effect on the ‘periphery’ currencies depends on which leg is driving the EURUSD pair. In CEE, CZK is our favourite pick. SEK and NOK should also appreciate once EMU economic prospects improve, while CHF is one of our preferred shorts. 

## **EEMEA equity strategy | Overweight Poland and the Czech Republic, equal-weight** 

**Hungary:** From an EEMEA equity strategy standpoint, we remain overweight Poland and the Czech Republic and equal-weight Hungary. However, we find that the change in expectations for interest rates one year ahead has explanatory power in the relative performance of local equities, mainly in the Czech Republic and Poland. Therefore, if interest rate hikes keep being pushed out and rates expectations maintain a downward trend, this could be a headwind for local stocks. We prefer to have exposure to those countries via Pekao SA. The stock is on our stock ideas list for EEMEA because: i) Positioning in Poland and the stock is light among GEM funds, on our estimates (see our note here); and ii) Our EEMEA banks analyst likes the company's resilient asset quality, mix shift, appealing valuation and bottom-up turnaround story.

## Economics: What is the 'ECB periphery'? 

_Pasquale Diana, Georgi Deyanov_ 

**Introducing the 'ECB periphery':** Throughout this note, we define the 'ECB periphery' as those countries in Europe where there is an independent currency and monetary policy and investors perceive that there is a strong link to the ECB, due to the open nature of these economies. More specifically, in this note we will focus on CEE (the Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland and Romania), Scandinavia (Sweden, Norway) and Switzerland. The UK – as one of the G4 central banks (together with the Fed, ECB, and the BoJ) – does not form part of our analysis. 

**How do these economies compare to the euro area?** The seven economies we look at have a combined nominal GDP of just above 20% and population of close to 30% of the euro area. While the ECB periphery is fairly diverse, two broad groups can still be distinguished. Poland, the Czech Republic, Hungary and Romania (the CEE economies) have lower GDP per capita while Sweden, Norway and Switzerland stand out with higher GDP per capita relative to the euro area (Exhibit 1). The difference is still valid but less pronounced when expressed in purchasing power parity terms (i.e., correcting for price-level differences). As well as being richer, Sweden, Norway and Switzerland stand out as having a much more leveraged private sector than the euro area and the CEE countries.

## How ECB-dependent? 

## _Pasquale Diana, Georgi Deyanov, Matthew Pennill_ 

Our euro area colleagues have analysed in detail how they expect the end of QE to play out (see ECB QEnd: Removing the Punch Bowl, January 28, 2019). They have also recently scaled down their expectations for ECB normalisation (see European Economics & Strategy: From Boom to Gloom, February 11, 2019). What does the new ECB outlook mean for the 'ECB periphery' central banks? And more broadly, how do they factor the ECB into their reaction functions? 

In terms of the peripheral central banks, they all target inflation, with varying degrees of focus on underlying measures of inflation (Exhibit 8). Their monetary policy horizon will change, as will the degree of transparency in the information they provide. 

The **Riksbank** , **Norges Bank** and **Czech National Bank** are extremely open and transparent in the information they make available. Their forecasts include endogenous policy rates and FX paths, which are published. The **National Banks of Hungary** and **Romania** also produce forecasts based on endogenous policy rates, but these are not made available to the public. FX assumptions are also not revealed. At the other end of the spectrum, the **NBP** in Poland and the **SNB** in Switzerland produce a conditional forecast based on unchanged rates, and they do not reveal the FX assumptions underlying their forecasts.

## **ECB base case: A cautious approach** 

_Daniele Antonucci, Matthew Pennill_ 

**With growth now set to slow below trend and core inflation going nowhere for some time, we've revised our ECB call. The central bank should now stay on hold throughout this year and raise the depo rate just once in 2020.** 

**Weaker growth…** Part of this slowdown is due to growth reverting to trend, as we've been expecting for a while. But there's another factor which has dragged our forecasts lower, related to transitory effects lasting longer than expected and external demand remaining subdued. We envisage the euro area economy expanding by just 1%Y this year, well below the trend rate and consensus. 

**…and a slower rise in core inflation:** With growth proving softer, the output gap is unlikely to close much further. If anything, resource utilisation may fall somewhat. This means there's limited or no upward pressure on underlying inflation from the labour market, and so core inflation is set to move broadly sideways for the next few quarters. Further out, we still expect some modest acceleration in core inflation on our revised forecasts, but it's likely to be mild and at least in part driven by temporary factors dropping out of the annual comparison. 

**ECB lift-off to come later:** With the central bank set to update its forecasts in March, we expect significant downward revisions. The ECB will then have to communicate its next steps by mid-year. Given the uncertainty around the outlook and the likelihood that some of the more transitory factors that are currently weighing on growth will only peter out later this year, we expect the central bank to wait for longer before making the depo rate less negative. Our base case is that its guidance will be adjusted to commit to keeping rates unchanged "through the spring" of next year, with a single depo rate hike of 15bp to come in June 2020. 

**QE reinvestments continue throughout:** While the central bank has now ended net asset purchases, we expect reinvestments to continue through 2020, and probably beyond. Over time, this could also involve a passive 'operation twist', with the central bank reinvesting the principal from maturing short-term bonds into longer-term ones, to control long-end rates. More immediately, we also expect confirmation of plans for a form of TLTRO replacement this spring, but at less generous terms than the existing programme. 

**Still some policies left in the toolkit:** Beyond yield curve control of sorts, through an operation twist, the ECB could also adjust the parameters of its purchase programme to expand the scope of its asset holdings and restart QE if needed. But this isn't our base case for now, and even if the ECB did manage to overcome a number of challenges, we think that the resulting programme is unlikely to easily match the one that's just ended. To see this, one should consider that the central bank could raise the issuer limit for AAA-rated sovereigns to 50% from 33% and add extra corporate, covered and even bank bonds to the pool of eligible assets. These steps, which aren't likely to happen (let alone in one go) without a deep crisis unfolding and, even if they were to materialise definitely not likely to happen in one go, could leave ~€1.5 trillion of extra assets available to purchase. This isn't a small amount, but it also may not last very long in a more severe downturn.

## FX/rates strategy: How do movements in the core affect the periphery? 

_Gek Teng Khoo, Filip Denchev, James Lord_ 

**The ECB impacts 'periphery' assets through central bank dependency…** ECB policy undoubtedly has implications for assets in other European countries. For example, in 2015 the SNB removed the EURCHF floor after the ECB signalled QE expansion, as maintaining the floor would have resulted in an unsustainable expansion of the SNB’s balance sheet. The decision sent CHF soaring 17% against EUR within two days. Moreover, for years the Riksbank has also kept monetary policy accommodative despite the economy closing its output gap in order to keep pace with the ECB and prevent large currency-strengthening capital inflows. This pushed Swedish real yields lower as local inflation rose, contributing to SEK becoming the most undervalued G10 currency. Clearly, ECB policy has an impact on 'ECB periphery' rates and FX through influencing the actions of the smaller regional central banks. Even though CEE central banks' policies may be less dependent on the ECB compared to the 'DM periphery' (Sweden, Norway, Switzerland), there is still limited room for them to deviate from the ECB significantly. 

**...and correlation of yields:** However, the impact of ECB policy on other European rates and FX goes beyond influencing local central bank policy. Globalisation has reduced the barriers to cross-border flows, increasing the correlation of bond yields across countries, such that local yields are no longer just driven by domestic monetary policy. When the ECB engaged in QE, the significant liquidity injection depressed yields in the EMU, causing investors to hunt for yield abroad. This resulted in an outflow of capital which put downside pressure on foreign yields too. ## **How do we identify banking sectors that should be structural winners when rates move up?** 

Bank revenues benefit from policy rate hikes. This is from a combination of not passing on the total rate move to time deposits (deposit beta), while hiking yields on variablerate loans and benefiting from higher yields on cash; thus expanding net interest margin. How the banking market is structured will shape the ability of banks to pass on higher rates to borrowers while delaying a hike in funding costs. This relationship is slightly more nuanced than policy rate transmission, which does not discern between transmission to loans versus liabilities. 

In our analysis of banking markets that are more structurally geared to benefit from higher rates via expanding margins, we highlight that banks have a greater capacity to pass on higher yields to borrowers often when _policy transmission is lower,_ i.e., borrowers in economies with low leverage and lower interest burden have a greater ability to absorb higher interest rates. We superimpose our macro colleagues' policy transmission scorecard and introduce banking sector-specific considerations to determine which banking sectors are more structurally geared to improving margins in a higher rate environment (Exhibit 64): 

- _Banking sector liquidity:_ Banks have a greater ability to delay passing on higher rates to depositors if there is a low system loan to deposit ratio; this implies that banks can afford to let depositors switch into different products without stressing liquidity. The downside to a system with a low loan to deposit ratio is that banks may compete to channel excess liquidity into lending at competitive rates, offsetting the benefit to margins. Hence, we must think of liquidity in conjunction with competitive factors such as consolidation and market shares of foreign and state-owned banks. 

- _Sight versus time deposits:_ The convenience of a current account, with instant accessibility to funds, means that banks can often pay little to no interest on sight deposits/current accounts, even as policy rates are increased. For a depositor, the cost of switching from a non-interest-paying current account to a time account is likely to outweigh the benefit when the delta is 10bp, but as rates increase this becomes a more attractive substitution. 

36 

_Banking sector profitability:_ Banking sectors where profitability is depressed will find less pushback from stakeholders when converting higher rates to increased returns. 

_Consolidation of the market:_ A more consolidated banking sector implies a greater ability of banks to pass on higher rates to borrowers while delaying passing on higher rates to higher funding costs. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) is a standard measure of consolidation. HHI of <1,500 is considered a competitive market and HHI of 1,500-2,500 a moderately concentrated market. 

_Market share of foreign banks:_ CEE banking markets are dominated by foreign players native to Austria (Erste and RBI), Italy (UniCredit), France (SocGen) and Benelux (KBC). CEE margins have historically been more attractive than domestic margins in the European markets these banks originate from. Hence, CEE markets with a greater share of foreign banks may prove more competitive on loan prices as a highly competitive margin in CEE may still be considerably more attractive than in developed Europe. 

_Market share of state-owned banks:_ State-owned banks may be affected by factors outside of traditional competitive forces to either delay passing on higher rates to borrowers or accelerate deposit transmission. Therefore, a higher share of stateowned banks in a country could imply a lesser margin benefit from higher rates.

## Real estate: Elevated leverage in Sweden is our key concern 

_Bart Gysens, Chris Fremantle and Jonny Walker_ 

**Lower for longer – is that a positive for real estate stocks, or not?** It is difficult to ignore the interest rate debate when contemplating the outlook for real estate. 

**So, first the facts (i.e., what we do know):** Historically, more often than not (around 75% of the time over the last 40 years), property stocks across Europe including the UK have outperformed the European equity market when bond yields were rising and underperformed when they fell. Over the entire period, the quoted property sector's outperformance relative to the equity market was highly positively correlated (+86%) with US bond yields, suggesting a statistically meaningful coefficient of determination (R  of 74%). Over that period, the correlation with Bund yields was also strongly positive2 but arguably less so (correlation of +71% with a 50% R ).2 

**There are two distinct periods during which this sector has traded as a bond proxy:** The only two periods when this wasn’t the case were around the 'tech bubble' when real estate was considered 'old economy’ and therefore out of favour while bond yields were rising, and the reversal of that post-bubble; and also most of this decade during which the equity market seems to have said: “if there is hardly any growth or inflation, how can there be rental growth? And if there is no rental growth, this is not _real_ estate but _nominal_ estate. And that means these stocks are bond proxies”. 

**Something for the bulls, but probably more for the bears:** Last year European property stocks outperformed the market as the 10-year UST was rising, while Bunds are broadly at the same level as where they started the year. So, if you chose to be bullish you could argue that the meaningful rise in US Treasury yields (and as mentioned above, these have historically been the bonds with the strongest correlation to the European property sector's relative performance) has coincided with this sector outperforming, heralding the end of this sector trading as a bond proxy. However, you could also argue that investors see the US recovery as US-specific, and that the Bund trajectory now matters more than ever. For what it's worth, our core Overweight-rated stocks in this sector are supported by structural growth (logistics, German multi-family), with material rental growth potential irrespective of the growth or rate outlook. 

**Sweden has been outperforming:** Swedish property prices have been rising strongly across the board – both commercial and residential property, across the quality spectrum. We estimate that prime Stockholm office values are up more than threefold over the last decade, more than any European market we monitor. Swedish property companies have seen their share prices rise on average by 20% per annum for the last eight years. 

**Efforts to curb house price inflation:** The Swedish regulator has tightened mortgage amortisation rules, which heralded a (short-lived) correction in asset prices. We are yet to see a property market where property prices merely stabilise after a protracted multi-year double-digit rise in values. And we are yet to see a correction in residential property markets without this feeding through into commercial markets; over the last 35 

45 

years, Swedish residential and commercial property markets have experienced overlapping cycles. 

## **We are mainly concerned about highly levered plays (and many Swedish property** 

**companies have above-average financial leverage):** Swedish property companies tend to finance with shorter-duration debt and with high loan-to-value (LTV) ratios. There are multiple reasons for this. Swedish commercial property leases are short (often three or five years) and companies often match duration of debt with income duration. Swedish listed property companies do not have tax-efficient Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT) status, as such leverage acts as a tax shield, while they are not required to pay the majority of recurring earnings in dividends, providing them with more flexibility to retain earnings when needed. Moreover, some market participants also attribute this aboveaverage leverage appetite to the fact that Sweden came through the Global Financial Crisis relatively well. 

## **We consider Swedish property companies to be most exposed to higher rates within** 

**our pan-European coverage...** They have above-average financial leverage, and their debt financing tends to be short term, with a relatively large part at floating rates. Moreover, their short lease structure drives a fast feedback loop into the profit and loss account from weaker demand. 

**…if and when rates rise (which is now further away than we all assumed before):** But it looks as though such a negative catalyst is further away than assumed previously, and so it looks as though these companies have more to time to adapt to a different environment – indeed, Fabege management said on February 5 that "an economic slowdown is fast approaching". We remain cautious on Swedish property stocks such as Fabege and Castellum, both of which we rate Underweight. 

**CEE property also not without challenges:** The significant amount of capital inflow in CEE property, in recent years mainly from South Africa, has driven material yield compression and higher capital values. A key issue with CEE is that developing markets are vulnerable to bouts of oversupply; planning and investing for the long term in markets with above-average growth potential can lead to material oversupply when growth disappoints – CEE property markets have historically been victim to this. 

## **Not just a supply issue; lack of liquidity and domestic institutional capital are even** 

**more significant, we argue:** We also worry about the fact that CEE property markets are illiquid with limited domestic institutional capital, and which therefore are at the mercy of the ebb and flow of foreign capital. Many CEE markets are funded with eurozone debt, and as such higher eurozone interest rates could have a material impact.
