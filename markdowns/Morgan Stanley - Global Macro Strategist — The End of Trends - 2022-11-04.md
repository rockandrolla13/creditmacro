## The Three Most Consensus Views In European Macro 

Last week, we covered three views in US macro that we believe are held by the consensus, based on our conversations with clients. This week, we cover what we believe are the most consensus views in European macro, again based on the client interaction we have had in recent weeks. It is not hard to find reasons to be bearish on Europe or investors to articulate them. Here are three views we commonly encounter: 

High energy costs will result in a trend of de-industralisation, pushing the EUR lower over 2023 and beyond. 

The bull case for the EUR is USD weakness. 

Rates in Europe have further to rise, given ECB rate hikes and the start of QT. 

## **High energy costs will result in a wave of de-industrialisation** 

Since the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the common view is to see energy security as a new and semi-permanent threat to European growth potential and the value of the EUR. For decades, Europe has gained a competitive advantage from access to plentiful supplies of cheap Russian gas. This underpinned Germany's export sector and ultimately the euro zone's vast current account surplus.

European gas price futures have been extremely volatile over the course of 2022, with one-month forward TTF prices significantly lower versus the August 2022 extremes. Yet, prices remain well above levels seen in 2021. More worryingly for Europe's long-term competitiveness, is that the longer-term forward prices remain significantly higher vs 2021 levels, suggesting a long-term rise in European input costs. 

How Europe responds to this loss of competitiveness will determine whether the bears are right to view it as a long-term threat. The initial response of European governments has been to subsidise industry and households to protect them against high prices. This is an understandable political response to the sudden shock of significantly higher prices. Without it, many businesses would be at risk of failing, and many households would be unable to afford utility bills. Social cohesion and political instability would likely rise. 

The long-term response should be more debatable. Europe is pushing ahead with greater investments into onshore renewable sources of energy, many of which are cheaper than fossil fuels. But this will take time to come online, and the prospect of this source fully replacing Russian gas seems remote. Imports of more expensive sources of energy like LNG and thus permanently higher energy prices look set to be the norm. 

Persistent government subsidies would prevent private sector innovation to raise energy efficiency, and so would likely worsen European fundamentals and result in persistently higher energy demand and imports, weighing on the EUR. A gradual withdrawal of subsidies would likely force innovation to improve energy efficiency and thus lower the demand for energy per unit of economic activity. This would be a positive for productivity, competition, Europe's trade balance, and the euro.

Last week, we wrote about the possibility that European economies embrace automation with greater vigor in the years ahead (see EUR | The Rise Of The EURobots?). Demographic pressure from Europe's ageing societies does provide some incentive to investment in greater automation, while manufacturing represents a large sector in which jobs could potentially be automated. 

3 

Should the necessary capex be delivered to create this automation, it would likely be positive for productivity and growth – assuming also that lost labour can be absorbed effectively back into new sectors. The competitiveness shock from energy prices could potentially force greater efforts towards increasing efficiency. 

This is very much a long-term story and highly tentative. It's not at all clear that Europe will respond to pressures from high energy costs in this way. Yet, the consensus view we are hearing is that Europe will not rise to meet the challenge, and this is what is reflected in the low value of the EUR, and the likely persistent decline in estimates of fair value for the EUR. That is the consensus. 

## **The bull case for the EUR is USD weakness** 

This view is based on the idea that there isn't a great deal of positive news in Europe that can represent upside risks to the EUR. Since the value of the EUR is always relative and not absolute, investors tend to think the EUR's value can only really rise if the value of other currencies falls, and particularly that of the USD. 

The view that the EUR faces long-term pressure from high energy prices and a trend of de-industrialisation is just the latest manifestation of the view that the primary drivers of Europe's outlook are typically negative for the outlook, with positive efforts around reforms tending to be reactive and stabilising in their effect, rather than representing upward momentum. 

The idea that EUR upside can only come at the expense of the USD has some obvious but hardly useful logic to it. As two of the world's most important currencies, it would be natural to think they might move inversely. If we look at the rolling 48-m correlation of monthly changes in the EUR and USD trade-weighted currency indices, it is indeed typically negative (Exhibit 5).

Over time, there have been periods when the USD and EUR rose on a trade-weighted basis, like the first half of 2018, but those occasions are relatively rare. Yet, if we look at the performance of the EUR and USD in level terms, it's clear that both have risen since 2016. And so while the EUR's performance has been lacklustre vs the USD itself, it is not the case that EUR has performed poorly as a result. 

While the performance of the EUR (which has been positive on TWI basis since 2016) is not consistent with the general sentiment (structurally bearish), what investors mean when they say that the catalyst for EUR upside is USD weakness is that there are few ways to make a positive case for the EUR. This is because there are few positive catalysts and so the only way the EUR can strengthen is if it "gets lucky", and the US outlook deteriorates more than the European one. 

There are a number of long-standing issues that have turned many investors into "perma bears" , such as the lack of common fiscal policy, weak demographics, the absence of a strong capital market or banking union, and barriers that result in persistently weaker levels of investment in the euro zone vs the US, hindering trend rates of growth. 

This is a consensus view that we would broadly agree with. We see EUR/USD recovering next year, but largely because of the USD weakness that we expect. Nonetheless, the idea that there are few positive EUR catalysts in the year ahead is not something we agree with. 

One positive case to make for the EUR next year is the possibility that European savings held abroad are repatriated to take advantage of the higher yields at home. European nations exported significant amounts of capital into US bonds and equities during the years when ECB interest rates were at zero. 

With European yields higher, and a possible deficit position on the current account to finance, this large stock of offshore savings could well be returned and support the EUR. (See Global Macro Strategy: EUR: The Taming of the Shrewd.) 

## **Rates in Europe have further to rise, given ECB rate hikes and the start of QT** 

The ECB's deposit rate is just 1.5%, with headline CPI at 10.7% and core inflation at 5% for October. Our economics team do not expect to see inflation peak until December. Against this backdrop, many investors see value in paying front-end rates as they expect to see the ECB hiking rates considerably further, to above 3.0% vs the Morgan Stanley expectation for a terminal rate of 2.5%. 

With QT expected to be announced at the December ECB meeting too, the consensus amongst investors is to position for further weakness in peripheral European bonds and wider peripheral yields. 

Given the tendency for inflation in most economies to surprise on the upside, and for central bank officials to accelerate monetary policy tightening over the past year, it is not much surprise to see that investors remain convinced of higher yields to come. 

Earlier this week, ECB President Lagarde would have done little to dissuade these views by stating in her interview in Latvia (see here), in language mirroring that used by Chair Powell, that they " _still have quite a way to go_ " to raise rates to the level needed to reduce inflation back down to the 2% target.

## Beware the Lack of US Debt Growth 

The dog days of summer - and US debt growth - are well behind us. It's clear that increases or decreases in debt - from the US federal government and from the private sector - have implications for demand. Mapping the relationship between debt-driven demand into economic activity - either GDP or inflation - can be difficult. 

However, that doesn't mean we should ignore debt growth altogether. In the wake of the pandemic, the federal government delivered stimulus that far surpassed any amount seen - in real terms (constant dollars) - since at least 1962 (see Exhibit 10). 

Federal government debt increased at an annual rate of over $4 trillion dollars from the year ending June 2020 through March 2021 - about double the annual rate of debt growth after the Great Financial Crisis (GFC). More recently federal government debt hasn't grown at all over the year ending June 2022 (see Exhibit 11).

## A (YCC) Change Will Do You (No) Good 

Investors increasingly express understandable concerns about the Bank of Japan (BoJ) ending its Yield Curve Control (YCC) policy. Investors can't bring themselves to declare an end to the global bond bear market while this possible policy change lingers. 

The idea is that, once the BoJ ends YCC, yields on JGBs - especially with 10y maturities - will rise dramatically. And as those yields rise, investors in Japan will no longer have appetite to buy non-yen bonds because JGB yields will look more attractive suddenly. 

When analyzing possible bond and currency market flows from investors in Japan, we always think about each major investor type separately. Commercial banks in Japan will invest differently than life insurance companies in Japan. And pension funds in Japan (and trust banks) will invest differently from asset managers in Japan. 

Commercial banks in Japan have had, over time, the largest impact on non-yen rates markets. In contrast, life insurance companies and pension funds have had the largest impact on the currency markets. Below, we focus on the commercial banks in Japan and the possible rates market behavior if and when YCC ends. 

Assume that YCC ends and 10y JGB yields rise 100bp to 1.25%, for example. Commercial bank portfolios own multiple trillions of yen worth of JGBs. City banks hold JPY 63 trillion (47% of nominal GDP) while regional banks own JPY 14 trillion (11% of nominal GDP, see Exhibit 18 and Exhibit 19).

## Turning Neutral on the USD 

We turned neutral on the USD earlier this week in the aftermath of the FOMC decision. We have reflected this in our G10 and EM positioning, by closing our short EUR/USD position and removing our bearish call on EM FX. See US Economics & Global Macro Strategy: FOMC Reaction: Are You Gonna Go My Way & EM Strategy: Time to Square Up. 

While we published the view in the aftermath of the Fed, the decision to turn neutral on the USD is more multi-faceted than that. However, on the US rates side of the equation, there are several factors that have reduced our conviction that USD will continue to climb in the near term. 

Our colleagues in rates strategy view US rates as likely to consolidate around current levels, limiting further upside for rates differentials to boost the USD. 

Market pricing now implies an expectation that the Fed delivers rates hikes to 5-5.25%, more than our economics team expects. That is in part because our economists expect inflation to decline in the US next year, in line with the path that swap markets currently imply. A decline in inflation would limit upside potential for US rates as the Federal Reserve takes comfort that inflation is on track to return to 2%. 

As we have previously written, our conversations with investors reveal a widespread belief that economists and markets will underestimate inflation again next year. As a result, we believe markets would move much more if inflation were to fall (as our economists forecast and inflation markets price) than if inflation remains supported. 

11 

Limited upside for US rates also limits the risk-reward of long USD positions, given the close relationship between US yields and the USD this year. 

Monetary policy in the US is now at a level that can be considered restrictive and so with that there is a reasonable probability that we see more of a slowdown in the US economy, and with it more downside surprises in individual data points. Admittedly, we are not quite there yet with the US labour market report reflecting a tight labour market still. Thus, at this stage of the cycle, we are only recommending that our clients reduce their long USD exposure and turn neutral, and not that they turn bearish and go short. 

Outside of the US, the outlook is improving. Our European economists have recently revised _up_ their GDP forecasts for the eurozone, while gas storage levels are about as healthy as they could be, and the weather has so far turned out to be mild. 

The outlook for China, while difficult, appears to be shifting, with a decent chance that the authorities make moves toward ending the Zero Covid framework. This would be in line with Morgan Stanley's view and would help to bring greater balance to the global economy with less upward pressure on the dollar. 

In line with this, we are recommending short USD/THB, and USD/CNY exposure via 6m risk reversals. This short USD exposure offsets the remaining long USD positions that we have, which are short GBP/USD and long USD/CAD.

**Distance = speed * time:** Imagine you are covering a distance of 500 miles, at a speed of 75 miles per hour. With around 375 miles covered, you consider slowing down your speed to 50 miles an hour and may need to slow down further to 25 miles and hour. Because _distance = speed * time,_ a slower speed means more time to cover those same 500 miles. Said another way, when you "slow the pace", there is a "ways to go" before you stop (or pause). This is exactly what transpired at the November FOMC meeting. 

_**Powell on speed/pace:** As we move more into restrictive territory, the question of speed becomes less important… That's why I've said it's appropriate to_ _**slow the pace** of increases._ 

_**Powell on time:** It's very premature in my view to be thinking about or talking about pausing our rate hike. We have a_ _**ways to go** . We need ongoing rate hikes to get to that level of restrictive. We don't know where that exactly is._ 

_**Powell on destination:** I've also said that we think that the level of rates that we estimated in September, the incoming data suggests that that's going to be higher_ 

The Fed is hiking rates toward a terminal rate of about 5%. It has been hiking in clips of 75bp per meeting, and wants to slow down the pace of hikes to 50bp per meeting, as early as December. If the Fed wants a slower pace of hikes, it will need more time to get to the same terminal rate. That explains Chair Powell's comments at the press conference at the November FOMC meeting, where he said it is appropriate to slow the pace of rate increases, while also suggesting that it is premature to talk about pausing (see comments in grey box). That is generally what has played out in the market since the FOMC meeting (see Exhibit 29).

## How might the rates market react if the BoJ scraps YCC? 

With overseas investors increasingly focusing on how an eventual removal of the BoJ's YCC might disrupt the JGB market, we revisit the topic of how the rates market might react to this. We have addressed this question in a number of previous reports including "FAQs On JGB Market Functioning" and "Might A Widening Of 10y YCC Bands Trigger A Sharp Rise In Interest Rates?". The degree to which JGB yields end up rising will ultimately depend in significant part on prevailing economic conditions as well as the way the BoJ communicates its intentions. 

If the BoJ were to make its “normalization” move with Japan facing an overseas-like inflation overshoot well beyond +2%, then market participants might very well start to price in further rate hikes beyond the exit from YCC and negative interest rates, with overseas experience having already demonstrated that the resulting repricing of Japan’s policy rate trajectory could end up driving yields quite sharply higher. 

**But markets are perhaps unlikely to price in a full-fledged BoJ rate hike cycle if Japan’s CPI ends up stopping short of a major overshoot.** Our economists do expect core CPI inflation (all items less fresh food) to accelerate beyond +3.0% (YoY) in the near future, but then envisage a sharp deceleration from January 2023 as electricity and gas charges are driven lower by the Kishida administration’s recently announced “comprehensive economic measures” (see Exhibit 51). 

Moreover, our economists still do not expect core-core CPI inflation (all items less foods and energy, meaning that “cost-push” inflation is largely excluded) to reach +2% within our current forecast horizon. 

32 

**Given such circumstances, we would not expect the JGB market to price in all that much more than an exit from YCC and negative interest rates.** Any rise in JGB yields is thus likely to be determined mostly by the degree to which the term premium is repriced, while rates expectations would likely be anchored.

## **Why Z-Spreads vs. ASW Spreads?** 

Asset swap spreads are another commonly cited RV metric, in no small part because they are tradable (unlike z-spreads, which are theoretical constructions). As a brief summary, there are two principal ways of trading linker ASW, namely (1) proceeds (most common for TIPS) and (2) par-par. Going long each package is done similarly: 

**1. At inception:** An investor buys a cash linker, and enters into a paid swap position 

**2. Until maturity:** The investor pays the linker coupons, and receives the floating rate _plus_ the ASW spread 

**3. At maturity:** The investor pays the inflation uplifted principal and receives the notional 

The difference between the packages is that: 

**1.** In **proceeds ASW** , the investor buys the linker at market price, P, and enters into a swap with notional P 

**2.** In **par-par ASW** , the investor buys the linker at market price, P, but enters into a swap with par notional. At maturity, the investors receives par and pays (inflation uplifted) par

Neither ASW spread is an ideal RV measure. First off, both spreads depend (to different degrees) on the linker's dirty price, and thus are functions of the linker's coupon (see Exhibit 83). Second, both spreads depend on the linker's time-to-maturity, as longerdated issues' dirty price will deviate more significantly from par (see Exhibit 84). 

Third, there is a mismatch between linkers' and swaps' rate sensitivity, since linkers' cash flows are generally back-ended. This implies that a 1bp real-yield shift moves a linker's ASW spread by more than 1bp. This problem is exacerbated for longer-dated linkers, further showing how ASW are not comparable across issues.

Z-spreads amend these issues, by alleviating the impacts of issue-specific characteristics. Furthermore, by construction, a 1bp shift in real yields results in a 1bp move in the z- spread. This means that z-spreads, while not tradable, are a more informative measure of RV for linkers.

## Trading Strategy 1 – "Trade Longs/Fade Shorts" 

When the 5-day moving average crosses above the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract (long duration) and hold for a 25-business-day period. When the 5-day moving average crosses below the 20-day moving average, buy the futures contract and hold for a 25-business-day period. In short, this strategy buys futures when the Simple Moving Average Crossover (SMAX) generates both a long and a short signal, given the historical outperformance of long signals traded long and underperformance of short signals traded short. Given that the SMAX could generate both a long and a short signal within the predefined holding period, an investor may have a 200% long position since each of the two signals would be traded in separate portfolio sleeves.

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period.
