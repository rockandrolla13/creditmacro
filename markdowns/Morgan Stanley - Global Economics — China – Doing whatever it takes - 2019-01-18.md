## China – Doing whatever it takes 

Investors are most skeptical about China's growth outlook in the near term. In contrast, we expect further easing, which would help bring about a growth improvement from 2Q19. This, plus accelerating growth in other EMs, reaffirms our key 2019 macro call that EMs will retake the lead.

**Skepticism on China's growth outlook remains high:** Investors have pushed back against our view that China's easing will work and that growth will improve from 2Q19. The skepticism is largely based on the fact that past easing efforts have not worked and growth has been persistently surprising on the downside in recent months. 

**Why past easing efforts has not worked...:** The pace and nature of easing was subdued until Nov-18 as policy makers had been constrained by a conflicting objective of seeking to maintain a tight lid on controlling financial stability risks. The easing efforts have also been aimed at encouraging the private sector to lift its spending, which has been less effective, considering that private sector sentiment was dampened by both past regulatory tightening and a rise in trade tensions. 

**…and why it will work now:** While trying to assess the outlook for China, it is important to keep in mind the policy objectives and the outcome policy makers are striving for. Maintaining financial stability has been the focus for the last two years, but as the downdraft in growth begins to weigh on the labour market, managing the growth cycle has become the top policy priority. Policy makers have therefore moved the counter-cyclical growth management into full swing. What that translates into is a policy response that will be tailored to address the extent of the downward pressures on growth. Put simply, the greater the downward pressures on growth, the stronger the policy response. 

**China's growth to improve from 2Q19:** The pace of easing has intensified since Dec-18. Moreover, the nature has also changed as policy makers are now encouraging the public sector to do the heavy lifting in spending. Indeed, we expect further easing measures in the months ahead, which will culminate in broad credit growth accelerating to 12.5%Y by year-end (vs 10.6% Y now), and that the augmented fiscal deficit will widen by 150bps in 2019 relative to 2018. To be sure, we expect 1Q19 growth to still be weak at 6.1%, but that should mark the bottom of the growth cycle as we expect growth to improve from 2Q onwards.

## China – Doing whatever it takes 

**China in focus among investors:** Our key macro call for 2019 was that emerging markets would retake the lead in driving global growth. The key building blocks of this call are that (1) US growth would slow, (2) while growth in EMs would hold up well due to a favourable domestic policy mix and receding external headwinds. In our conversations with investors so far, there has been a greater acceptance of a slowdown in US growth and an acknowledgement of positive fundamentals in most emerging markets outside of China. However, it is with regard to the China outlook where we have received the most pushback. 

**Why has there been skepticism?:** Our base case thesis is that China's easing works and that growth will stabilise from 2Q19 onwards, while investors are skeptical on both counts. Skepticism has emerged because the easing efforts since Jun-18 have not yielded results, as growth has decelerated further and persistently surprised on the downside in recent months. We attribute this to three causes: (1) the pace and nature of the easing efforts had been different until Nov-18, (2) external developments in the form of Fed rate hikes, stronger US dollar had been somewhat of a constraint for China to ease (though lesser as compared to other EMs), and (3) trade tensions escalated in a more meaningful manner than expected before the G20 meeting in late November. 

**Don't forget – labour market is always the overarching policy priority:** As growth has slowed more meaningfully and further away from the policy makers' growth objective, there will be an attendant impact on the labour market. Policy makers have therefore stepped up the pace and also altered the mix of easing efforts. While trying to assess the outlook for China, it is important to keep in mind the policy objectives and the outcome policy makers are striving for. In today's context, our read is that the overarching objective is addressing the growth deceleration such that it does not impact the labour market. That will likely translate into a policy response that will be tailored accordingly to stabilising growth. In other words, the more the downward pressures on growth, the stronger the policy response. 

**Skepticism is the highest at around the trough:** Drawing on our experience in tracking China, pessimism and skepticism on the growth cycle builds with every downside surprise and tends to peak right around the trough in the growth cycle. For instance, post the August 2015 move by the PBOC to adopt a new fixing mechanism, investors were concerned that economic growth momentum would slip even further, which then prompted a sharp move in USDCNY. Instead, in line with our expectations, policy makers stepped up on their easing efforts and this helped stabilise growth by early 2016. Similarly, in early 2017, investors were highly skeptical about the impact of the supplyside reforms and remained of the view that the risk of a financial shock was high. Instead, as we argued in our blue paper, the productivity trend in China was improving, the worst of the debt-disinflation cycle was behind us and China would be able to stabilise its debt to GDP trend, which was eventually achieved in 2018, driven by a decline in corporate debt to GDP.

**Degree and nature of easing had been relatively subdued until Nov-18...:** Initially, the degree and nature of easing had been different compared to other cycles. As noted earlier, policy makers had made a concerted effort to maintain financial stability and had achieved the initial results of stabilising its debt to GDP ratio. While they have eased in reaction to rising external uncertainties, maintaining the objective of controlling financial stability risks has kept them from moving too aggressively. What was also different in this cycle was that easing measures were aimed at encouraging the private sector to spend. However, private sector sentiment had been impacted by various regulatory tightening measures (such as tightening of financial regulations on shadow banking activities and wealth management products as well as the potential imposition of stronger enforcement on social insurance schemes) and was dampened further by the escalation in trade tensions, which then prevented spending from picking up.

**More easing to come:** We expect that policy makers will take up further easing measures in the coming months in the form of: 

1) Higher quota for local gov't special bond issuance (Rmb2.2trn in 2019 vs. Rmb1.35trn in 2018 - a delta of 1% of GDP); 

2) VAT cuts amounting to RMB 400-600bn (0.4%-0.6% of GDP); 

3) Further 300bps of RRR cuts or even potentially interest rate cuts if the economy deteriorates significantly; 

4) Further relaxation of property market measures 

5) Pro-consumption measures such as subsidies for purchase of autos / household appliances.

**We expect growth to improve from 2Q19.** To be sure, we expect growth to still be weak, at 6.1% Y in 1Q19. However, that should mark the bottom of the growth cycle as we expect growth to improve from 2Q onwards, eventually reaching 6.4% Y in 4Q19. This growth improvement in China, plus the acceleration in growth that we expect in other EMs excluding China will help emerging markets retake the lead in driving global growth. 

**Risks:** There are two key risks to the China macro outlook - the efficacy of the recent policy easing measures and the outlook on trade and trade tensions. On the former, if the recent policy easing measures prove to be less effective than we think or if the transmission to actual economic activity were to take longer than expected, it would delay the start of growth improvement. On the latter, if trade growth were to weaken significantly or if the outcome of trade negotiations were to turn adverse, it would be an additional drag on growth. However, we do think that, given the reported progress in bilateral trade negotiations, the chances of escalation beyond March 1 are lower.
