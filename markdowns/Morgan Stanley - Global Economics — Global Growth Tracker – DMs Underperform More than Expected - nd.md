## DMs underperform more than expected 

**Weaker DMs dragging global growth to below trend:** We estimate that global GDP growth is likely to clock in at 3.4%Y for both 4Q18 and 1Q19, marking two consecutive quarters of below-trend growth. In our year-ahead outlook, our key call for 2019 was that emerging markets (EMs) retake the lead. We expected developed markets (DM) growth to slow, led by the US, and EM growth to hold up well. Recent developments have meant that DMs are surprising to the downside earlier and in a more pronounced fashion than we expected, taking global growth below trend. 

**DM growth weakness a dominant theme in 1Q19, not just about idiosyncratic factors:** Coming into the year, we projected slower DM growth in 2019 principally because of fundamentals such as a maturing business cycle and tighter resources. In addition, the fading impact from fiscal stimulus and past monetary tightening would weigh more on growth momentum in the US, where our forecasts are significantly below consensus. This is now being exacerbated by a number of idiosyncratic/transitory factors (government shutdown in the US, weak car production due to a change in emission standards in Germany and a protracted impact on confidence, French protests and a slow recovery from natural disasters in Japan), leading us to project a much sharper sequential slowdown in growth in 1Q19. Specifically for the US, the direct impact from the government shutdown (coupled with the indirect impact on consumption) now lead us to GDP growth of 1.7%Q versus 2.5%Q previously. These estimates account for the impact of the shutdown until end-January, and our US economics team estimates that real quarterly GDP growth will be directly impacted by about 0.05pp for every week the government is shut down, with the indirect effects likely to be much larger (see US Public Policy Brief: The Lowdown on the Shutdown, January 18, 2019). 

**Growth in EMs excluding China is holding up well; China’s growth to improve:** While DM growth is slowing, the growth momentum in EMs excluding China has held up relatively well as they make headway on their respective recovery paths. Softer oil prices, a weaker USD and lower US yields have eased financial conditions in EMs, lending further support to growth. Moreover, inflation in EM has continued to surprise on the downside, pushing back the need for further rate hikes. In China, we expect growth to improve from 2Q19 as the impact from easing measures kicks in fully from March. 

**Tracking high-frequency indicators:** A number of high-frequency data points – most prominently trade and PMIs – indicate that the global economy lost further momentum in the last two months. PMIs have fallen in a sharp, broad-based manner to a 27-month low (see Global Macro Chart Board: PMI Update - December 2018, January 3, 2019), driven by a fall in new orders. Flash PMIs for both the euro area and Japan indicate a further drop in activity for the month of January. Global trade momentum has also softened, as evident in the December Asian trade data (see China Economics: Exports Confronted with Twin Headwinds, January 14, 2019, and Korea Trade Tracker: December Trade: Ending the year on a weak note, January 1, 2019), corroborated by a further fall in the first 20 days of Korean exports in January. However, we think that the magnitude of the recent slowdown has been somewhat amplified by trade tensions and inventory unwind payback from front-loading. For instance, Asian exports had continued to post strong growth despite a slowdown in global growth in 2Q-3Q18, due to front-loading as

## Three policy anchors of the growth outlook 

In our framework to assess the global growth outlook, we are focused on the following three factors: (1) China’s pace of easing (watching broad credit growth as the key metric) and confirmation of the effectiveness of easing in growth data points; (2) The Fed’s policy flexibility; and (3) The outcome of US-China trade negotiations. 

Relative to where we were late last year, these factors have moved somewhat in the right direction, though the overhang and its impact on global growth have yet to clear completely. 

3 

**1) China – doing whatever it takes:** The key debate around China is the pace and effectiveness of easing. While investors are sceptical on the China outlook, we are more confident that the pace of easing is already intensifying and that it will be effective. We think that a stabilising labour market is the key policy priority now. We expect further easing measures which will help to bring about a growth improvement from 2Q19 onwards (see Global Economics: China – Doing Whatever it Takes, January 18, 2019). 

**2) The Fed – how flexible?** The Fed has signalled some flexibility on its policy path – though more so on rates and to a lesser extent on balance sheet normalisation (which is still ongoing). We are watching the next FOMC announcement on January 30 (and the minutes of this meeting to be released on February 20) on its guidance on balance sheet policy (see US Economics and Fixed Income Strategy: Insight into the Balance Sheet, January 11, 2019). 

**3) Trade – deal or no deal?** Finally, on trade policy, our base case is that given the reported progress of bilateral negotiations between the US and China, the chances of escalation beyond March 1 have reduced. 

**Bottom line – US to underperform EMs:** We continue to expect that US growth will slow, projecting 4Q/4Q 2019 growth at 1.7% versus the consensus estimate of 2.2%. In contrast, growth looks set to improve in China and other EMs, allowing the growth differentials to swing in EMs' favour and meaning that EMs will retake the lead.
