## **European Banks - Equity & Credit | Europe** 

## Untested Cycle - watch for corporate risk 

Deteriorating credit cycle will have a tangible impact on banks' earnings into 2019/20. Watch for corporate exposure on peaking margins and general slowdown in exports-led Eurozone economies. Unsurprisingly, given mix, UBI, Deutsche, Commerzbank, Intesa, Unicredit appear most exposed. 

## **While we remain ECB watchers, we think the European corporate credit cycle is** 

**at risk from broader macroeconomic weakness.** We are 9 years into the current cycle for core Europe, and while we see no significant structural imbalances and private load debt seems manageable, the corporate cycle is more of a question mark. We are concerned about the diverging GDP growth cycle in Europe, and see higher risk to corporate credit quality in the export-led and manufacturing based economies of Germany, Italy, Belgium and the Netherlands. We will be watching banks with higher exposure to wholesale lending in those countries as bellwethers into 2019/20 . 

**Credit view: Defaults in European credit remain low for now, but more selective funding markets are likely to weigh on the weak tails.** We expect bond markets to be more selective in the companies they fund in the post-QE environment and default rates to start rising from the current lows. We anticipate high yield default rates in Europe to rise to 2.5-3% in our base case by year-end, which would take us back to the middle of the 2012-18 range. The ratings drift of European corporates (% of upgrades minus downgrades) has been worsening since mid-2018, indicating that downgrade pressures may be starting to build for the first time in several years. 

**We assume provisions increase 40% in Europe by 2021e, +10bps in aggregate, yet with significant country differences.** In our 2019 outlook, we increased provisions significantly, arguing that while retail should hold up, corporates will lead the provisioning increases from a low base. Our 3-year provision forecasts are still well below the previous peaks - as the debt burden has not increased, financing costs in aggregate are still low and corporate loan growth in Europe has been lacklustre. We see provisions normalising most in the UK, Belgium and the Nordics. 

**Sector view:** We are ~6% below consensus for our banks, largely on normalisation of provisions. We continue to be defensive, as economic fundamentals are deteriorating too quickly for our liking. Our preferred list reflects this view - we look for resilient business models with sustainable dividends. European most preferred list: **Santander, Credit Agricole, Lloyds, HSBC, KBC.**

## Untested, shallow cycle. Industrial recession key risk. 

_Stepping back, the last three years have seen de-risking of European banks' balance sheets via capital raises, resolutions, low credit growth and high savings growth. Credit risk at the banks has diminished structurally, also as a function of banks having been disintermediated by deepening corporate bond markets, intra-industry financing and private credit. At the same time, however, banks' ability to generate operating profits has stagnated (negative rates, competition, regulatory cost inflation, technological service revolution) leaving this first line of defence vulnerable to any deterioration in the credit environment. 2019/20 will serve as a test of how those rehabilitated balance sheets will take on the broader based economic down cycle in the Eurozone._ 

**Fundamentally, we see a relatively benign credit cycle in the Eurozone.** Private debt load and its affordability look manageable. Consumers should be more resilient than corporates, given stable levels of unemployment and c.2-4% wage inflation. Our corporate concerns lie less with leverage accumulation but rather with slowing GDP in key export economies. We expect corporate defaults to only creep higher, consistent with other cycle signals. 

**Our total provisions are 40% higher in 2021 than 2018 for the sector, 20% in Eurozone, >100% in the UK.** Our forecasts assume cost of risk for European banks increases from 30bps average in 2018 to 34ps in 2019 and 40bps in 2021e (Eurozone from 32bps in 2018 to 39bp in 2021e). Although, we note that this is still low versus the 5-year average, and it also reflects substantial balance sheet progress since the Eurozone crisis. 

Yet, we also are wary of the disproportionate slowdown of the export and manufacturing led economies of Germany, Italy, Belgium and the Netherlands, for which we screen in our exposure analysis. We have seen enough deterioration in cyclical industrial data year to date to make us nervous. Unsurprisingly, domestic banks - UBI (Underweight), DBK (Underweight), Intesa (Equal-weight), Commerzbank (Equal-weight), Unicredit (Overweight) screen as most exposed. The next most exposed names in our screen are the Benelux banks, but in contrast their earnings are more protected given they have the highest pre-provision profit margins, particularly KBC. 

**Credit markets in Europe still remain benign and we expect greater idiosyncratic, rather than systemic, stress - the view from our credit colleagues.** Modestly deteriorating fundamentals and rising defaults point to continued progression of the European credit cycle. At the same time, however, we do not foresee an indiscriminate turn in our 2019 base case. Instead, we believe that the market will re-test the weaker links within the European credit universe, namely lower quality HY and BBBs.

## Stock implications 

**Our analysis suggests the following stocks look exposed to the corporate exportdriven credit cycle theme: UBI (Underweight), Deutsche Bank (Underweight), Commerzbank (Equal-weight), Intesa (Equal-weight), Unicredit (Overweight). The list isn't a surprise, given their domestic positioning or geographical mix. Yet what sets them apart from Benelux banks, are weaker operating margins providing lower first line of defense should corporate credit risk deteriorate further.** 

**Who is most exposed to risks from the credit cycle?** We score five factors: 

**1.** Overall exposure to segments that we view as higher risk, blended for corporates and SMEs, country (Italy, Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands) and sector splits. 

**2.** Growth in corporate lending since 2016, where we aim to capture the banks that have accumulated the most exposure, most recently. 

**3.** The position of individual banks within their own credit cycle, comparing current cost of risk vs 5-year average and worst print since 2013. 

**4.** Asset quality starting point (net corporate NPE ratio), with our assumption being that banks with cleaner starting books are better placed to withstand any changes in the current cycle. 

**5.** Pre-provision profitability, i.e. the first line of defense. On our framework, using combined credit cycle metrics, BMPS, KBC, UBI, UCG, MB are the five lowest scoring banks.

## Where are we in the EU credit cycle? 

**Untested Cycle.** Sector provisions are currently very benign, but for the next 3 years, we assume cost of risk normalises upwards. Our provision estimates in 2021e are more than 40% higher than in 2018 (although still benign vs. historical peaks), split into the Eurozone at ~40bps, UK domestics ~50bps and whole sector (incl Nordics) at ~35bps. 

## **Several leading indicators suggest the cycle is turning...** 

- Credit market pricing has deteriorated as of late, with the distressed ratio rising to ~5% now (from ~1% a year ago). 

- Lending growth in the Eurozone is declining noticeably with consumer/ corporate loan growth down to ~6% and ~3.4% (from recent highs of 7.5% and 4.1%). SME originations are also slowing across several geographies. 

- The banks expect lending standards to tighten in 1Q, a turning point post substantial net easing of conditions since 2014, and a function of asset quality expectations - rejection rates for loan applications have increased across all categories. 

**...yet corporate cost of financing is currently at an all-time low** across bank loans and rates on wholesale markets are also attractive. This, together with corporate leverage (net debt/EBITDA) near historical highs, leaves margins sensitive to any increase in funding costs. 

**What are European banks pricing in?** Our base case forecasts are 7%-6% below consensus in 2019-20e. Looking at the composition of bear case risk, a turn in the credit cycle is the biggest factor, post EUR rates expectations reset. In our bear scenario we see 20% average earnings risk to consensus estimates (and 16% vs MSe), putting the sector on 10.8x P/E in 2020e for an 8.6% bear case ROTE (vs a 10-year average of ~10x NTM, which incorporates two crisis periods). We stay defensive and reiterate a quality bias in our key Overweights. 

**Where are we in the EU credit cycle?** A credit cycle typically tends to have four stages: expansion, downturn, repair and recovery. Stepping back, the post-crisis years have been indicative of a repair/recovery phase, characterised by cost-cutting, deleveraging and business right-sizing. Balance sheet de-risking of the European banks has been substantial via capital raises, resolutions, low credit growth and high savings growth. Balance sheets look healthier and asset quality repair has been notable, particularly in the periphery, with NPE stock down by 40-60% in absolute terms across Spain, Italy and Ireland since its peak, while the outstanding volumes of nonperforming exposures relative to GDP have also improved, now at ~10% (Exhibit 11).

## **Looking back over the past 10 years, the cycle can be broken down in three periods:** 

**1) Global Financial Crisis (2008):** During this period, wholesale/UK banks were particularly affected, writing down on ~100% of their starting TBV from 2006 through 2009 in total provisioning charges (Exhibit 12). A number of institutions ran into capital and liquidity problems, with state aid extended to banks ranging from Northern Rock, Lloyds, and RBS in the UK, several Irish banks, as well as ABN and ING in Benelux, among the most prominent examples. 

**2) Eurozone Sovereign Crisis (2012):** In this period of stress Eurozone retail banks were naturally the most affected due their geographical mix and sovereign holdings composition. Provisions were sustained at very elevated levels over a number of years, eroding TBV by ~25% pa over 2011-12, with some peripheral countries continuing to pay the price until very recently (e.g. Italy where provisions taken over 2014-18 were comparable in magnitude to the provisions taken during the peaks of the sovereign crisis). 

**3) Current Cycle (2014-2019):** Provisions broadly normalised from 2014 onwards and have stayed at very benign levels since. ## **Step 2: Screen for geographical exposures to Italy, Belgium, Germany and Netherlands.** 

Next, we narrow down our screen to banks with corporate exposures where the borrowing counterparty is located in Italy, Belgium, Germany and Netherlands, since these are the economies within the Eurozone that are export led and therefore we see as most at risk from a slowdown in the credit cycle. 

Using the EBA Transparency Exercise data, we have visibility on €9.4tn of corporate credit risk (broadest definition) held by European Banks. Of this, we identify €1.7tn (~18%) where the end counterparty is located in Italy, Belgium, Germany or the Netherlands across large corporate, specialised lines and corporate-SME loans. Bank lending in Europe remains fragmented predominantly among national lines, therefore unsurprisingly domestic champions screen as the most exposed to these segments on our analysis of bank-by-bank exposures. Top down, domestic banking groups control over 90% of total banking assets in the main European countries (including international exposures). Similarly, nearly 80% of credit risk of Eurozone residents is held by domestic banks, which is the most inward-focused banking structure out of the main European regions (i.e. relative to Nordics or the UK).

**ii) Growth in corporate lending** since 2016, where we aim to capture the banks that have accumulated the most risk. We base our analysis on the EBA credit risk data, again narrowed to corporate/SME risk exposure in our selected geographies. 

27 

**iii) The position of individual banks within their own credit cycle:** For this purpose we compare 2018 cost of risk vs the bank's own 5-year average (2013-2017), or our own assumption for a normalised level for the Italian banks, as well as its own worst cost of risk print (i.e. trough point) over the period. 

**iv) Asset quality starting point:** Using NPE data, we rank banks on their starting net NPL ratio, with our assumption being that banks with cleaner starting books are better placed to withstand any changes in the current cycle vs. a bank with a higher burden of nonperforming exposures, all else equal. 

**iv) Pre-provision profitability and ability to withstand shocks** . German banks screen poorly on this metric, while Austrian banks and Mediobanca screen relatively better vs the sample average.

## **We prefer banks with stronger capital and more resilient** 

**earnings.** A combination of low visibility on earnings and weak macro leaves us cautious on Italian banks. The higher starting point of NPLs, together with lower profitability and regulatory headwinds has meant the market has been discounting capital shortfalls. One could argue that, at a median of 0.4x TBV 2019e, this is already priced in, with the market discounting €15-20bn1 provisioning shortfall. We remain defensive: Overweight Mediobanca and Unicredit, and Underweight UBI. 

## **NPL rundown accelerated, but some banks risk being too late in** 

**the cycle.** Despite the increased efforts by the banks and the progress achieved so far, we estimate it will take ~3-5 years for Italian midcaps to reach an NPL ratio of 5%, assuming 0-1% growth in the loan book and current NPL inflows. We think the tangible risks of ratcheting regulatory standards from TRIM, Basel IV, EBA guidelines, calendar provisioning to name a few, combined with a multi-year NPL reduction plan increases execution risk. 

**M&A needed to complete clean-up of balance sheet.** The subdued ROE at midcaps underscores the need for a structural change. We think consolidation of a highly fragmented market would be critical for banks to reach scale and improve returns. M&A would allow banks to raise capital and fund the clean-up of the balance sheets as well as benefit from efficiency gains.

## Appendix: Glossary 

## **ECB glossary of key terms (full list here)** 

**Monetary financial institution (MFI):** Financial institutions which together form the money-issuing sector of the euro area. These include the Eurosystem, resident credit institutions and all other resident financial institutions whose business is to receive deposits and/or close substitutes for deposits from entities other than MFIs and, for their own account (at least in economic terms), to grant credit and/or invest in securities. The latter group consists predominantly of money market funds. 

**Other financial intermediary (OFI):** A corporation or quasi-corporation other than an insurance corporation and pension fund that is engaged mainly in financial intermediation by incurring liabilities in forms other than currency, deposits and/or close substitutes for deposits from institutional entities other than MFIs, in particular those engaged primarily in long-term financing, such as corporations engaged in financial leasing, financial vehicle corporations created to be holders of securitised assets, financial holding corporations, dealers in securities and derivatives (when dealing for their own account), venture capital corporations and development capital companies. 

**Insurance corporations and pension funds (ICPFs):** The insurance corporations subsector consists of all financial corporations and quasi-corporations which are principally engaged in financial intermediation as a consequence of the pooling of risks mainly in the form of direct insurance or reinsurance; the pension funds subsector consists of all financial corporations and quasi-corporations which are principally engaged in financial intermediation as the consequence of the pooling of social risks and needs of the insured persons (social insurance). Pension funds as social insurance schemes provide income in retirement, and often benefits for death and disability. 

**Investment fund (IF):** A collective investment scheme that raises funds by issuing shares or units to the public. The proceeds are invested in financial assets and in non-financial assets (usually real estate). 

**Non-financial corporation (NFC):** A corporation or quasi-corporation that is not engaged in financial intermediation but is active primarily in the production of market goods and non-financial services. 

**Households (HHS):** The household sector covers individuals or groups of individuals as consumers, but also as entrepreneurs (i.e. sole proprietorships and partnerships). Non-profit institutions serving households are a separate institutional sector, although they are often reported together with households.
