## A Premium for Size 

We study the impact of issuer size on secondary liquidity and bond performance. We conclude that the liquidity advantage in 'mega caps' is overestimated while 'small caps' offer a generous premium for hold-to-maturity investors. 

**The big became bigger in the QE era...** Of the 582 issuers in the iBoxx € IG index, 40 have an outstanding notional in excess of €12 billion. Together these 'mega caps' account for 36% of the outstanding index balance. The shift in size composition is largely driven by the growth in the size of the non-financial complex, which now accounts for 52% of the mega cap basket, up from 41% in 2015. There is also a much higher representation of BBB names and long-dated bonds versus the broader market. 

**...while the small remained small:** At the other end of the size spectrum, we count 170 issuers with less than €1 billion of iBoxx EUR IG index-eligible bonds. While the number of these issuers has increased in the QE era, the relative weight of these small caps has remained flat over the past five years. We create custom indices based on these size baskets to analyse liquidity and performance patterns over the past five years.

## A premium for size 

The concept of size premium is well established in equity markets. That stocks of smaller companies should offer a premium versus their larger counterparts is deeply embedded in academic work and conventional wisdom. Although there has been much debate on whether the premium still exists, most valuation frameworks still adjust for market capitalisation, in keeping with the celebrated three-factor model. 

By contrast, size effects are much more difficult to define and replicate in bond markets. While each issuer in an equity index has a single equity instrument associated with it, the iBoxx EUR IG index on average has four bonds associated with each issuer (2,410 bonds in total from 582 issuers). Bond characteristics also vary significantly even when they are issued by the same issuer, which in turn affects the pricing and returns analysis. So, differentiating between bond-level and issuer-level factors is difficult. 

Despite these constraints, we believe that size considerations have become more important within credit markets in the post-QE environment. At one extreme, the substantial growth in mega BBB balance sheets prompts the question – does better liquidity in these names increase their downside beta or reduce it? After all, the sheer size of these debt structures is likely to make them the mainstay of most credit portfolios, which in turn creates concentrated flows in down markets. At the other end of the spectrum are small cap issuers that have been able to access bond markets for the first time. Has the market started pricing in a higher illiquidity premium in these names, creating some alpha opportunities for hold-to-maturity buyers? 

**Sample and methodology:** In this note, we study the relationship between issuer size and spreads. As a first cut, we try to answer the question by creating customised issuer and bond baskets rather than adopting regression or clustering techniques. For the purpose of this note, we use a €12 billion size threshold for 'mega cap' and €1 billion upper limit for 'small cap'. The subjectivity of these size definitions introduces some limitations to the analysis which we intend to remedy in later work. We then create custom spread indices to estimate and analyse the size premium over time. The issuer composition of the size baskets is based on current debt outstanding, but we add a duration constraint of 3-7 years on the bonds to account for composition differences. Finally, we study the size premiums within key sectors.

## The 40 largest issuers account for 36% of the IG index notional 

Of the 582 issuers represented in the iBoxx EUR IG index, 40 had at least €12 billion of index-eligible bonds outstanding. The total stack of these mega cap structures is a little over €700 billion – more than 35% the total index balance. In 2015, before the ECB launched its asset purchase programmes, these €12 billion+ issuers accounted for 26% of the index notional. The shift in balance sheet mix is particularly apparent in the nonfinancial sectors, where the mega cap contribution has increased from 18% in 2015 to 30% now. At the other end of the size spectrum are issuers with less than €1 billion of index-eligible debt outstanding. The number of such small issuers has increased from 135 to 170 over the past five years, but their relative contribution has been flat at ~5% by volume and 30% by count.

**Financials still dominate the mega cap basket, but less so than in the past:** Not surprisingly, the sector distribution of the largest issuers is weighted towards financials (specifically banks). However, the relative contribution of banks has declined from nearly 60% in 2015 to 48%, consistent with the bank deleveraging theme and a partial replacement of market funding with central bank programmes. As a result, non-financial names now account for more than half of the mega cap balance now. 

Within non-financials, consumer goods (mainly autos), telecom and energy all find a larger representation in the mega cap basket than their broader market share would warrant (Exhibit 4). The 'other' basket is comprised of one healthcare name (Sanofi). Sectors that are under-represented within the mega cap index and have also seen their weights drop since 2015 are utilities and industrials. Meanwhile, the small cap concentration has increased most in REITs and consumer goods sectors, while utilities and oil & gas have seen a modest decline.

## **Term structure of debt issued by mega caps is comparable with the broader IG market:** 

For the non-financial names, we compare the maturity profile of the different size buckets in Exhibit 8. ## Liquidity dynamics 

**Liquidity is a function of size in normal markets:** Sector, ratings and maturity distributions help us to understand the compositional differences, but the key difference between large and small caps is their liquidity. As trading volume data are sparse, we focus on the bid-ask spread to assess the evolution of secondary liquidity. Exhibit 9 compares this measure across different size baskets defined in the previous section. 

We make three observations based on the chart. First, although the aggregate bid-ask has recovered from its late 2018 high, it is still ~10bp off the QE lows. In other words, implied transaction costs have increased with the drop in ECB purchases. Second, the liquidity premium between small caps and their larger counterparts is above the pre-QE levels. In other words, there is clear evidence of increased tiering in secondary liquidity based on the size of the issuer. Finally, we also note that the liquidity gap (difference in bid-ask spreads) based on size narrowed sharply during the December sell-off.

## Estimating size premium 

To better understand the effects of size on spread performance, we created custom indices based on issuer size and tracked them over time. The bond baskets are rebalanced every month in sync with the overall index. As noted earlier in the note, the size categorisation is at the issuer level based on the _current_ index-eligible debt outstanding. However, when analysing absolute spread performance, variations in bond maturities and other factors need to be normalised for. We therefore specifically focus on non-financial issuers but also include the real estate names in these spread indices. Next, we restrict the spread calculations to bonds with 3-7-year duration at each point in time and incrementally introduce ratings and sector constraints. As a result, our overall sample shrinks to 793 bonds from 353 issuers. 

**A spread discount for mega caps in BBBs and a premium in As:** Our spread analysis indicates a high correlation to the overall market, with mega-caps acting as a slight leading indicator (Exhibit 13). This basket typically trades marginally tighter than the overall market, consistent with the better ratings distribution and the liquidity advantage. However, the size effects are more visible only in the BBB ratings cohort, with single As trading in a much tighter band (Exhibit 14). Interestingly, single A rated mega caps trade wider than their smaller counterparts while size favours performance in BBB rated names. The directionality to some extent is counterintuitive because we expected the latter to price in a higher premium for downgrade risk. Our hunch is that the outsized exposure of the single A mega cap basket to auto names is introducing some distortions. So, it is not the size factor but the sector skew that is reflected in the calculations here.

## Investment implications 

**We recommend reducing exposure to large BBB balance sheets in non-fins:** While the mega cap basket had tended to outperform the market as spreads widen (Exhibit 18), we believe that liquidity trends late last year suggest that the assumption of better liquidity can be challenged in periods of stress. More importantly, we believe that the impact of fundamental deterioration may be more significant for the largest BBB balance sheets. These 'fallen angel' concerns have taken a back seat in recent weeks as companies start pulling financial flexibility levers, which in our view explains the outperformance of these sectors. But at current levels, we believe that investors should consider reducing exposure to the mega cap BBBs and either move up in quality or down in the size bucket. 

**Small caps may offer some alpha opportunities for hold-to-maturity investors:** For some time now, we have emphasised the risks posed by small first-time issuers in periods of stress (see 2019 Outlook). We finally see evidence that markets are charging higher illiquidity compensation for funding smaller issuers. The size premium has also been sticky in the recent market rally and appears generous in comparison to the broader market spread (Exhibit 17). Investors running hold-to-maturity mandates are best positioned to lock in this illiquidity premium.
