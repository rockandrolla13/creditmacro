## **Overview** 

## **Euro area** 

**ECB monetary policy strategy.** The ECB aims to keep the annual inflation rate in the euro area below but close to 2.0% over the medium term. Its interest rate decisions are based on a comprehensive analysis of the risks to price stability, which takes into account both the short- and medium-term risks emanating from current and prospective economic and financial developments, as well as the longer-term risks emanating from monetary and credit developments. 

**Inflation.** Among the Euroland inflation data, the German regional state data are the most important, as they are published during the reference month and, together with data from Spain, Italy and Belgium, are used in the preliminary estimate of EMU HICP (Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices). They account for almost two-thirds of the euro area and are published at least two weeks ahead of the French inflation data. 

**Money and credit indicators.** Because of its role in the ECB’s monetary policy strategy, M3 money supply growth still receives some attention. The ECB’s preferred gauge is the three-month average of the annual growth rate. The ECB also looks at credit growth, in particular loans to the private sector. 

we have found that aggregating answers to specific questions (assessment on current production, orders and expected production) from the main surveys is more relevant than headline indices. Our proprietary ‘business compass’ (Surprise Gap and GDP Indicator) is largely based on these manufacturing surveys. 

**Industrial production.** Industrial production is an important monthly indicator that comes ahead of GDP and thus provides a first-cut indication about GDP growth in the same quarter. While industrial output accounts for only 24% of GDP, it explains half of its volatility. The reliability of the series as a tool for business cycle analysis is questionable, however, because data are often significantly revised. Moreover, it is released with a considerable time lag. We therefore tend to rely on business surveys for up-to-date information about activity growth. 

**Labour market data.** The German labour market report is the first to be released in the euro area, at the end of the month under observation. However, as the German employment data lag the unemployment data by one month, the report only provides timely information on German unemployment. Therefore, like the Euroland unemployment rate, it is of less significance than the US non-farm payroll report. 

## **United Kingdom** 

**Purchasing managers’ surveys.** Markets pay attention to the Euroland PMI surveys (manufacturing and services) produced by Markit because they offer a simple reading (above or below 50), are comparable to the US ISM tally, and are released in the first business days following the month under review. More recently, Markit has also started to publish a flash estimate. However, we find that the PMIs do not add much to the information available from national business surveys (see below), which have much larger samples and a longer history. 

**National business surveys: Ifo, INSEE, ISAE and BNB.** The most closely watched of the traditional business surveys is the German Ifo survey, which historically correlated closely with German GDP growth. However, in recent years, there has been a growing gap between the Ifo headline index and German GDP growth, which makes the _changes_ in the Ifo index more relevant than its _level_ , in our view. Most business surveys are released around the 25th of the month under review. For the purpose of euro-area business cycle analysis, 

**The UK is on the outskirts.** Although a member of the EU, the UK does not belong to EMU. Hence, the handling of monetary policy falls to the Bank of England (BoE). 

**2.0% CPI inflation target.** As it has since May 1997, the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) of nine members meets once a month to make a decision on interest rates, which are set with a view to meeting the government’s 2.0% inflation target in (broadly) _two years’ time_ . As a result, data-watching in the UK is often focused on assessing the future development of prices. 

**Key data.** Economic indicators can be broadly divided into two categories: information emanating directly from the BoE and data from the real economy.  Decisions made by the MPC, minutes from the MPC meetings, and the quarterly _Inflation Report_ fall into the first category, and inflation, GDP, retail sales, and average earnings fall into the second.

## **ECB Council meetings** 

The ECB Council, which consists of 6 Executive Board members and the governors of the national central banks of the 16 euro-area countries, usually meets twice a month at the Eurotower in Frankfurt am Main, Germany. But only the first meeting in the month is used to discuss monetary policy. The interest rate decision is announced at 12:45 pm London time and is usually followed by a press conference with the ECB President at 1:30 pm. A live webcast and transcripts of the press conference are available from the ECB’s website, www.ecb.int. The primary goal of the ECB is to maintain price stability in the euro area over the medium term (defined as “an annual increase in the HICP of below but close to 2%”). 

ECB monetary policy is based on a two-pillar strategy. In the first pillar, the ECB assesses the outlook for price stability over the short and medium term, taking into account a broad range of factors. In the second pillar, the ECB carefully analyses 

monetary developments in the euro area, notably broad money supply (M3), to gauge longer-term inflationary pressures and to monitor the financial system. The results are then cross-checked against each other. 

Four times a year, the ECB staff (not the Council) publishes macro projections for growth and inflation over the next two years. Even though these projections now assume market-based interest rate and oil price expectations and unchanged exchange rates, they are only one factor in the Council’s decision on interest rates and do not necessarily represent the Council’s view of the most likely outcome. A week after the monthly press conference, the ECB publishes its Monthly Bulletin, which is largely backward-looking in nature, and usually reiterates the points made in the press conference. The in-depth research articles, contained in the bulletin, tend to be more interesting. The second monthly meeting typically focuses on operational matters, and is usually  not followed by an announcement or a press conference.

## **Euro-area M3 money supply** 

Even after the revamp of the ECB’s monetary policy strategy in May 2003, which slightly downgraded the role of monetary aggregates, M3 money supply remains among the 10 most important indicators in the euro area.  The focus now is mostly on the bank lending dynamics, a key driver of M3.  Traditionally, the ECB assessed monetary developments by comparing actual M3 growth (based on a three-month moving average) with its reference value (currently at 4.5%), which was regarded as compatible with price stability.  This comparison has been abandoned, however, in favour of a broader analysis of monetary developments. 

As the annual rate of money supply growth can be affected by base effects, more recent trends in money supply growth are best judged by looking at the seasonally adjusted month-on-month change. Alternatively, as the monthly data can be volatile, the six-month annualised rate, also computed by the ECB, is a useful gauge of monetary developments. Among the determinants of money supply growth, loans to the private sector and longer-term liabilities of the banking system (not part of M3) stand out. By contrast, little attention is paid to credit to the public sector and changes in net foreign assets. 

The report has a fixed release date (around the 28th day of the month following the month under observation) and is released at 9:00 am London time. The press release is available from the ECB’s webpage. In May 2003, the ECB Council also dropped its annual review of the reference value for money supply growth, which stood at 4.5% at the time. It is derived from the ECB’s estimate of potential output growth in the euro area, the ECB’s medium-term inflation target, and the change in the velocity of money. 

## **Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP)** 

In contrast to the US CPI, the Eurostat HICP is not seasonally adjusted and monthly changes can be difficult to interpret because they depend on seasonal factors, such as sales or movements of fresh food prices. The annual inflation rate, by contrast, makes it difficult to gauge the most recent trends and can be influenced by base effects. Taking on board this gap in the statistics, the ECB publishes seasonally adjusted HICP data. Contrary to the US CPI, the HICP does not contain an estimate of the costs of owner-occupied housing. Core inflation (that is, inflation excluding food and energy) gives a better indication of the underlying trend than headline inflation. According to the ECB’s goal of price stability, annual increases in harmonised consumer prices should stay below but close to 2% over the medium term. For future ECB policy, however, the price outlook is more important than the contemporaneous inflation rate.

Consumer price data are aggregated using harmonised national data. The country weights in the HICP are based on share of private consumption expenditure in total euro-zone spending. Eurostat releases a preliminary flash estimate around the 30th day of the month under observation at 10:00 am. This estimate is based on CPI data from Germany, Italy, Spain and Belgium, and on energy price information across Euroland. 

## **What to make of the German state CPIs** 

As the first inflation data to be reported in the euro area, the German regional state CPIs receive considerable attention. Together with the Italian, Spanish and pan-German flash HICP data, which come out shortly afterwards, they account for almost two-thirds of the euro-area aggregate. In addition, the next important data point, the French CPI data, is usually released only about two weeks later. Fortunately, it is not necessary to look at all six German regional CPI reports in detail. The numbers to focus on are the monthly increases in the CPI in the bigger constituencies. Applying the average monthly increase to the national CPI shows what the figures imply for CPI at the national level. In Germany, the importance of the regional states should be (in descending order): North-Rhine Westphalia, Baden-Wuerttemberg, Bavaria and Hesse. The two eastern states of Saxony and Brandenburg are of less importance because of their small size and a different basket underlying the CPI. Note that in some cases the HICP dynamics are characterised by different base effects or rounding errors. In these cases, HICP and CPI inflation can diverge. German, Italian and Spanish HICP data form much of the basis of the preliminary flash estimate of the EMU HICP, which is released shortly after the national data.

The final report, which takes into account the remaining countries, is usually released on the 18th of the month following the month under observation, at 10:00 am. The release dates are confirmed a week in advance by Eurostat. 

## **Producer Price Index** 

The index of industrial producer prices measures the selling prices at the factory gate of industrial goods that are sold on the domestic market. Producer prices are one of the indicators the ECB looks at for the assessment of future price developments. Producer prices are not a key indicator as, in contrast to the US, the CPI data for the respective month under observation are released beforehand. However, the sub-indices give valuable indications on pricing power and inflation risks further down the pipeline. The data are released by Eurostat at 10:00 am London time on the 6th of each month, with the release date announced a week in advance. Data releases have a time lag of five to six weeks and are preliminary, as past data are often substantially revised. 

The series is broken down into energy, intermediate, capital, durable consumer and non-durable consumer goods. The sub-indices on consumer goods give only a rough indication of future price developments at the consumer level. The index of intermediate producer prices provides information on manufacturing input prices. Differences in the movements of intermediate and final output prices give an indication of producers’ pricing power. The index consists of industrial output prices, based on national data that are not fully harmonised yet. Discrepancies among countries therefore tend to be greater than with consumer prices. 

## **Other price indicators** 

**GDP deflator.** One additional very broad measure of price developments in the euro area is the GDP deflator. It measures the average price increase in all domestically produced goods and services. During periods of big swings in imported energy prices, the GDP deflator can deviate substantially from the CPI 

and is often regarded as an economically well-founded measure of ‘core inflation’. Indeed, the GDP deflator change combines changes in unit labour costs and profit margins. However, since the nominal GDP data for the euro area get released only with a time lag after the real GDP data, the GDP deflator is released only with a considerable time lag. 

**Hourly labour costs.** Eurostat publishes an index of harmonised hourly labour costs in the euro area that comprises wages and salaries (including bonuses, etc.), as well as employers’ social security contributions. The data are published on a quarterly basis and are difficult to interpret because they tend to vary substantially with the number of working days in the quarter under observation. We therefore prefer to look at unit labour costs to gauge inflationary pressures stemming from wages. 

**Unit labour costs.** Euro-area unit labour costs, which are still not fully harmonised across countries, are calculated by the ECB and published in its Monthly Bulletin. Unit labour cost developments are driven by two factors: compensation per employee and labour productivity (that is, output per person employed). Note that labour productivity varies substantially in the course of the business cycle as output usually expands and contracts faster than payrolls. Notwithstanding this caveat, unit labour costs, together with profit margins, are often thought to be the most important domestic determinants of inflation in the medium term. 

**HWWA index of raw material prices.** The index of raw material prices calculated by the HWWA (Institute fuer Wirtschaftsforschung, Hamburg) on a daily basis includes more than 30 different commodities, including energy, imported by Germany. In the absence of a euro-area index of import prices, the ECB uses this as a proxy. Energy accounts for about 60% of the total index. The index is available in US dollar terms and in euro terms. The latter makes it very convenient to use.

## **Economic Activity Indicators — How Do They Tally?** 

Before we start tackling the specifics of each country’s statistics, here is some background on how the main economic activity indicators fit together. 

## **GDP — the core concept** 

The broadest measure of economic activity is Gross Domestic Product (GDP). Because it paints the most comprehensive picture of an economy, GDP provides the _single_ measure that encapsulates what we want to know about how activity is and how much the economy can deliver. The integrating framework into which nearly all economic indicators can be fitted is given in the national accounts (ESA 1995, consistent with SNA 1993, which will soon become SNA 2008). 

## **Several faces to the same coin** 

There are several ways of looking at GDP that correspond to different ways of estimating its size. One is to look at it as the total _output_ of goods and services. This means the total ‘value added,’ or what each producer adds to its inputs. This is the output measure of GDP. On this measure, GDP splits into three broad categories: industrial, construction and services value added (Exhibit 4, left-hand-side box). 

Another way to estimate GDP is to look at the _expenditure_ on total output (excluding spending by residents on imports and including spending by foreigners on exports). This is the expenditure measure of GDP (Exhibit 4, right-hand-side box). On this approach, GDP breaks down into consumer spending, government spending, investment, inventories and net trade (that is, exports minus imports). 

A third way to estimate GDP is to aggregate all institutional sectors’ _income_ (households, companies, government, external sector). Ultimately, these three different measures genuinely converge as statisticians accumulate in-depth information on the domestic economy and the balance of payments. 

## **Expenditure side in the US, but output side in Europe** 

The first GDP estimates mostly favour one of these approaches. In the US, statisticians rely on the expenditure side, while Europeans prefer the output side. 

## **Disentangling volumes from prices** 

‘Real’ GDP is nominal GDP deflated by a price index, or GDP deflator. Until recently, statisticians built GDP deflators by aggregating individual price indices using weights derived from the breakdown of value added for a given year. Recently, they have moved toward chained indices, i.e., using a rolling 

reference period, in order to limit any bias resulting from rapidly changing production structures. The flip-side of this improved methodology is that computing contributions to GDP is trickier, although differences are very small as long as growth rates are low. 

## **How to interpret it? Not too hot, not too cold** 

A ‘strong’ rate of GDP growth — and the interpretation of strong will differ among countries — is good news inasmuch as it means, other things equal, higher profits and stronger employment. However, because excessive growth puts pressure on capacity — which does not necessarily expand as quickly — it represents an inflationary risk. So, the growth rate of GDP should be strong enough to create wealth, but not so strong as to disturb price stability. 

## **The ‘problem’ — GDP data released with a long time lag** 

GDP data are released quarterly. Because of the size of the information it encompasses, GDP is a late indicator. In the best of cases (the UK), a ‘flash’ estimate is released a month or so after the end of each quarter. But, usually, it will be published six to ten weeks after the end of the period under review. This, admittedly, is late for analytical purposes. 

## **The ‘solution’ — look at more timely activity indicators** 

Since GDP data lack timeliness, attention will typically focus on other data to get a picture of economic activity. These indicators fall into two broad categories: (i) hard data, such as industrial production, retail sales, trade and labour market data; and (ii) survey evidence from companies or consumers. There remains a snag, however, as none of these indicators provides a complete picture of the concept of GDP. Thus, to get an idea of overall activity, the solution is to cross-examine the pieces of information that each indicator provides. 

## **Several channels** 

The main relationships among the different components of GDP and the various economic indicators (hard economic data, as well as surveys) are illustrated in Exhibit 4. Note that, for simplicity, all the reasoning is done assuming all other things are equal. For instance, traditional surveys will gauge manufacturers’ opinions on current and future levels of output growth, inventories, etc., which helps form an opinion on production. On the expenditure side, let us assume that labour market data recorded a rise in employment. Inasmuch as it implies stronger income for the household sector, this will mean stronger sales and stronger consumer spending. Higher household income also means more tax receipts, which should influence the government balance.

## **Which Macro Indicators Move the Market?** 

Elga Bartsch _(Elga.Bartsch@morganstanley.com)_ 

A number of academic studies try to assess the typical market impact of unexpected outcomes of macroeconomic announcements using intra-day data on bond yields, exchange rates and money market rates. Here we provide a quick overview of their main findings. Generally, these studies conclude that unexpectedly weak or strong US announcements have a larger impact on financial markets than European data (see Exhibit 5). Apart from the relative size of the financial markets and the economies, a key factor in explaining this phenomenon is that some US economic indicators are released ahead of their European counterparts. However, our roadmap to US and European macroeconomic indicators shows that this isn’t the case for all of them (see page 38). 

In some cases, the perceived irrelevance of European data could stem from the time window during which the market reaction is studied. In addition, a number of studies neglect indicators such as the German Ifo survey, the German regional state CPIs or German unemployment, which we consider key indicators for data-watchers and market participants alike. Another element blurring the potential market impact is that some of the German data were often leaked before the official release. 

Looking at four recent studies on the market reaction to macro announcements, we ranked the different indicators based on how often they were among the top 25% potential market movers in the respective market (see Exhibit 5). We looked at this ranking in absolute terms (how often a given indicator came out in the top 25%) and in relative terms by taking into account that not all indicators were considered in all the studies. Based on the latter metric, the US labour market report comes out on top. Consumer confidence (both Michigan and Conference Board) follows. The ISM survey has the same relevance for markets as the advance US GDP estimate. The German Ifo survey ranks slightly ahead of M3 money supply and US retail sales. 

## **Bond market impact** 

According to a study by the New York Fed, the biggest moves in bond yields on both sides of the Atlantic in recent years have come from surprises on US payrolls or unemployment (including claims) as well as the advance estimate of US GDP and consumer sentiment. ## **Manufacturing watch** 

_Getting manufacturing production numbers right is key for two central reasons. First, even though manufacturing output accounts for less than 24% of Euroland GDP, it explains most (over 50%) of its quarterly volatility. Second, when deciding its monetary policy stance, the ECB has a picture in mind of expected activity in Euroland._ 

## **Industrial production** 

Between the 15th and 20th of the month following the month under review, Eurostat releases industrial production data for the euro zone and EU-27 and individual countries. Headlines are available at 10:00 am London time. Almost instantaneously, aggregate data (for the euro zone and the EU-27) and individual country data are available on the Eurostat webpage. The reference period is a calendar month. Eurostat publishes the monthly (MoM) seasonally adjusted and the annual (YoY) workday-adjusted growth rates for the euro zone and EU-27 and individual countries. For the euro zone and EU-27, Eurostat further publishes a breakdown by industries: intermediate goods, capital goods, durable consumer goods, and non-durable consumer goods. 

Industrial production is a monthly indicator of industrial activity that comes ahead of GDP and thus provides a first-cut indicator of GDP growth in the same quarter. Moreover, while it accounts for only 25% of GDP, it explains half of its volatility. However, the reliability of the Eurostat series as a tool for business cycle analysis is questionable. Indeed, data are often revised and by a significant amount. When looking at industrial production data, it is useful to look at the QoQ change in order to extract information about the underlying trend.

## **Unemployment** 

Eurostat releases monthly euro-zone unemployment data generally at the end of the month following the month under review. Data are released at 10:00 am London time and are available on Eurostat’s website. The statistical office provides seasonally adjusted unemployment rates for the euro zone and EU-27 and individual states consistent with the ILO (International Labour Office) definition of unemployment[1] . National unemployment rates reported by Eurostat are based on labour force surveys and thus may differ from those shown in some national sources, which are often based on claimant counts. Since national data from large countries such as Germany and France are released before EU data, this release does not tend to move markets. However, markets are sensitive to the trend in EMU unemployment data, which are less volatile than country data. 

## **Employment** 

Employment is released only on a quarterly basis and at an individual country level. This release does not have the same importance as the US non-farm payrolls release. We therefore focus on the ECB employment data for the euro area released as part of the National Accounts. 

## **Retail sales** 

Eurostat releases preliminary data on retail sales in the first week of the second month following the month under review. Data are released at 10:00 am London time and made available on the Eurostat press release web page. The retail sales index is a business cycle indicator that measures the monthly activity of the retail sector in volume terms. It is a short-term indicator of final domestic demand and accounts for 40% of total private consumption. Retail sales exclude purchases of motor vehicles and repair of personal and household goods. While production data provide information on trends in volume concerning value added at factor cost (supply side), turnover data are used to assess current developments in sales (demand side). Eurostat releases the MoM and YoY growth rates in retail trade for the EU-16 and EU-27 and MoM growth rates for individual countries.

## **Gross domestic product** 

Eurostat releases a flash GDP estimate in the first two weeks of the second month following the end of the quarter under review. Data are released at 10:00 am London time. The flash estimate is based on flash estimates from the largest EMU members and does not provide any demand breakdown. ## **The Business Cycle Compass** 

## **The manufacturing production indicator** 

We are able to estimate the quarterly growth rate of manufacturing production (MP) for the current quarter and predict the next quarter thanks to a two-stage process. For the current period, the quarterly growth rate of MP is a function of a polynomial of lags of two components of manufacturing surveys: companies’ judgment on current production rate and on demand. The same econometric model is used for the next quarter, with assessment on demand, current production and expected production being extrapolated thanks to a vector autoregressive model. (See _A New Indicator for Euroland Manufacturing Production_ , March 1, 2005.) Next to our standard model, we also estimated a non-linear model to better capture the extreme swings during the crisis.  The non-linear model includes the deviation of the components from their long-term average explicity. 

## **The Surprise Gap index** 

Every month, companies provide a qualitative assessment of their production plans for the next three months. Three months later, the same companies provide a qualitative assessment of their actual production. By comparing the latter to the former on normalised time series, we measure the amount of surprises, good or bad, faced by producers. We have shown that this index is a reliable measure of the probability of production accelerating (Surprise Gap above 0.26) or decelerating (above 0.28). 

Pooling together the manufacturing production indicator and the Surprise Gap index, we have a two-dimensional indicator that describes both the momentum and the probability of acceleration or deceleration of the core manufacturing sector. The Compass typically visits nine different states of the economy: turning point (up), moving out of recession, strong and accelerating, strong and steady, turning point (down), risk of recession, recession deepening, recession continuing and the grey zone. ( _See A Compass for the Cycle_ , July 27, 2005.)

## **Some modelling issues** 

There are many tools in an economists’ toolbox. While some of the models are useful to investigate certain questions, none of them can describe all the relevant economic relationships in all instances. Among the various modelling approaches we use or review, the main ones are: 

- **Macroeconometric models:** These are mainly used for policy analysis. In general, they are sufficiently detailed to give a reasonable account of the bahaviour of the main macroeconomic variables in a given country or region. These models assume a set of long-run structural relationships. The parameters of those relationships and their short-run dynamics are estimated on the basis of past experience. 

- **Small-scale econometric models:** These are aggregated models with varying theoretical content that provide a stylised representation of one or a few aspects of the economy. For example, the so-called Taylor rules – which stipulate how much the central bank would or should change nominal interest rates in response to divergences of actual inflation rates from target inflation rates and of actual GDP from potential GDP – belong to this category. 

- **Phillips curve models:** These relate nominal price or wage inflation to some measure of excess demand or real disequilibrium, conventionally measured by either an unemployment or an output ‘gap’. Being fairly simple and transparent, they are used to cross-check the forecast output from the macroeconometric models, or to conduct pilot studies for more thorough analysis using more elaborate models. 

- **Vector autoregression models:** These are systems of equations that examine the interrelationships between economic variables, using minimal assumptions about the underlying structure of the economy. They aim to provide good statistical representations of the past interactions between variables. They are used for forecasting and as a tool to analyse the effects of various shocks. 

- **Optimising models:** These capture the interactions between the different types of agents in the economy (consumers, firms, government, foreign sector), each 

of which is assumed to solve well-defined dynamic optimisation problems, subject to certain informational and technological constraints. They can be used to examine likely explanations for observed patterns of behaviour in the data. 

The upshot is that, for the same shock – say, a 10% depreciation of the euro – different models will likely yield a different response, depending on the underlying theoretical assumptions, the estimation method and the time horizon used for the calculations, among other things. 

Bearing in mind that the types of shock and the variables involved vary somewhat, below we show the response of popular models to various shocks – and our own estimates. 

- **Monetary policy shock** – A 1ppt cut in the policy rate would raise euro-area GDP between 0.1% and 0.2% the first year and between 0.2% and 0.5% the second – relative to the baseline. Inflation would increase by 0.1% at most the first year and between 0.2% and 0.4% the second. A drop in short-term _and_ long-term interest rates by 1ppt would raise euro area GDP by 0.9% after one year; inflation would rise by approximately 0.6%. 

- **Fiscal policy shock** – A cut in government spending of 1% of GDP would reduce euro-area GDP between 0.9% and 1.3% the first year and between 0.5% and 1.6% the second. Inflation would decline by 0.2% at most the first year and between 0.4% and 0.6% the second. The impact can differ depending on whether the shock has to do with changes in government spending or in the tax regime. 

- **Exchange rate shock** – A 10% depreciation in the trade-weighted exchange rate of the euro would raise euro-area GDP between 0.4% and 0.8% the first year and between 0.6% and 0.9% the second. Depending on whether consumer prices or the GDP deflator are used, inflation would increase between 0.4% and 0.8% the first year and between 0.6% and 0.9% the second. 

- **Oil shock** – A 10% increase in the price of oil would reduce euro-area GDP by around 0.02% the first year and between 0.04% and 0.07% the second. Inflation would increase between 0.08% and 0.2% the first

## **Some caveats** 

There are at least three important caveats with all the above-mentioned responses from the various models: 

- **Non-linear affects.** The response of the models is invariant relative to the baseline scenario; that is, it is the same whatever the starting point. This is plausible within a reasonable range. But in the real world the relationship between GDP growth and consumer inflation, on the one hand, and, say, oil prices, on the other hand, becomes non-linear beyond certain limits. Hence, the impact of an oil shock is not proportional to the magnitude of the shock: a given percentage increase would have a stronger effect on GDP growth and consumer inflation if applied to a higher oil price.

## **European System of Central Banks (ESCB)** 

The ESCB comprises the ECB and the national central banks (NCBs) of all EU Member States whether they have adopted the euro or not.  Meanwhile, the Eurosystem comprises the ECB and the national central banks (NCBs) of those countries that have adopted the euro.  Since 1 January 1999, the European Central Bank (ECB) has been responsible for conducting monetary policy for the euro area. In all operational matters relating to monetary policy – including foreign exchange interventions or bond purchases for monetary policy purposes – the NCBs act on instructions from the ECB.  The legal basis for the single monetary policy is the Treaty establishing the European Community and the Statute of the European System of Central Banks and of the European Central Bank. Except for the UK and Denmark, who negotiated an opt-out clause, all other countries are obliged to join the euro once they fulfill the convergence criteria. 

## **European Council** 

The Council of Europe comprises the heads of state or government of all 27 European Union member states, next to its President Herman van Rompuy, and the President of the European Commission José Manuel Barroso. It has no formal legislative power. Yet, it is where the political decisions are taken during European Summits because it brings together the executive powers of all member states. The presidency rotates on a six-month basis between different EU member states. The Council decides by qualified majority voting on most issues, except on the following fields, amongst others, taxation, harmonisation of social security, certain parts of justice and home affairs, the common foreign, security, and defense policy, and the finances of the EU, EU membership. 

## **ECOFIN** 

Economic and Financial Affairs Council (ECOFIN) brings together the finance ministers of the European Union.  The ministers meet at least once a month (typically on the Tuesday before the ECB Council meeting).  The main focus of the discussions is the coordination of fiscal, financial and economic policy initiatives.  The peer review of the fiscal policy stance under the Stability and Growth Pact (SGP) and the Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP) is another key aspect of its work.  The ECOFIN has become the key forum in formulating the European policy response to the sovereign crisis (e.g., the EU stabilization fund). 

## **Eurogroup** 

This is an informal get together of the 16 euro-area finance ministers ahead of the Ecofin (usually the Monday evening before the Ecofin meeting itself). JC Juncker, the president of the euro group (until June 2012), usually holds a press conference after the euro group meeting – often together with the Commissioner for Economic and Financial Affairs Olli Rehn. The Lisbon Treaty clarified that within the Ecofin only euro-zone member states can vote on matters that only concern the euro zone. 

## **Economic and Financial Committee** 

The Economic and Financial Committee (EFC) has been set up to provide support to the work of the ECOFIN.  Its work comprises economic analysis, formulation of broad economic policy guidelines, and contribution to macroeconomic surveillance.  The EPC comprises two senior officials from each member state, the European Commission and the ECB. The proceedings of the EPC are confidential.  Votes by majority (one member one vote, the Commission and ECB abstain). 

## **European Commission** 

The European Commission is the executive body of the European Union.  It is responsible for initiating legislation and enforcing EU Treaties (e.g., in the area of the Single Market). The Commission consists of a college of 27 Commissioners (one for each member state) who together form a cabinet. The Commission President (currently José Manuel Barroso) and the Commissioners are appointed by the European Council. All 27 Commissioners are subject to the approval of the European Parliament as a single body.  The Commission currently has about 25,000 European civil servants, who are split into departments called Directorates-General and Services, is based in Brussels.

## **The Stability and Growth Pact (SGP)** 

The Stability and Growth Pact (SGP) is a rule-based framework for the coordination of national fiscal policies with the monetary union. It was established to safeguard sound public finances. The Pact consists of a preventive and a corrective arm (see Exhibit 15). 

Under the provisions of the **preventive arm** , Member States have to submit annual stability or convergence programmes, showing how they intend to ensure sound fiscal positions in the medium term. The Commission then assesses these programmes and the Ecofin gives an opinion on them. Ecofin can, on a proposal by the Commission, issue an early warning to prevent the occurrence of an excessive deficit. The EU’s budget surveillance – the preventive arm of the SGP – is essentially a peer review process of the fiscal policy stance of the individual countries. It is only binding for the euro area countries though. With the current sovereign debt crisis, it is clear that the SGP has failed to prevent the budget deficit from ballooning and the debt level from climbing ever higher. 

## **European Parliament**

The European Commission is also accountable to the EP. The two largest groups are the European People's Party (EPP) and the Progressive Alliance of Socialists and Democrats (S&D). The Economic and Monetary Affairs Committee holds regular hearings with the members of the ECB Executive Board.

## **Ifo business climate** 

The pan-German Ifo business climate is one of the most important, if not the most important, cyclical indicator for the euro zone. The headline business sentiment correlates closely with quarterly German GDP growth. After the Belgian BNB survey, it is usually the first traditional business survey released in the euro zone for a given period of observation. It is released on a fixed date, around the 28th of the month under observation, by the Ifo Institute, one of Germany’s public research institutes. The widely watched headline business sentiment is released at 9:00 am London time on all newswires. Advance copies of the details are available later in the afternoon for a small set of subscribers. The publication of the details is usually embargoed for another week. While headline business sentiment is an index (2000 = 100), with a long-term average of 95.0, the details are diffusion indices — that is, the net balance of positive and negative answers, weighed according to the size of respondent companies. 

Data are seasonally adjusted, but not workday-adjusted. Headline business sentiment comprises business expectations over the next six months and assessments of current business conditions in the manufacturing, construction, retail and wholesale sectors. Business expectations typically have a lead of a few months over the assessment of current conditions. Within the details, the main focus is on the manufacturing sector. The key indicators for the sector are output plans and export expectations for the next three months, as well as the assessment of demand and of inventories of finished goods. At the end of each quarter, the Ifo also releases an estimate of the rate of capacity utilisation in manufacturing. 

## **ZEW business expectations index** 

Unlike the Ifo survey, which is a direct poll of company captains, the ZEW index is based on a poll of financial market 

participants (around 320 institutional investors and analysts). The index is watched closely by markets as it is the first information about business sentiment in a given month and, in addition, has been found to correlate closely with the business expectations component of the Ifo survey. The ZEW index is released on a fixed date, usually around the 15th of the month, and thus more than 10 days ahead of the Ifo survey. 

## **Unemployment** 

The headline measure of unemployment is the seasonally adjusted series of absolute changes. The labour market report is important, as it is the first hard economic data for any given period, released before the end of the month under observation. There are unemployment rates for eastern and western Germany. The western German series is the main measure for cyclical developments, as structural unemployment in eastern Germany is very high and is heavily dependent on the very volatile construction sector. Furthermore, vocational training measures and employment schemes distort the data in eastern Germany. 

The national definition used for the unemployment rate is wider than the ILO definition, leading to a higher rate (by 1.5-2 percentage points) than that calculated by the ILO. The unemployment rate conforming to the ILO definition is released with a one-month time lag. The monthly series has a fixed release date, around the 30th day of the month under observation, at 8:55 am London time on all newswires. Non-seasonally adjusted data and seasonally adjusted data on people enrolled in government programmes are released by the Federal Labour Office, with more seasonally adjusted data released shortly afterwards by the Bundesbank. Finally, watch out for leaks starting at least a day before the official release. These leaks, which are usually on the non-seasonally adjusted unemployment data, are often difficult to interpret as the seasonal adjustment factors change from year to year.

## **Consumer Price Index** 

The **preliminary CPI** is important as it is the first CPI data on the month in observation in the euro zone. Details of regional state CPIs also provide first insights into the factors driving inflation, as well as the trend of core inflation. There is no fixed release date, but the data are usually released around the 22nd of the month under observation. The preliminary release schedule is usually announced a few days in advance, and the actual release times for the separate regional states are typically announced on the day 30 minutes in advance. Once six regional states have published their data, the preliminary non-seasonally adjusted pan-German index (CPI and HICP definition), based on these data, is released by the Federal Statistics Office. The weighting scheme used by the Federal Statistics Office to aggregate the regional state data is based on private consumption weights, with NorthRhine-Westphalia (23%), Bavaria (16%) and Baden-Wuerttemberg (14%) carrying the largest weights. Similar to the Italian city CPIs, the focus is on the weighted average of monthly increases in the regional states, which are applied to the pan-German index to derive the annual inflation rate for the whole of Germany. 

The **final CPI** is released about two weeks later. The final CPI should not contain any major news, unless there are sizeable revisions of the preliminary data. It also has a more detailed sector breakdown. The non-seasonally adjusted series is published by the Federal Statistics Office (at 7:00 am London time), while the seasonally adjusted series is published by the Bundesbank about an hour later. 

## **Industrial production** 

Industrial output is an important factor in determining the value added in the industrial sector and is therefore important for the current quarter’s GDP forecast. There is a fixed release date, around the 10th of the month, but the release time is uncertain. Typically the data are released at 11:00 am London time. The industrial production data have a time lag of six weeks and are preliminary. They are revised when the final data are reported together with the subsequent release. Data in the press release by the Economics Ministry are broken down into sectors: manufacturing (82%), construction (9.5%) and energy/mining (8.6%). Output in both construction and energy is very volatile and dependent on the weather. 

## **Manufacturing orders** 

Manufacturing orders are very volatile due to the effect of large orders. Therefore, one should not over-interpret the monthly changes, but rather look at the 3m/3m trend. Total orders give an indication of future developments in the manufacturing sector as a whole, whereas sub-sector domestic orders in capital and consumer goods give an indication of domestic 

demand. Orders and sales correlate highly with manufacturing output, which is released one or two days later. There is a fixed release date, around the 8th, at 11:00 am London time. The data released have a time lag of one month and are preliminary. Past data are still sometimes revised substantially. The data in the press release by the Economics Ministry are broken down into domestic and foreign orders, and further by sectors (intermediate, capital, consumer goods). Foreign orders are further broken down into EMU and extra-EMU orders. 

## **Retail sales** 

While retail sales account for only about one-quarter of consumer spending, they are an important factor for estimating private consumer growth in the current quarter. The non-seasonally, non-calendar-adjusted headline number published by the Federal Statistics Office is significantly influenced by differences in the number of shopping days and strong seasonal effects (Christmas shopping, for instance). The data have a fixed release date, around the 1st of the month, with the exact date confirmed the Friday before in the weekly calendar of the Federal Statistics Office. The data come with a time lag of one month. 

## **GDP** 

There are two sets of GDP data: the widely watched quarterly GDP report and the annual report. While the former conveys important information on the business cycle, the latter contains information on the general government budget deficit (also known as the Maastricht deficit). Quarterly GDP data are published jointly by the Federal Statistics Office and the Bundesbank, with the latter data being more conclusive because they provide substantial detail in seasonally adjusted terms. It is worth bearing in mind that the year-on-year headline figures published by the Federal Statistics Office are not calendar-adjusted and hence can vary substantially according to the number of working days in the quarter under observation. The first report, which is only a flash estimate, is not very comprehensive as it contains no details on the composition of GDP growth. Only the second report, about 10 days later, includes the expenditure side in both nominal and real terms, a breakdown of the production side by sectors, and data on household disposable income.

## **Five hazards of German data-watching** 

1. **Looking at annual retail sales growth based on the non-seasonally adjusted data published by the Federal Statistics Office.** Because the growth rates are influenced considerably by the number of shopping days, they can be misleading. The seasonally and calendar-adjusted Bundesbank data that also comprise car sales are a better gauge. 

2. **Taking the leaks on German unemployment at face value.** The leaked numbers are usually not adjusted for seasonal variations. As seasonal labour market effects are sizeable, the non-seasonally adjusted data can be misleading. Even though leaks have become a lot less frequent, always have the seasonal adjustment factor and a pocket calculator handy! 

3. **Two potential pitfalls loom in the regional CPI data.** First, the annual inflation in the regional states can be subject to different base effects from the national index. It is therefore important to look at the monthly change. Second, bear in mind that monthly changes in regional data are non-seasonally adjusted and thus have to be compared with your forecast of the non-seasonally adjusted pan-German index of consumer prices. 

4. **Thinking that the trade balance data have** 

- **straightforward implications for the growth contribution of net exports.** Because trade data are subject to considerable seasonal effects, one should look at the seasonally adjusted monthly changes that are reported in the press release by the Federal Statistics Office and later by the Bundesbank. However, these are still relatively volatile and not adjusted for price changes, a factor that is decisive, especially for import volumes. 

5. **Spending a lot of time pondering wholesale prices.** Leaving aside the potential danger of mixing up the wholesale price series with the wholesale sales series, one should not pay too much attention to the WPI. The WPI is very volatile because of its high energy and food content. In addition, the CPI for the month under observation has already been released.

The two key elements of the report are ‘headline’ average weekly earnings growth (which measures the change in earnings over the past three months compared to one year ago) and claimant unemployment (the claimant count). The MPC monitors earnings carefully as it yields information on wage cost pressures. Unemployment data are also included in this release, including the claimant count and the ILO unemployment rate. Changes in the claimant count measure are closely watched. The claimant count measures the number of people claiming ‘Jobseekers Allowance’ and is more timely than the ILO unemployment rate. The unemployment statistics help assess the current state of the labour market and have implications for demand and wage pressures. 

**Halifax and Nationwide house price indices.** The Nationwide Building Society publishes its measure around the end of the month under coverage. The Halifax house price index is published around the beginning of the following month. Market attention centres on the annual and monthly changes in the seasonally adjusted, all-houses, all-buyers monthly index. Inasmuch as residential buildings account for around 50% of households’ total assets, developments in house prices are

**Money Holding & Lending.** The Bank of England publishes a range of monthly statistics relating to money and lending, typically around 5 weeks after the end of the month to which the statistics relate.  The MPC’s preferred measure of money holdings is the three-month annualised growth rate in ‘M4 excluding intermediate other financial corporations’.  Money holdings and inflation have been shown to move together over the long run. On lending, the focus tends to be on the growth in M4 lending and on the seasonally adjusted net change in consumer credit.  The number of mortgage approvals is also released. However, two more timely (though less comprehensive) measures of mortgage approvals are published a week or so before this release in the BoE’s ‘Trends in Lending’ Report and by the British Bankers Association. Data on lending to individuals can give an indication of the state of consumer confidence and household finances and the state of the housing market. The release of money supply and lending data is normally also accompanied by the Bank’s monthly statistical compilation ‘Bankstats’ that includes the effective (average) mortgage rate on new and existing mortgages to households, among other data. 

**Producer prices (input & output).** The ONS publishes monthly data on producer prices (input and output prices). Input prices provide information on manufacturers’ costs. The difference in movement between input and output prices serves as a rough indication of changes in manufacturer pricing power (margins). These data may also give an indication of future price pressures. 

**Purchasing managers’ surveys.** These are surveys of the manufacturing, construction and services sectors published by Markit Economics. Typically the manufacturing report is released on the first working day of each month following the month under review, the construction report on the second day, and the services report on the third. For all PMI indices, a reading of 50 is considered consistent with stable activity, and a reading above this indicates positive growth.  The PMI indices provide some of the first economic indicators that can be used to inform our forecasts of GDP. 

**CBI industrial trends survey.** Versions of this manufacturing sector survey are published monthly and quarterly. The monthly survey is normally released towards the end of the month under 

coverage. This survey contains manufacturers’ sentiment on a number of areas, including orders and output expectations. The components are reported in the form of a ‘percentage balance of opinion’, where a reading on the total orders component of -10, for example, would indicate that 10% more respondents felt that their order books were below normal levels than above. The business expectations indicator (‘optimism regarding general business situation versus 3 months ago’), published in the quarterly survey, is a particularly useful lead indicator for the UK economy, in our view. 

**CBI distributive trades survey.** This monthly and quarterly survey is normally released towards the end of the month under coverage. It tracks sentiment on areas including sales volumes and order volumes in the retail sector. Its components are reported in the form of a ‘percentage balance of opinion’ in the same way as in the CBI industrial trends survey. The series most often reported is ‘the volume of sales compared to a year ago’, which bears a close relationship to the official measure of retail sales volumes (discussed below). 

**GfK consumer confidence.** The pollster GfK conducts a monthly survey of households on behalf of the European Commission. The ‘headline’ reading is the Consumer Confidence Barometer Index, which is based on questions concerning households’ financial situation (past and future), the general economic situation of the country (past and future), and the timeliness of major purchases. 

**Retail sales.** The ONS publishes data on retail sales volumes monthly. Market attention typically focuses on the monthly change in seasonally adjusted retail sales _volumes_ . Recently, the headline measure was adjusted to include the sale of automotive fuels (reports citing the ‘consensus’ for this release can also refer to the ex-automotive fuel measure). Given the volatility of the series, the trend is best captured in the quarterly or annual comparison. Retail sales do not include services or cars and so account for around 40% of total consumer spending.  They are, however, useful in providing an early indication of trends in overall consumer expenditure. 

**Industrial production (IP)/manufacturing output.** The ONS publishes IP data monthly. Industrial production comprises manufacturing output (80%) along with the so-called energy components. The latter can be volatile and markets typically focus on progress in the manufacturing sector. The quarterly annual growth rate is often used as a guide to the underlying trend. Interest in industrial production (as opposed to just manufacturing output) arises mainly because it accounts for around 17% of GDP. **Visible Trade Deficit.** The ONS publishes monthly estimates of both the goods and services trade deficit and of the Visible Trade Deficit (seasonally adjusted); that is, the values of goods exported less the value of those imported.  Data on goods trade _volumes_ is also published and can be used to inform forecasts for export volumes in the GDP release, though the two may differ somewhat. 

**BRC Retail.** The BRC shop price index and the BRC retail sales monitor (RSM) are published in the first and second weeks of the following month, respectively. The food price component of the shop price index can help to forecast the same component in the consumer prices index.  The RSM reports retail sales values; it represents approximately 60% of UK retail sales value and is based on sales data from a survey of retailers. 

**Public Sector Finances.** The UK has two main measures of the Government’s deficit: ‘Public Sector Net Borrowing’ (PSNB) and ‘PSNB-ex’; the ‘ex’ refers to the exclusion of the impact of financial sector interventions. The two measures are the same prior to 2007/08.  The ONS publishes monthly estimates of PSNB (not seasonally adjusted), usually in the latter half of the following month; (PSNB-ex is only published on a quarterly basis). Tracking PSNB allows markets to determine whether the Government is on course to meet its Budget projections.  The monthly release also shows the Central Government Net Cash Requirement (CGNCR), which is closer to the money that the Government needs to raise via Gilt sales (described below). 

**The Budget, the Pre-Budget and the Remits.** Each year the Government publishes a Budget and Pre-Budget that lay out the policies which they intend to implement over their 

remaining term. Both documents include the Government’s projections for Public Sector Net Borrowing (PSNB) and Public Sector Net Debt (PSND).  In recent years, the pre-Budget has been published in autumn and the Budget has been published in spring.  For financial markets, the Budget is key for assessing the health of the Government’s finances.  Usually coinciding with the Budget, the Treasury will set a ‘remit’ for the Debt Management Office that specifies the annual total of gilt sales planned for the coming year.  The remit is usually revised each November/December with the publication of the Pre-Budget Report, and it can also be revised in April when the actual borrowing numbers for the previous financial year are published. 

At the time of publishing, the new Government had recently established an Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) that will make forecasts for the economy and the public finances. 

**GDP** The ONS releases three different sets of GDP data: 1) a preliminary estimate of GDP about a month after quarter-end; 2) a second estimate about two months after quarter-end; and 3) a final GDP estimate around three months after the quarter-end. Each release contains a varying amount of information. The first release provides a breakdown of GDP by output in different industries (e.g., manufacturing, services and construction). The second release incorporates and shows new information on the expenditure and income measures of GDP. Revisions to the first GDP estimate are possible. Market attention in this release tends to focus on the breakdown of the expenditure measure of GDP into its various components (for example, government spending and household consumption). The final GDP data are a full set of national accounts. The release describes any revisions to the second GDP estimate, provides additional details on the breakdown of economic activity and includes the household savings rate and financial balance sheets of different sectors of the economy.

## **Monetary policy — main Riksbank releases** 

Monetary policy in Sweden is carried out by the Riksbank. The Riksbank conducts monetary policy within a flexible inflation forecast-targeting framework. The main objective of the Riksbank is to maintain CPI inflation at a target of 2%. In June 2010, the Riksbank removed the tolerance band of of +/-1.0 percentage point around the target. Of secondary nature, the flexibility in the inflation-targeting regime allows the Riksbank to take into account other economic variables such as asset prices and debt as well as real economic variables such as unemployment. The Riksbank’s favourite measure of inflation is the CPIF measure, which strips out the impact of interest rate changes. Next to its Monetary Policy Report and the statements accompanying its decisions, the Riksbank tends to communicate on current monetary policy through a number of speeches published during the year. 

**Monetary policy decisions and minutes from the meeting** The monetary policy decision is taken by the six-member Executive Board at approximately seven meetings per year. Each member has one vote and the central bank governor holds a casting vote in case of a tie. The decision is released at 8.30 am following the day of the meeting. On the occasions when a decision to change interest rates has been taken, a press conference is held at 10 am. The minutes are published approximately two weeks after a monetary policy meeting at 8.30 am. The release contains a summary of the discussions at the previous monetary policy meeting and provides valuable information about the current stance of the Executive Board and the future path of monetary policy. Since the votes of the individual members of the Executive Board are published with the decision itself, the minutes have become less of a focus for markets.

## **Monetary Policy Report** 

The MPR reports are now published three times per year, in February, June and October. Most valuable are the new inflation projections and whether they are above or below the 2% target on a two-year horizon. Inflation projections assume that interest rates will follow current financial market expectations as measured by the forward curve. Inflation above 2.0% on a two-year horizon would indicate that financial market interest rate expectations are too low. The inflation reports contain thematic articles on the Swedish economy and issues related to monetary policy or financial stability. The reports are published at 8.30 am, at the same time that monetary policy decisions are announced. 

## **Our ‘Repo-Meter’ gauge of the Riksbank’s policy stance** 

As part of our coverage of Sweden, we carefully try to gauge what future monetary policy path the Riksbank _will_ likely follow. This is contrary to academic institutions, where the focus is more related towards what future monetary policy path the Riksbank _should_ take. To gauge the chance and timing of future rate hikes, we have developed a quantitative model called the ‘Repo-Meter’ (see _Reviewing the Repo-Meter and the Riksbank_ , January 13, 2006). The Repo-Meter consists of so-called Probit models, which give an estimate of the probability of a repo rate adjustment at the upcoming meeting (see Exhibit 21). Due to what seems to be asymmetric behaviour by the Riksbank, we use one version for gauging rate cuts and another one for judging rate hikes. The latter one comes in two flavours: one that is based on economic variables such as consumer confidence, business sentiment and inflation, and another that also includes the exchange rate. The Repo-Meter has historically been a good predictor for the timing of future Riksbank moves. The model should always be used as a complement to the direct communication from the Riksbank.
