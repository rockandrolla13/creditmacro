## **No Rush for the Exits** 

Despite the intense bout of repricing in EM assets this past summer, it appears that investors are still finding the EM asset complex as an attractive destination for their portfolios. We should say _institutional_ investors. 

The high frequency and ready availability of retail-oriented fund flows data imply that there has been a rush to the exits from EM; however, other – arguably more representative – evidence suggests that this contention is broadly misplaced. 

Balance of payments data do show a decline in net capital flows to EM economies, in 2Q13 from 1Q13 – including portfolio flows, especially equities. In fact, on aggregate, we saw net portfolio outflows from EM equity in 2Q13 in stark contrast to 1Q13, with AXJ bearing most of the brunt. Debt portfolio inflows into EM saw a significant decline as well, falling by nearly 29% from the prior quarter, with inflows into AXJ again more than halved. 

Even so, we did see a US$26 billion net portfolio inflow in 2Q13. Yes, this was down on the net portfolio inflow of US$42 billion in 1Q13; but on a 4Q-sum rolling basis, net portfolio flows to EM were also US$26 billion – particularly comforting considering 2Q12 saw a net portfolio outflow (see Exhibit 1).

That said, taking a broader perspective, a decline in net capital flows to EM is a trend that has been in place since 2011, a time in which macro stresses and market underperformance became more evident for EM and, incidentally, a time also marked by a steady decline in core market yields and extraordinary monetary accommodation by G4 central banks. 

The steady decline in capital flows since 2011 argues our point again that, beyond concerns over the implications of Fed tapering of asset purchases on the demand for EM assets, EM has been adversely impacted by a steady deterioration in macro fundamentals – something that for markets wasn’t offset by ongoing G4 monetary easing.

It’s not that retail-oriented fund flows don’t matter; for retailoriented funds, to the extent that they have not done so already in anticipation of redemptions, portfolio managers are impelled to sell assets in order to raise cash to meet these retail redemptions. Depending on prevailing cash balances, this certainly puts downward pressure on markets. 

Yet, not all EM asset managers are retail investor-oriented; we estimate that dedicated EM bond funds are split broadly evenly between retail orientation and institutional orientation. Those that are institutional in orientation are not in a position to have to forcibly sell assets, to the extent that their investors remain committed to investing in the asset class – which as we discuss later appears to be the case. 

Moreover, we believe that institutional flows matter more. For one, we believe that they have accounted for the majority of portfolio inflows to EM in recent years.

So again, no signs of a broad-based rush for the exits. 

Based on available data, completed third-party manager searches have accounted for US$220 million per month, on average, in new money mandates to EM debt since 2010 – with each individual ticket averaging US$80 million. However, these figures considerably understate the flows emanating from long-term institutional investors, given that they don’t include ongoing investment once the search is complete. 

Second, we gauge institutional interest in EM via third-party manager searches (which end up as new money mandates). 

Searches are a gauge of institutional investor interest in allocating to an asset class, via the hiring of a third-party manager. And as far as searches for EM are concerned, both debt and equity have been on a steep gradient upwards – and 2013 has seen a continuation of this trend (see Exhibit 6). 

Certainly, the size of relative investment pools would argue in support of this contention. Long-term institutional investor AUM – pension/endowment, insurance and SWFs – is upwards of US$125 trillion, whereas private sector AUM is upwards of US$40 trillion, with most of this tied in cashequivalents rather than duration, spread product or equities.

To be sure, we are likely to experience additional search activity in coming months, not captured in our pipeline estimates. That said, further buttressing our hypothesis is that a decrease in searches into 2Q14, leading to a quite possible overall decrease in EM inflows, could serve to push EM yields higher and EM credit spreads wider around that time, consistent with our expectation of asset re-pricing. 

Longer out, if investor concerns about the sustainability of EM growth (relative to DM) become more acute, we may see a reassessment of the reward/risk in continuing to allocate towards EM, even among longer-term investors. 

Two likely implications: i) Long-term investors allocate less to broad EM mandates (i.e., broad indices) and have more specialised, focused exposures to specific countries, markets or sectors; or ii) They just allocate less to EM. 

Irrespective of prevailing attitudes and plans towards EM allocation, to the extent that macro challenges continue to assert themselves in EM and are not reflected in asset prices – as is our current view – it is likely in coming months that we see a bit of both. 

The prospect of more specialised mandates resonates with us as we see an increasingly bifurcated market. Countries such as Brazil, India, Indonesia, Turkey and South Africa – and increasingly Russia – may be perceived as more vulnerable and require a higher hurdle for investing in terms of risk premia, relative to the broader market. 

But as these investors are more deliberate in their allocation strategies, we believe that it will take time.
