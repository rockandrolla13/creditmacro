## A High Yield Hedge 

We exited our bullish tactical call on credit at the end of February and none of our macro concerns have changed since then. We think buying protection on short-dated HY CDX is a good way to position for rising defaults and default expectations over the next 2 years, a risk that is not in the price. 

**Differing trends across credit:** High yield went through a mini default cycle in 2016, and since then, issuance has steadily declined, leading to no growth in par outstanding, very different from the trends observed elsewhere in credit. As a result, some argue that high yield is more insulated from the fundamental challenges in other pockets of credit markets. In our view, HY is and will remain highly sensitive to changing growth expectations as well as to changes in credit conditions. 

**HY is not out of the woods:** First, the HY market is still much larger than it was in 2008, but all the growth happened in the first 6 years of this cycle. Second, while some fundamental metrics don’t look as extreme in HY, certain "qualitative" measures are arguably more problematic, such as higher exposure to sectors with long-term operational challenges. Third, because some of the fundamental issues are more widely discussed in other markets, they are likely also a bit more in the price. For example, the basis between BBs and BBBs is at cycle tights. Finally, don't forget HY spreads widened sharply in all 3 growth scares in this cycle (2011, 2016, and 4Q18). 

**Short HY S25 as a default hedge:** With spreads at very tight levels and curves still steep, the front end of the CDX HY market is priced for very few defaults. Shorting CDX HY25 to expiry would make money if the index sees just 4 defaults over the next ~2 years (assuming a 30% recovery). A default rate this low is unusual, even in a benign environment. Given our view on the credit cycle, we do not think it is unreasonable to assume a more notable rise in defaults looking out into 2020. 

**Short HY S25 as a risk premium trade:** On top of hedging against actual defaults, short-dated HY CDX also works as a positively convex way to position for a large sell-off in credit, where the market brings default risks forward. Looking back at prior sell-offs, credit curves have flattened meaningfully. Additionally, the trade has a lower holding cost than the 5Y index and is more efficient for investors looking to “lock-in” protection, without the headwind of roll-down and MTM volatility for more modest moves.

## Introduction 

We exited our bullish tactical call on credit at the end of February and stick with our cautious view. In addition to the recommendations we made at the time, we also now like buying protection on short-dated HY CDX, which we walk through in more detail below. Our broader views on the macro backdrop, earnings headwinds and the credit cycle have not changed from what we laid out in US Corporate Credit Strategy Brief: Selling the Rally (28 Feb 2019); hence, we will set aside our bigger picture view in this note, and focus on the high yield recommendation mentioned above. 

## High Yield Headwinds 

Trends in the high yield market over the past few years, in particular, have been somewhat different from what we have seen elsewhere. For example, when thinking about the excesses in credit, many (us included) talk about BBBs in IG, which have seen enormous growth in this cycle, or the leverage loan market, where credit quality has arguably deteriorated for years (higher leverage levels, weaker covenants, weaker structures, etc.). But the high yield market is often left out of the discussion. After all, high yield went through a mini default cycle in 2016, centered on Energy, and since then, issuance has steadily declined, leading to no growth in par outstanding, very different from the trends noted above. Some investors assume that as a result, high yield is more insulated from the fundamental risks present in other pockets of credit markets. In fact, for much of 2018 (at least for the first three quarters), we regularly heard the view that the resilience of HY (relative to the weakness in IG, for example) was a testament to healthier fundamentals in the former. 

Through the third quarter of last year, we published several notes (see: US Corporate Credit Strategy Brief: I Can't Believe It's Not Beta, 25 Apr 2018) on why we thought HY was so resilient at the time (i.e., low supply and very strong earnings growth, among other factors), but more importantly, why we also believed HY was very much not immune from the broader macro challenges to come. Fundamentally, we agree, it is hard to point to specific metrics that seem as glaring in HY as in other credit markets, especially since 2016. For example, post the Energy recovery, leverage now looks weaker in IG than in HY (beta-adjusted), while both the growth in and deterioration in ratings quality of the IG and loan markets has also been much more extreme than that of HY over the same time period. However, in our view, this certainly does not mean high yield is out of the woods.

Second, while easily-tracked fundamental metrics like leverage don’t look as extreme in high yield, we think other harder-to-track, more qualitative measures are more problematic. For example, when digging into the quality of the companies in various markets, we would argue high yield is more exposed to sectors with longer-term operational challenges (as we originally discussed in Cross-Asset Dispatches: Why We Prefer Equities Over Credit, 3 Nov 2017). In investment grade credit, 30% of the index is made up of Financials, a sector where balance sheets are very strong, thanks in part to a decade of financial regulation. The large cap equity indices are skewed towards fast growing technology companies. High yield, in our view, is more heavily exposed towards “old economy” business and Energy. Many of these companies also have high leverage, but they have survived because of such cheap money for so many years. We are guessing some of them will have trouble through another recession, especially if credit conditions tighten for a prolonged period of time. 

Third, because some of the fundamental challenges are more widely discussed in other markets, they are likely also a bit more "in the price." A good example is that the BB/BBB spread basis is at cycle tights, in part because, on the surface, long-term problems seem more material in low-quality IG than in HY. However, while we have been very vocal around the issues with BBBs, we would actually buy BBBs over BBs, simply because we think the potential challenges in high yield in a credit cycle are less appreciated than the risks elsewhere (like in BBBs).

Finally, for those who still believe HY will be relatively resilient through a credit cycle due to better fundamental trends, let’s look at recent evidence. Going forward, our view has been clear – we think the weakness in 4Q was not just a temporary valuation adjustment in a broader bull market, or about one-off headwinds like trade. We believe credit is in a bear market and the credit cycle is slowly turning. Defaults should remain low in 2019, but we think default expectations may rise this year, and actual defaults could start trending higher the year after. In the section that follows, we walk through the details behind this trade. 

## Buy Protection on Short-Dated HY CDX (S25) 

In our derivatives note last week, we recommended shorting the off-the-run CDX HY series which mature in 2-3 years (see: Credit Derivatives Research: The Basis for Hedging). Bigger picture, CDX volumes (and hedging flows) tend to be concentrated in the on-therun 5Y indices given much better liquidity relative to older series. However, for investors that want to "lock in" hedges over a long time horizon, using the 5Y indices can be quite expensive because of the roll-down from the 5Y to the 4Y point on the curve. In comparison, shorting the front end improves the economics of owning the hedge over a longer holding period quite significantly. In addition, the short-dated indices provide explicit exposure to realized defaults without the drawback of much daily mark-tomarket volatility relative to longer tenors. At the same time, in a large credit sell-off, shorting the front end can also work as a positively convex trade that benefits from rising default risk premium in the shorter tenors of the HY market (as curves tend to flatten in such a scenario). To summarize, shorting off-the-run CDX HY offers better risk-reward and a low-cost hedging option relative to the 5Y index, at the expense of liquidity. We discuss the mechanics of shorting front-end CDX HY in more detail below, specifically the S25 CDX HY index. 

## **Short-dated CDX HY as a Hedge for Rising Defaults** 

The CDX HY 25 index matures in December 2020 with the most current version comprised of 94 names as 6 entities have already defaulted. In addition, Windstream will be removed from the index on April 3 after the CDS auction. Excluding Windstream, the new version of HY25 trades at a spread of ~163bp and price of $105.7 (i.e., investors get paid 5.7% upfront to go short). If there are no more defaults in the index through maturity (a very optimistic assumption), the net cost for investors through expiry on the HY25 short would be just 3% (8.7% in cumulative coupon payments less the upfront amount received). And while 3% is not an insignificant number, if defaults are actually 0 in the next 2 years, we would assume that HY investors have realized significantly higher returns in their cash portfolios. 

Below we show how this trade performs if held to expiry for various default and recovery assumptions. As an illustration, a single default in this index with a 30% recovery, would cause a principal loss of ~0.75% [(1-0.3)/93]. As the table shows, assuming an average recovery of 30%, this trade would break even if HY25 experiences more than 3 defaults over the next 2 years. If we get 5 defaults, this trade would return 0.8% of notional, increasing to roughly 4.5% for 10 defaults.

We would add a couple of caveats here. First, while a 30% recovery rate is a standard assumption for HY CDS, we note that recoveries tend to be lower when defaults occur in clusters. For example, the average CDS recovery on HY defaults through the 2008/2009 period was just ~18%. And second, our analysis above assumes that the defaults happen just before the actual index expiry. In other words, investors pay the carry cost on the defaulted names all the way to expiry. However, if the defaults happen well before expiry, the PnL would look even better as investors would pay the carry cost only until the actual default date. 

Thinking about the break-even default rate in a historical context, we note a few points. First, just in the last 5 months, the CDX HY on-the-run index has seen three defaults (SRAC, PKD, WIN). Second, a 4% default rate over about 2 years is quite low even for a benign market environment. While we don't have a CDX default history going far back in time, below we show the 2Y trailing cumulative issuer-weighted default rate for the HY market from Moody's. Based on these numbers, there have been just 2 instances when the 2Y cumulative default rate has dipped below 4% (1997 and 2007). And, in a latecycle environment prior to an economic downturn, 2Y forward default rates have ranged from the high single digits to the low teens.

## **Short-dated CDX HY as a Risk Premium Trade** 

On top of hedging against actual defaults, short-dated HY CDX also works as a positively convex way to position for a large sell-off in credit, where the market brings default risks forward. As we show in Exhibit 14, the 3s-5s HY CDX inverted into the 2008 downturn and also flattened meaningfully in 2011 and 2015/16, when overall spreads were widening. In absolute and percentage terms, the CDX HY credit curve remains quite steep, providing a fairly good entry point for shorting the front end. While we will skip the details on why curves have remained so steep, the short story here is that structured credit flows (e.g., bespoke synthetic CDOs) in recent years have been focused on very short tenors (3 years and in). As a result, the demand for short-dated credit risk in CDS form has outpaced the demand for protection at these tenors.

Even after duration-adjusting the HY25 leg (2.4x ratio), the cost is much lower at 7.2%.
