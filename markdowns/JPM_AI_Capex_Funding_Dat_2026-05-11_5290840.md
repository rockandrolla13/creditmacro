## Data Center - Hyperscaler Bond Matrix Version 1.0 

**27 different flavors and growing.** The rapid growth of data center funding across corporate credit markets has been extraordinary. The combined hyperscaler and data center universe across the high grade and high yield indices encompasses 27 different issuers with over $450 billion in outstanding bonds. The interrelationships between hyperscalers and the various data center projects they lease are fascinating, and create a total investable universe of effectively related obligations at different risk profiles. To be abundantly clear, leasing from a project that issues its own project finance bonds and issuing your own bonds are not the same from a guarantee and security standpoint, and should not be treated the same from an accounting or pricing standpoint. Nonetheless, understanding the investable universe is valuable as investors weigh relative value amongst a rapidly growing opportunity set. We expect the universe to continue to grow, and therefore we expect this to be a living document, and welcome any and all feedback.

## Some thoughts on HG index inclusion - introducing new Data Center sub-sector in JULI 

**The sheer scale of issuance tied to hyperscalers and data centers over the past year is rapidly reshaping bond markets.** The treatment of this debt from an indexing standpoint has become an interesting and hotly debated question. That has many implications for which types of investors can participate and thus the scale of secondary trading liquidity for these securities. 

There are two key reasons why most of the data center securities have been excluded from the various key indices: **144A for life** registration status and **limited syndication** .

**The 144A for Life exclusion is the simpler of the two, in our view.** The reason this exclusion matters altogether is because currently the Bloomberg US Aggregate family of indices has overwhelming market share (81%) of the public mutual fund and ETF marketplace which we track, and presumably similar market share in the non-public SMA space as well.  Along with certain of the more popular iBoxx indices, the US Agg index family currently excludes 144A for Life securities. We believe this exclusion made a lot of sense in the 1970s when the index was rolled out and non-QIB individual investors were oftentimes direct buyers of corporate bonds. Fast forward to today and the vast majority of bond-buying is through QIB-eligible mutual funds and ETFs and thus this exclusion makes far less sense. There are of course other differences as it relates to 144A for life securities, such as a lack of public disclosure, but these, in our view, are not grounds for index exclusion relative to for example the $38bn of mostly EM bonds in the US Agg that are fully registered but in jurisdictions such as the Cayman Islands, Bermuda and the Virgin Islands.  Bloomberg led an investor consultation to add 144A for Life to the US Agg in October 2023 but this proposal never went forward (we discussed this here at the time).  In contrast, the Bloomberg Universal and Global Agg indices as well as the benchmark ICE indices do include 144A for Life securities, as does every major HY index to our knowledge. This matters given many of the recent data center issuances straddle the BBB/BB divide. **Our view is that the present day IG market includes many 144A for Life issuers and thus a representative index should do so too.**

**“Limited syndication” is a more valid reason for index exclusion in our view.** Limited primary market access does in theory prevent many investors from being able to properly replicate the index. However, the boundaries of what constitutes limited syndication are very hard to define using publicly accessible data and therefore hard to define with any degree of objectivity. Case in point, the recent RDMICH issuance was excluded from the 144A For Life friendly Bloomberg Global Agg and US Universal indices, whereas it was included in the ICE indices that had previously excluded Beignet (RPLDCI) for this same reason. It is our understanding that the more recent RDMICH bond had more investors involved in the primary process than RPLDCI and thus reason for different index providers to draw this line at a different point in the sand, but this shows the frailty of this criteria altogether. **Our view is that when there is public disclosure pointing to limited syndication, this should be supplemented with some sort of objective criteria** . Those criteria could be spread related. For example, if a bond issued with limited syndication tightens more than say 5% of its issuance spread over the first five days then it should be excluded; if not, it should be included, so long as its secondary market volumes are in line with similar size/rating broadly syndicated, registered securities. Or to keep it simple, benchmark indices should tighten their definition of limited syndication to exclude just deals with very little immediate secondary liquidity.
