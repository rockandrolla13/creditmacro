## **Banging the Drum on the Balance Sheet** 

Over recent weeks, our preference for owning US duration has strengthened. We have been suggesting that investors express that preference on a cross-market basis. We have favored owning duration in the US and Canada against duration in Germany and the UK. While we continue to like that trade, we are growing more comfortable owning US duration outright. 

One factor that has contributed to our preference for duration in the US has been our view on the Fed’s balance sheet normalization process. As we initially described in A Surprisingly Early End to Balance Sheet Normalization, and then reiterated in Insight into the Balance Sheet, we expect the balance sheet normalization process to end in September 2019. 

Given how much earlier this end would be relative to consensus, we think market participants will have to adjust lower their expectations for US Treasury issuance. As investors lower their expectations for Treasury issuance, yields should fall. We estimate this effect alone will be worth 20-25bp on the 10y yields. 

As we discussed in our FOMC Preview: Patience Turned Prudent, we expect the US rates market reaction to be limited if the FOMC meets our economists' expectations. Without an update to the Summary of Economic Projections and a new dot-plot, the meeting is unlikely to move rates markets – which we see as priced for the flexibility and patience. However, an update to the Fed's balance sheet normalization principles, and possibly its plans, has the potential to move the rates market. 

In particular, markets would move if the Fed provided clarity on (1) when its balance sheet normalization process may come to an end – perhaps along the lines of “later this year” or “within a year from now” or “by mid-2020”, (2) the amount of reserves the Fed believes it may need for effective control over its short-term target rate, and/or (3) how the duration of the SOMA portfolio will evolve once the balance sheet has reached its equilibrium size. 

Most market participants agree that the composition of the System Open Market Account (SOMA) portfolio will tilt more toward US Treasuries and away from agency MBS. Disagreement, however, remains entrenched on its ultimate duration. Recall the minutes from the December 2018 FOMC meeting: 

_...participants discussed the advantages of different portfolio maturity compositions. Several participants noted that a portfolio of holdings weighted toward shorter maturities would provide greater flexibility to lengthen maturity if warranted by an economic downturn, while a couple of others noted that a portfolio with maturities that matched the outstanding Treasury market would have a more neutral effect on the market._ 

4 

Any clarity on which direction the consensus is shifting could move markets. Aiming for a smaller duration would lead to a yield curve steepening, while aiming to keep the duration in line with Treasury issuance would lead to outperformance of intermediatesector Treasuries, in our view.

## **Is it just technical?** 

The previously mentioned media report also suggested that the decision on the balance sheet is being driven by technical considerations. These relate to the demand for reserves and the Fed’s ability to control the effective fed funds rate (EFFR), as opposed to the impact of the balance sheet on financial conditions. As discussed in Insight into the Balance Sheet, we believe balance sheet normalization, in conjunction with higher risk-free rates, causes private sector portfolios to rebalance away from risky assets and toward US Treasuries and agency MBS. 

This portfolio rebalancing – known as the portfolio balance channel effect – has been taking place for the last year in increasingly larger volumes. The fourth-quarter rebalancing simply happened to coincide with a period of reduced liquidity and softening growth expectations, both at home and abroad. Of course, the environment in which portfolios rebalance matters. And the environment in December was not supportive of de-risking flows. 

It’s worth emphasizing how portfolio rebalancing works in conjunction with interest rate levels to move markets. With quantitative easing (QE), the Fed removed assets of long duration (Treasuries and agency MBS) and replaced them with assets of zero duration (cash). This occurred just as the incentive to own longer-duration assets of all kinds – e.g., corporate debt and equities – was rising, thanks to near-zero yields on shorterduration assets. As a result, portfolios rebalanced toward scarce, riskier assets of longer duration, which drove prices higher. 

Exhibit 4 outlines the process from a balance sheet (T accounting) perspective. We present the T accounts of 3 economic agents: the Federal Reserve, the public "banking sector", and the "public" non-banking sector. In theory, QE could work as in Scenario 1 where the Fed simply removes Treasuries and agency MBS from the banking sector and replaces them with reserves. In the exhibit, we label Treasuries and agency MBS as "Group 1" securities. We include every other asset class in Group 2. In Scenario 1, QE does not affect public, non-bank balance sheets.

## **January BoJ meeting** 

As had been foreshadowed in the media, the BoJ ended up leaving monetary policy unchanged at its January 22–23 meeting while significantly lowering its FY2019 core CPI inflation forecast, citing "the decline in crude oil prices" as well as the impact of eliminating preschool education fees when the consumption tax rate is hiked to 10% in their outlook report. 

The Board members' consensus as of the July 2018 BoJ meeting was already that Japan would find it very difficult to achieve +2% core inflation (ex. C-tax hike effect) by FY2020 (see this). The BoJ is currently focusing primarily on maintaining "momentum toward achieving the price stability target", which means that the economy's output gap remains positive and medium- to long-term inflation expectations are projected to rise gradually. Given that both conditions have been satisfied so far, the additional easing seems to be unnecessary at this juncture. 

Questions at BoJ Governor Haruhiko Kuroda's post-meeting press conference focused primarily on the central bank's views on overseas economic and political risk, which is understandable given the Outlook report's declaration that "downside risks concerning overseas economies are likely to be heightening recently". However, it was surprising to hear almost no mention whatsoever of adverse "side effects" of protracted monetary easing, which until recently had been such a hot topic. The sole question on that issue pertained to market functioning, and Kuroda responded as below. 

_Market functioning has indeed improved to some degree since the BoJ's end-July 2018 widening of its "yield curve control" target range for the 10y JGB yield in the sense that yields are now fluctuating to a considerably greater degree than they were in 1H 2018 (when there were several days on which no 10y JGBs changed hands whatsoever)._

## **Definition of terms** 

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period. 

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period. 

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period. 

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period. 

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase. 

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease. 

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
