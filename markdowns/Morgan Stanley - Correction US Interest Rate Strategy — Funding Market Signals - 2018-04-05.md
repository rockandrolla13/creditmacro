## **Correction: US Interest Rate Strategy** 

## Funding Market Signals 

Demand for 'risk-free' short-term agency debt and T-bills picked up. We see this as a signal for tighter FRAOIS as FHLBs channel funding to banks, and we expect tighter Libor-Repo. Outstanding CPs fell $81bn from the peak on a seasonally adjusted basis. 

**2y Swap Spread Tighteners** now look attractive since 3m T-bill-OIS has tightened 11bp from its March 15 peak, while Libor-OIS remains wide. **The result is that Libor-Repo and Libor-T-bill spreads are at the widest since at least MMF reform.** We believe this offers an attractive entry point given that CP issuance has been trending down, which eases the funding pressures in unsecured lending markets. 

**The Libor-OIS-Repo Framework** we decompose the Libor-OIS yield into a: Credit spread component (Libor-Repo, or Libor-T-bills spread) 

Risk-free funding demand component (Repo-OIS or T-bill-OIS) 

The fact that the risk-free component, T-bill-OIS spread, widened 22bp since November 2017, while Libor-OIS widened 48bp, suggests that T-bill issuance could explain over 40% of the move in Libor-OIS. The rise in CP issuance in January and widening credit spreads since February account for the rest of the move. 

**FHLBs, Lenders of Next-to-Last Resort** , **have possibly moved cash out of the Fed Funds market and into Repo as the Triparty Repo yield surpassed the FF rate for the first time in years. We believe this was a driver for upticks in the FF rate.** We also provide an overview of the FHLBs' role as bank lenders in the years since the introduction of Basel III regulations. **FHLB funding spreads to OIS have tightened 11bp from the peak over the past few weaks and we expect this to filter into lower rates on bank advances, thus helping banks diversify funding.** 

**Our T-bill Issuance Projections** remain unchanged. We expect that total outstanding T-bills will not reach new highs later this year since we forecast more coupon issuance increases in May and August. 

**BEAT:** We detailed in a previous publication that the BEAT has almost no implication for CP issuance. 

We also list the factors that could lead FRAOIS to widen contrary to our expectations. Those include a reversal in CD-OIS spreads, an increase in CP issuance, or a credit risk event.

## 2y Swap Spread Tighteners 

## **Libor-Repo spread vs history** 

The CP/CD issuance seen in January as well as the widening in credit spreads have pushed the Libor-T-bill spread more than 25bp above its historical long-term value (see Exhibit 1). It is now at MMF reform levels, while Libor-Repo is higher than pre-MMF reform levels. 

We will discuss in the The Libor-OIS-Repo Framework section that this is a result of total CP outstanding reaching pre-MMF reform levels while Prime MMF AUM is still significantly lower than 2015 levels. Exhibit 2 illustrates that lagged correlation between CP issuance and Libor-OIS, which we will discuss in more detail later. 

There a few reasons we expect both the Libor-Repo and Libor T-bill spreads to tighten back towards their long-term averages: 

- **Wider Libor-OIS has historically led to a drop in CP issuance with a two-month lag** (see Exhibit 2 and Exhibit 9). Indeed, Exhibit 2 shows that total CP outstanding fell $38bn from their early February peak on a non-seasonally adjusted basis, and $81bn on a seasonally adjusted basis. 

- The reduction in CP issuance will be replaced by other funding sources. **FHLB advances is one of those sources since funding spreads for FHLBs have fallen 11bp** 

**vs. OIS from the peak reached in March.** We discuss this dynamic in detail in the FHLBs, Lenders of Next-to-Last Resort section. Terming out debt issuance and attracting deposits are two other avenues we expect banks to utilize. 

- Meanwhile we expect the Treasury to issue another ~$300bn of net T-bills early in 2019. This is likely to renew the upward pressure on Repo relative to Libor, which would lead to a tighter Libor-Repo spread on average over the next two years.

The expected Libor-Repo spread over the next two years represents the theoretical fundamental value of the 2-year swap spread. Intuitively, this is because the counterparty that's long the Treasury and paid fixed on the swap would be paying the repo rate to fund the Treasury, while earning the Libor sets on the floating leg of the swap. The difference between the repo paid and the Libor earned over the life of the swap should equate to the swap spread locked in at the beginning of the trade for the swap spread trade to breakeven. 

One point to highlight is that part of the reason for the elevated 2y swap spread is that the spot 3m Libor-Repo is elevated. **Once the negative carry is realized, the remaining 1.75y swap spread would naturally be expected to tighten if the forwards are realized,** 

**but the P/L on the trade would be zero.** This would be the case if the realized LiborRepo spread in the future follows the solid yellow line in Exhibit 4. If the forwards instead follow the green line, the P/L on the trade would be positive. 

In other words, the forwards are implying a Libor-Repo spread averaging ~22bp over the next two years. **However, our expectation is that Libor-Repo would average closer to 10bp over the next two years given the reasons listed above, which are likely to push Libor-Repo even below the 12bp average realized since 2014 (see Exhibit 3).**

## The Libor-OIS-Repo Framework 

We acknowledge the breadth of opinions on Libor-OIS moves, so we suggest breaking down Libor-OIS into two subcomponents when analyzing the drivers: 

**Repo-OIS: a measure of USD availability** , balance sheet capacity, and supply/demand for secured borrowing. 

**Libor-Repo: a measure of credit risk** and supply/demand of unsecured borrowing. 

This framework complements the arguments we presented in the Funding Supply and Demand piece on fundamentals drivers of Libor-OIS and could be useful for investors whether they are long or short FRAOIS as it helps isolate the following drivers of LiborOIS: (a) T-bill issuance; (b) Commercial paper issuance; (c) Credit risk premium.

## **Credit spreads** 

Finally, we address what could be a measure of the fundamental component of the Libor-OIS spread: the bank default risk. Lending to a bank on an unsecured basis for 3 months (i.e., Libor) as opposed to overnight lending in the Fed Funds market (i.e., OIS) entails additional credit risk. This risk is represented by the equilibrium Libor-OIS spread; at least in theory it should be. 

As a result, widening credit spreads and stock market volatility can very well change that fundamental value. ## FHLBs, Lenders of Next-to-Last Resort 

## **FHLB advances** 

The 11 Federal Home Loan Banks are often referred to as lenders of next-to-last resort, since they offer advances to the banking sector in exchange for collateral even during periods of funding stress. For example, they increased their bank advances by 50% between 2007 and 3Q 2008. In this section we summarize their funding activity and how it could be a leading indicator of Libor-OIS. 

All federally-insured depository institutions and insurance companies engaged in residential housing finance may apply for membership to an FHLB. That makes them eligible for secured funding through the FHLB referred to as an "advance". 

Originally, this lending activity was intended as a catalyst to mortgage lending. The higher credit quality of the FHLBs allows them to raise funds at lower cost before passing on the funds to banks. However, with the introduction of liquidity requirements on banks as part of Dodd-Frank and Basel regulations (e.g., LCR), the FHLB advances became a very useful balance sheet management tools. It allows a bank to pledge an illiquid asset such as a residential mortgage loan against a cash advance from an FHLB. 

**While a mortgage loan would not have counted towards the Liquidity Coverage Ratio, the cash obtained through the advance would be. This improves the overall LCR ratio for a bank since FHLB advances maturing within the next 30 days have an outflow ratio of only 25% in the LCR calculation. Effectively, the bank rotates out of an illiquid asset and into cash.**

## **LCR impact** 

This highlights an important link: while repo funding of UST holdings does not improve the LCR ratio of a bank (since both cash and USTs are HQLA), FHLB advances can improve the ratio. FHLBs emphasise this benefit in their efforts to advertise advances, for example, the FHLB of Cincinnati lists the following as uses for FHLB advances: 

- Facilitates balance sheet restructuring and reduces interest rate risk exposure by lengthening liabilities 

- A competitive substitute for deposits, assisting in the stabilization or reduction of interest rate risk 

Useful for financing mortgage portfolios (via strips of differing maturities) 

As a result of this dynamic, we look at average advance rate spreads to OIS as one of the leading indicators to Libor-OIS. Exhibit 14 shows that FHLB funding costs got more expensive relative to OIS starting in February; going from negative to positive. This increased funding cost was passed on to banks through higher advance rates. **However,** 

**FHLB funding spreads have tightened 12bp from their peak reached on March 20th. For their part, advance rates that we track tightened 3.5bp to OIS from the peak and we expect them to continue tightening.** 

One limit to how much FHLBs can offer in advances is their leverage ratio requirements. However, consolidated FHLB financial statements indicate that FHLBs have a sufficient capital buffer. Specifically, the ratio of actual capital to regulatory capital requirements is 1.3:1 as of December 2017. Counterparty risk limits are another potential constraint, but it is not clear to us if this is an issue for certain bank members. 

## **Fed Funds/Repo connection** 

The importance of FHLBs extends to the Fed Funds market. Exhibit 17 illustrates that 9% of FHLB assets are invested in the Fed Funds market. In fact, FHLBs are the largest (and at times the sole) lender in the FF market. This is because banks have no incentive to lend at the effective FF rate, when they can simply earn IOER from the Fed. Since FHLBs are not eligible for earning IOER, they are willing to lend at the effective FF rate to banks that then invest the cash as excess reserves to earn IOER. 

## **An alternative investment that FHLBs have is lending cash in the Triparty Repo market.** 

Both repo and Fed Funds lending count as eligible assets that meet the liquidity stress tests imposed on FHLBs (the stress tests are basically two scenarios that assume no access to funding for a period of time). However, Exhibit 16 illustrates that the Triparty Repo rate has been consistently lower than the FF rate, making repo investments less attractive. This is part of the reason why the asset allocation to Fed Funds is much larger than the allocation to repo. 

10 

Another advantage for FF lending over repo lending is that the FHLBs can receive their money back earlier the next day when an overnight FF transaction matures, while triparty cash is returned later in the day. The early repayment allows the FHLBs to meet the mid-day deadline on due payments to the Office of Finance that manages consolidated FHLB funding needs. 

Nevertheless, we believe that the FF-triparty repo spread is a strong determining factor for the Fed Funds vs Repo asset allocation. **Exhibit 16 shows there was a sharp drop in Fed Funds activity just as Triparty started offering higher yields relative to the FF rate. We believe this is reflective of FHLBs allocating around $20bn of cash out of the FF market and into repo. If repo funding remains elevated, we expect this trend to continue and it would put upward pressure on the effective FF rate, which is now only 7bp below IOER.** 

In short, we believe a change in FHLB asset allocation could have been a driver for the upticks seen in the effective FF rate in March.

## T-bill Issuance Projections 

Some market participants have been floating around the idea that T-bill issuance is likely to increase back to new highs by the third quarter of this year. This is contrary to our projections. We have strong conviction that net T-bill issuance for the rest of the calendar year will be zero or slightly negative. 

The difference between our view and that of other market participants does not seem to be coming from differing deficit estimates. Our estimate for $850bn deficit for FY2018 seems to be in line with consensus (although we will get confirmation from the Treasury refunding statement later this month). We estimate the equivalent calendar year 2018 deficit at $954bn, depending in part on when the appropriations bills are enacted. We discuss the details of our deficit projections in Treasury Refunding: More was Less. 

Adding $954bn deficit to $229bn in Fed balance sheet UST rundown and the need to raise the cash balance $100bn above 2017 year-end levels leads to a total estimated CY2018 funding need of $1,283bn. 

This need could be met a number of ways after accounting for the $1,274bn of coupon UST redemptions in CY2018. For example, the Treasury already announced an increase in coupon auction sizes in the February refunding statement. They could either increase coupons further at the May and August refunding statements or they could just rely on T-bills to fill the gap. It is possible that some market participants are not expecting a further increase in coupon issuance at the coming refunding statement; that would explain the very high T-bill issuance estimates we have come across from other market participants.
