## Crossing the Rubicon 

Market pricing of Fed policy crossed the Rubicon of pricing in rate hikes over the next 12 months to pricing in rate cuts. We expect the pricing of rate cuts to persist for now. We continue to suggest positioning long duration in the US and Canada against short duration in Europe. 

## **Government Bonds** 

In the US, we stay neutral on duration and curve. In the euro area, we continue to suggest that investors position for periphery spread tightening via long 50/50 10y PGBs/Bonos vs short 10y French OATs. In the UK, we suggest investors enter 30s40s steepeners coming out of the APF and into an expected ultralong syndication in May. We also suggest 2s5s GBP swap steepeners to fade the flatness of front end rate expectations. In Japan, we maintain constructive view for the long-end on back of strong demand from Japanese lifers. We also maintain our JGB 20s40s flattener. 

## **Inflation** 

In the US, we continue to suggest being long 5-year real yields as a mediumterm trade, especially given the significant risk adjusted carry in the next 3 months, while being neutral on breakevens. We examine the upcoming changes in March CPI, where the BLS plans to use a new big data source in compiling the index.

## How does the Fed read the yield curve? 

We recently discussed our views on the inversion of the 3m/10y Treasury curve in What Also Comes Before Recessions? Rate Cuts. We discovered the emergence of a Fed, post1982, that tried to prevent recessions with rate cuts, but without much success unfortunately. In a sense, our research suggested that investors who focus on curve inversions and recessions should really be focusing on curve inversions and rate cuts. 

In that sense, we should pay more attention to how FOMC participants see the yield curve as opposed to how the yield curve may see the economy. So how does the FOMC interpret the yield curve? Chairman Powell illuminated the framework in his Q&A with Dallas Fed President Kaplan: 

_So if the yield curve is inverted, that may mean that current policy is or is expected to be tight, which is to say higher than – that rates – you know, that you have rates that are actually higher than the neutral rate, so you’re deliberately slowing the economy._ 

3 

Powell suggests that an inverted curve implies tight policy, which in turn suggests that a flat curve implies neutral policy. In that sense, the longer the 3m/10y yield curve stays near zero, the more FOMC participants may gravitate to the view that the current policy setting is a neutral one. At this point, 6 of the 11 participants who advocate no rate hikes in 2019 believe policy is already at a neutral setting, according to the dot-plot. The remaining 5, along with the 6 who advocate for more rate hikes in 2019, believe policy is still below neutral (see Exhibit 1).

What might the yield curve mean for the future of Fed policy? Ultimately, it depends on how proactive or reactive the Fed wants its policy to be. If the Fed wants to add accommodation before being told to do so – i.e., before the yield curve inversion deepens and suggests Fed policy is too tight – it may cut rates even though the yield curve hasn’t inverted meaningfully. In this case, we wouldn’t expect the Fed to cut rates 50bp, but rather 25bp. 

However, if the Fed adopts a more patient, wait-and-see approach, then the yield curve may have to invert much further before the Fed decides to cut rates. With a deeper yield curve inversion than what we have seen so far, markets would be telling the Fed that its policy setting is too tight. If that curve inversion is accompanied by tighter financial conditions, the Fed may respond in a reactive way by cutting rates 50bp. 

## The asymmetry of cuts versus hikes 

The idea that the Fed could cut rates by 50bp in one go is an important one. It contrasts rather dramatically with the conclusion one may draw about the nature of another rate hike, should it occur. If the Fed were to hike rates at some point, and hike them more than once even, most people would expect them to do so in a very gradual way - perhaps even more gradually than the last 8 rate hikes, which were separated by 3 months each. If the Fed delivered 2 more hikes, they could be separated by 6 months, or maybe more. 

4 

This asymmetry suggests that markets, over the next 3 months at least, will struggle to price out any chance of a rate cut in the forward-looking 12-month period. As we saw during the early stages of the 2006 on-hold period (2H06), futures markets priced between a 25bp and 50bp cumulative rate cut in each forward-looking 12-month period.

## Bund yields head higher, curves steeper, periphery tighter 

10y Bund yields climbed out of negative territory, 2s10s and 5s30s curves were steeper in the core and semi-core, periphery spreads were broadly tighter and European equity markets climbed higher coincident with the positive tone in financial markets throughout the week. 

For the most part, fears about growth slowdowns globally subsided this last week, following the release of stronger than expected manufacturing PMIs in China and the US, generally stronger IFO and composite PMI data in Europe including in Italy, news about the possibility of a US/China trade deal, some positive repricing related to the prospect of a no-deal Brexit and finally, a repricing of US Federal Reserve rate cut expectations in 2019. 

In Europe specifically, the generally positive backdrop and data developments mostly stood in contrast to last week’s poor German and Euro Area manufacturing PMIs, increasingly making them look isolated and one-off, as noted by our European economics team. 

However, softer than expected inflation data led by a weaker than expected outcome for core means most of our potential NSA tracking scenarios now envision core CPI dropping to potentially as low as 0.40% to 0.60% YoY by the middle of Q3 2019. While we expect an acceleration in both core and headline in the back half of 2019, and classification/changes in methodology/Easter timing may have played a part in the softer inflation print in March, it won’t change the likelihood that YoY measures appear to be heading lower in the near term. 

Against this backdrop, and noting the generally dovish tone in the most recent ECB account of its March Governing Council meeting, we therefore remain cautious about outright duration shorts in Europe and prefer to hold any Bund shorts, via the Ryder Cup of Bonds trade. 

However, there are compelling ways to play for stability and/or a growth rebound in Europe that do not involve outright short duration positions, or in some cases curve steepeners, particularly before the trough in inflation and should the ECB remain dovish for the foreseeable future. 

One such expression may be in Italy, where BTPs are starting to look increasingly more attractive versus both Bunds and the broader periphery. We see room for the outlook for BTPs to improve over the medium term; particularly should the economic data flow take a positive turn. While our preference in the periphery currently remains with both Portugal and Spain, we believe that any potential upcoming political volatility associated with budget discussions may provide an opportunity for attractive entry.

## The case for Italian spread tightening may be building 

The combination of negative growth, concerns about budget deterioration, increased supply after the ECB ended PSPP purchases and political uncertainty have kept Italian spread performance subdued despite a dovish ECB and declining inflation in Europe. While Italy’s 10y spread relative to 10y Bunds has stabilized in 2019, it has underperformed the tightening experienced in countries with better growth and fiscal performance such as Portugal and Spain. 

However, we believe that the surprise jump in the Italian composite PMI may be a tentative sign that growth is stabilizing to improving in Italy at a time when markets are still reaching for yield and Italy offers EGB investors plenty of it. 

As our European economists have noted, the macro and debt paths remain challenging and there is potential for volatility to rise given the upcoming budget negotiations; however, many of these concerns do appear to be understood and respected by the market given Italy’s spread underperformance throughout the year. 

A better growth backdrop would alleviate some market concerns about further budget deterioration, additional supply, economy-wide bad debts and potentially even some degree of political uncertainty. As we note, a stronger composite PMI has also been consistent with a tighter 10y spread. We will continue to look for attractive entry in Italy, particularly as budget discussions near. 

## Growth data improving 

Italy’s composite PMI surprised strongly to the upside in March, climbing to its best level since September 2018 at 51.5, in perhaps one of the first signs that growth may be stabilizing to improving, after two quarters of weaker growth. 

We feel this to be important for the outlook for Italy’s spread performance, given the historic relationship we have noted previously between the composite PMI and Italy’s 10y spread to 10y German Bunds. Based on the most recent outcome, Italy’s 10y spread now appears roughly 51bp wide relative to implied fair value based upon this simple relationship, noting of course that the spread outperformed the relationship during heavier PSPP buying. Should Italy’s growth prospects continue to improve but the spread remain at current levels, perhaps due to concerns about budget discussions, the attractiveness relative to the historic relationship would increase.

## Bad debts continue to fall across the economy 

Concerns about a period of a protracted slowdown in Italy may have also led to concerns that bad debts throughout the economy may start rising again. ## A yield grab, tends to favor those that actually have yield 

The dovish ECB and global central bank backdrop, as well as the expectation that YoY levels of inflation will continue to fall for the foreseeable future, suggest to us that the reach for yield may continue in Europe. To date, spread tightening in both Portugal and Spain has been our suggested expression for this theme, but as noted, the case for Italy is becoming increasingly compelling. 

Carry and roll down in BTPs is attractive relative to the rest of Europe. On a historic volatility adjusted basis we have seen Italy’s attractiveness improving substantially over the last couple of months, even if it remains somewhat below volatility-adjusted carry profiles in Spain and Portugal. 

This, of course, does not mean that investors will buy Italy just because carry is attractive, but should a reach for yield environment persist and growth in Italy continue to improve, we think it will start to matter. For a short position, the significant decline in volatility relative to the higher carry obviously makes the position harder to hold over a sustained period. We believe any remaining short base in Italy would be quick to cover, particularly since growth improvement would likely lead to fewer concerns about budget deterioration and/or a larger increase in supply. 

## More cautious by the minute(s) 

This week saw the release of the minutes of the ECB’s March monetary policy meeting, and while revealing little new information they broadly corroborated the cautious and dovish tone policymakers have struck in communications during and after the meeting. An interesting point of note was the debate around the extent to which rate hike guidance should be deferred, with some members of the governing council wanting to push guidance on the first hike back to the end of Q1 2020. Interestingly, a counterargument raised to this suggestion was the desire not to give the impression that policy had been predetermined: a rationale in line with our opinion that the ECB is seeking to hedge its guidance in order to preserve its ability to react to data surprises while maintaining its credibility. 

The minutes gave confirmation to what markets had already surmised: that the potential negative effects of NIRP on financial stability are indeed being discussed within the context of the governing council. However, the heterogeneity of banks and the differentials in the burden of negative rates on each of them was also explicated, making it clear that the complexity of any tiering operation is already salient in the mind of the governing council (despite the fact that the question is still very early-stage).

## Brexit and non-Brexit steepeners 

We suggest investors enter tactical 30s40s steepeners coming out of the APF, and in anticipation of the ultra-long conventional syndication which is likely to take place midMay. Through the month of March and the first half of April, the Bank will have bought roughly £4bn in 10y duration-equivalent terms in equal notional across the curve, driving necessary bull flattening pressure. As we approach the end of the operation, the market will look to tactically fade the price action going into and through the APF, even though investors were much better set up this year than last. 

We expect the syndication to occur Tuesday, May 14th, as there is a large gap in the DMO issuance calendar for fiscal Q1 between May 9th and May 23rd. Based on the most recent consultation with investors and GEMMs, we would expect the syndication to be either the 2057s or the 2071s, as these were the most frequently requested amongst GEMMs. Of the two, we would place a marginally higher probability on the 2057s, given that, among investors, “a fairly common theme expressed was that the focus of demand has moved shorter down the curve from the 50-year area to the 25-35 year area.” 

As a result, we like to locate our long leg in the 30y sector and the short leg in the 40y sector. We acknowledge that this trade would behave very similarly (both in terms of directional exposure and volatility) to 30s50s, in the event we are wrong about the -57s and the DMO delivers the -71s instead. 

The near term risks to the view include the potential for bull flattening in the event of an adverse Brexit outcome, though the relationship between 30s40s and the level of rates is quite variable over time. A further risk could be the DMO not delivering on the expectation for an ultralong syndication, but we think this is highly unlikely given the DMO favours predictability of issuance which aligns very closely with what investors have asked for. 

We would also note that, while ultra underperformance may work tactically, we would not adopt the same view strategically, for a couple of reasons.

To estimate how much that impact would be, we consider the repo landscape before the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) was introduced (public disclosure of SLR started in 2015). The SLR is a very relevant regulation here since it measures leverage with no regard to the risk of the assets held by the dealer. So a repo transaction on the balance sheet would increase leverage by just as much as a holding of a junk corporate bond. 

Exhibit 26 illustrates how the introduction of SLR led to a widening of the spread between triparty repo rates and GCF repo rates. This is akin to a widening of bid-offer spreads on repo, since dealers typically borrow cash from money market funds at the triparty repo rate, then lend cash to smaller dealers via the GCF repo platform at the higher GCF repo rate. The spread between triparty and GCF repo rates is the compensation the dealer requires for the balance sheet cost associated with the intermediation. 

Exhibit 27 illustrates how after the introduction of SLR reporting in 2015, not only the spread between the two rates widened from about 4bp to about 12bp on average, but quarter-end spikes became more prevalent. As a result, estimating the impact of minimizing the balance sheet cost associated with the use of the sponsored repo model could be done through assuming that we move back to the pre-SLR triparty-spread (8b of tightening). That is as a base-line scenario, we can expect GCF repo rates to fall by 4bp, while triparty repo rates rise by 4bp on average. 

That is, we can expect the funding cost for levered buyers of US Treasuries to improve by about 4bp. Of course, there are many assumptions associated with that analysis: 

**1. GCF repo is only representative of the average funding cost for some dealers** , but not of the funding cost for other levered investors. Hedge funds that currently fund through non-cleared bilateral repo, for example, are probably currently funding at rates even higher than GCF repo depending on balance sheet availability. As a result, some levered investors could see an average improvement in UST funding rates of more than the 4bp base-line we calculated earlier. 

**2.** The above analysis also underestimates the improvement in funding rates since it **does not account for the term-premium** placed on potential spikes in repo rates at quarter-ends. That is, even though measuring the historical spread between triparty and GCF shows a 12bp spread ex-post; the ex-ante expectations for the average spread could have been higher to account for potential spikes at quarter-ends. A prime example of this is the pricing for the latest March 2019 quarter end. The GC repo turn was initially priced as high as 3.40-3.80% (140bp above fed funds), but it ended up only around 2.95%. As a result, only the 2.95% was accounted for in the historical averaging in Exhibit 27, but some investors actually had to pay as high as 3.8% to secure funding for the turn ahead of time. 

**3.** On the flip-side, the fact that **dealers using the sponsored model will have to post Clearing Fund collateral** based on the exposure of their sponsored members introduces some balance sheet cost and would skew the 4bp value we calculated lower.

Euro Inflation Watch: Pipeline Pressures Petering Out 

## _01 Apr 2019_ 

Today's fall in core inflation, while anticipated by weak country prints, was worse than consensus expectations. Seasonal effects and methodological changes may have played a role, but producer price weakness does point to poor underlying consumer price inflation. The ECB is likely to stay dovish. 

US Economics & Rates Strategy: Binary Outlook Binds the End _31 Mar 2019_ 

Based on the Fed's recent rhetoric, the odds of the next rate move being up or down look closer to balanced. We expect the Fed to remain on hold for longer to guard against downside risks, removing our expectation for a December hike. Our strategists expect UST 10y yields to end the year at 2.25%. 

## Global Interest Rate Strategist: Go On, Take the Money and Run _29 Mar 2019_ 

Having achieved our 2.35% target on the UST 10y, we no longer suggest investors overweight US duration outright. While we can conceive of a 25 or 50bp rate cut this year, pricing a September cut in March puts the cart before the horse. At the same time, we still suggest the Ryder Cup of Bonds trade. 

Japan Economics and Strategy: What If the C-Tax Hike is Postponed? _28 Mar 2019_ 

We think the risk of a postponement of the consumption tax hike has increased, from a 30% probability previously to 50% now. A delay would be bullish equities and supportive of an earlier move for TOPIX to our year-end base case target of 1,800 (+14%). 

Belgium Economics & Strategy: Pricing Political Uncertainty 

## _25 Mar 2019_ 

Elections in May raise political risks. A difficult process of government formation could increase uncertainty and result in a less favourable policy mix. Combined with slower growth, this makes debt sustainability more challenging. We highlight risks to term premia and value in certain equities. 

US Interest Rate Strategist: What Also Comes Before Recessions? Rate Cuts _22 Mar 2019_ 

Focus on the yield curve as a predictor of US recessions is commonplace. While the yield curve has a proven track record, another indicator has recently been as good at preceding US recessions: Fed rate cuts. Ever since it began targeting rates, the Fed has cut them ahead of recessions.

## **Definition of terms** 

Buy/Long: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be positive over the relevant time period. 

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period. 

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period. 

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period. 

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase. 

51 

Receive: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will decrease. 

Unless otherwise specified, the time frame for recommendations included in the Morgan Stanley Fixed Income Research reports is 1 - 3 months and the price of financial instruments mentioned in the recommendation is as at the date and time of publication of the recommendation. 

When more than one issuer or instrument is included in a recommendation, analyst expects one part of the trade to outperform the other trade or combination of other trades included in the recommendation on a relative basis.
