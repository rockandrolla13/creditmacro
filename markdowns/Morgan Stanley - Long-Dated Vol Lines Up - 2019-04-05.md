## Long-Dated Vol Lines Up 

Long-dated equity options are most attractive when vol is low, equity carry is high and markets are inexpensive. All these have lined up for stocks in the UK, Europe and Japan. 

**A framework for long-dated equity vol:** We look for a framework for assessing the attractiveness of buying long-dated equity options taking into account valuations, volatility and pricing of equity forwards. 

**The revenge of option pricing 101:** Dust off those textbooks. Options price off a forward point, which for equities is a function of the dividend yield less risk-free rate. Given that rates are now back at multi-year lows and much lower than dividend yields in several markets, options giving investors the right to buy a market at current levels in five years' time have rarely been cheaper. 

**Focus on Europe and Japan:** The UK, eurozone and Japan exhibit the dynamic noted above, trade at undemanding valuations and have historically low implied volatility. These dynamics are less compelling for EM equities (which we like) and US equities (which we don't). 

**Investment implications:** Long-dated (5yr) calls provide a defensive way to express positive views in relatively underowned and high carry equity markets in Europe and Japan. Specifically, we like 5yr ATMF calls on the FTSE 100. The calls will be profitable as long as the index is at the current level or higher in five years' time.

## Long-dated vol lines up 

The confluence of low equity volatility, high equity carry and attractive valuations (outright and cross-market) has created an unusually strong case to own equity exposure in the UK, Europe and Japan through long-dated options. We discuss why, and our preferred ways to express our view. 

## The revenge of option pricing 101 

Options are priced relative to a forward point, which in turn is based on the _no-arbitrage principle_ . That principle holds that investors should be indifferent between buying the market today and holding it for a given amount of time, and the price of the market that given amount of time in the future. 

While the reality can be a little more complicated, the concept still applies. If holding US dollars yields 3% more than holding euros, then the price of EURUSD in 12 months' time should be 3% more than it is today. If it wasn't, there would be a clear arbitrage opportunity that would be quickly exploited.

Calculating a forward price for bonds or FX is relatively straightforward, as there's a high level of certainty of the carry the holder will enjoy. Equities are a bit more complicated, but the logic is that an investor should be indifferent between holding a stock and collecting its dividend, and holding a risk-free asset until they buy the market at the forward point. 

The bottom line? As we see in Exhibit 3, **the higher the dividend yield relative to the risk-free rate, the lower the forward price is assumed to be.** This is extremely relevant today, given near-record differentials between the two. 

The FTSE 100 has a dividend yield of 4.8%, and a 5-year swap rate of 1.1%. This would imply that the FTSE 100, five years forward, should price about ~17% _below_ the current spot rate. Where do inflation swaps say that UK prices will be five years forward (on inflation breakevens)? About 17% _higher_ . Bottom line, FTSE 100 forwards are heavily discounted in real terms.

Buying a market forward, at well below its current levels, offers investors a good head start. Buying long-dated options, which price off these forwards, shares this benefit. 

## The convergence – carry, volatility, valuations 

Unusually low forwards boost the case for owning long-dated options. But alone, they are not enough. We look for a framework for assessing the attractiveness of buying long-dated equity options using valuations, volatility and pricing of equity forwards. The attractiveness of buying a long-dated option should be a function of four things. 

**1. Carry (difference between dividend yield and risk-free rate):** As discussed above, the higher the carry, the lower the markets imply the forward price to be. We have discussed this theme of discounted forwards before (see Cross-Asset Dispatches: Revisiting FX-Hedged Yields, October 9, 2018). 

**2. Volatility:** The lower implied volatility is, the cheaper the value of the option. Volatility across a number of markets is very low. 

**3. Valuation:** The cheaper the market is, the more attractive it is to own on a longterm basis. This isn't theoretical; valuation has a powerful relationship with returns, but you need to give it time (see All About Values, February 26, 2017). 

**4. Catalyst:** Is there the potential for change in the market that could give it a boost in the longer term? 

Catalysts are difficult to quantify (we'll return to them later). But the first three are not. We can look at each of carry, volatility and valuation as a percentile relative to history. The higher the carry, the lower the volatility, and the lower the valuation, the better the 'score'. 

On that composite basis, several markets look unusually good to buy calls, whereas S&P 500 looks closer to average today. Investors may have heard this story of long discounted forwards and cheap long-dated calls before but the economics have rarely been better when viewed using this framework.

## What to do? Investment recommendations 

Long-dated options are not for everyone. But for investors with a longer time horizon, and/or looking to simultaneously take advantage of low levels of volatility and historically wide differences between stocks and bonds, we think that buying 5yr calls offers attractive ways to take exposure in the UK, Europe and Japan. 

What about catalysts? Again, these are the hardest element to qualify. But we think that realistic candidates exist across each of these markets.

## **Definition of terms**

Sell/Short: The analyst expects the total or excess return (depending on the nature of the recommendation) of the instrument or issuer that is the subject of the investment recommendation to be negative over the relevant time period. 

Selling protection or Buying Risk: The analyst expects that the price of protection against the event occurring will decrease over the relevant time period. 

Buying protection or Selling Risk: The analyst expects the price of protection against the event occurring will increase over the relevant time period. 

Pay: The analyst expects that over the specified time period the variable rate underlying the swap agreement that is the subject of the investment recommendation will increase.

## Appendix: Strategy Risk Factors 

Buying calls or call spreads: Investors who buy call options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes below the strike price at expiration. Investors who buy call spreads (buy a call and sell a further OTM call) also have a maximum loss of the entire up-front premium paid. The maximum gain from buying call spreads is the difference between the strike prices, less the upfront premium paid. 

Buying puts or put spreads: Investors who buy put options risk loss of the entire premium paid if the underlying security finishes above the strike price at expiration. Investors who buy put spreads (buy a put and sell a further OTM put) also have a maximum loss of the upfront premium paid. The maximum gain from buying put spreads is the difference between the strike prices, less the upfront premium paid. 

Selling calls: Investors who sell covered calls (own the underlying security and sell a call) risk limiting their upside to the strike price plus the upfront premium received and may have their security called away if the security price exceeds the strike price of the short call. Additionally, the investor has full downside exposure that is only partially offset by the upfront premium taken in. Investors short naked calls (i.e. sold calls but don’t hold underlying security) risk unlimited losses of security price less strike price. Investors who sell naked call spreads (i.e. sell a call and buy a farther out-of-the-money call with no underlying security position) have a maximum loss of the difference between the long call strike and the short call strike, less the upfront premium taken in, if the underlying security finishes above the long call strike at expiration. The maximum gain is the upfront premium taken in, if the security finishes below the short call strike at expiration. 

Selling puts: Put sellers commit to buying the underlying security at the strike price in the event the security falls below the strike price. The maximum loss is the full strike price less the premium received for selling the put. Put sellers who are also long a lower dollar-strike put face a maximum loss of the difference between the long and short put strikes less the options premium received. 

Buying strangles: The maximum loss is the entire premium paid (put + call), if the security finishes between the put strike and the call strike at expiration. 

Selling strangles or straddles: Investors who are long a security and short a strangle or straddle risk capping their upside in the security to the strike price of the call that is sold plus the upfront premium received. Additionally, if the security trades below the strike price of the short put, the investor risks losing the difference between the strike price and the security price (less the value of the premium received) on the short put and will also experience losses in the security position if he owns shares. The maximum potential loss is the full value of the strike price (less the value of the premium received) plus losses on the long security position. Investors who are short naked strangles or straddles have unlimited potential loss since if the security trades above the call strike price, the investor risks losing the difference between the strike price and the security price (less the value of the premium received) on the short call. Additionally, they are obligated to buy the security at the put strike price (less upfront premium received) if the security finishes below the put strike price at expiration. Strangle/straddle sellers risk assignment on short put positions that become in the money. Additionally, they risk having stock called away from short call positions that become in the money.
