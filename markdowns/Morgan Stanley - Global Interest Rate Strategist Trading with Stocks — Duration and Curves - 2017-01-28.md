Digging into company filings from the top 7 life insurance companies in Japan by assets, we put together the data on FX hedge ratios in **Exhibit 10** and **Exhibit 11** . The data suggest that hedge ratios increased dramatically in 2Q16 and did so most notably in euros and dollars. The top 7 lifers in Japan hold 68% of their non-yen assets in US dollars, and 20% of their assets in euros. Hence, the 8pp increase in the US dollar hedge ratio in 2Q16 is notable - even more notable than the 11pp increase in the euro hedge ratio. 

Lifers can increase the hedge ratios on their non-yen bond portfolios in various ways. For example, lifers can add more FX-hedged non-yen bonds to their portfolio than unhedged non-yen bonds, compared to how their previous holdings were split. Lifers can also convert previously unhedged non-yen bonds to hedged non-yen bonds by selling USD/JPY in the forward FX market. Given the decline in USD/JPY during the Tokyo session starting in 2Q16, we think lifers increased their hedge ratios via the later method. 

We note that increasing hedge ratios via selling USD/JPY in the forward FX market would also place downward pressure on the USD/JPY basis swap - which it did during that period in fact. And, given the downward pressure on the USD/JPY basis swap, the cost to hedge out the currency risk in US dollar denominated bonds rose - making currency-hedged investment less attractive. This may explain part of why we have seen muted activity out of Japan so far this year (see **Exhibit 5** again).

## **Beware the inflation base effects** 

Key data releases to watch out for next week are the preliminary January inflation prints. If they continue to surprise on the upside, as they did (in aggregate) for December, then it is likely to add growing conviction to the view that reflation is indeed under way in the eurozone, especially if the core inflation figures are stronger than expected. 

The market has already revised up its inflation pricing for the next 12 months from where it was at the beginning of November, but it still sees the spike in inflation from Feb to Apr-17 as a transitory headline shock (see **Exhibit 14** ). By year-end inflation is priced to drift back below 1.40% Y/Y. If this inflation profile is realised, it will be difficult for the ECB to taper purchases in early 2018, as the market increasingly prices as likely. But if core inflation is seen to be picking up, then investors (and the ECB) are likely to have more conviction in the inflation recovery being sustainable. 

## **Why we think the real yield curve is too steep** 

Beyond the next 12 months, the market prices a very subdued pace of inflation normalisation. The forward HICPxT inflation swap curve prices the Y/Y inflation rate not rising above 1.50% for another five years, and taking 10 years to get above 2%. However, the Eonia curve implies the market expects the ECB to raise rates more than 100bp over the next five years, and then around a further 100bp over the subsequent five years (see **Exhibit 15** ). 

We find this scenario strange: the ECB is priced to start normalising monetary policy well before inflation has risen anywhere close to its inflation target. It then fails to accelerate its tightening even as inflation overshoots its mandate. Further, if we take the 9y1y Eonia forward rate as the market's pricing of the ECB's terminal rate, it suggests the ECB is never expected to fully normalise monetary policy: the policy rate doesn't rise above 2% even when inflation is printing above 2%, meaning even the longest-dated forward real yields are negative (in both bonds and swaps). 

Combining these two views suggests the real yield curve is too steep: 5y real yields ought to be lower, either through nominal rates falling or front-end breakevens widening; in longer tenors, too much inflation is priced in for the level of nominal rates, so either nominal rates should be higher or the forward breakevens need to be lower. In both swaps and bonds (i.e., DBRIs), the entire real yield term structure out to 30y is in negative territory (see **Exhibit 16** ).

## **Is the curve still pricing QE?** 

A popular "QE trade" was to be long the 20y sector on the curve, given Mediums were more heavily bought than other sectors relative to the amount of outstanding eligible paper, and indeed the sector richened into QE. ## **What does Italian Constitutional Court ruling mean?** 

The Italian Constitutional Court has decided to eliminate the two-round voting system in the electoral law, though they leave the majority premium of 40%, shifting from the current two-round system with a majority premium that was implemented in 2015. Eliminating both the two-round system and the majority premium would have been bullish for those wanting reform, while these smaller tweaks only favour coalitions of mainstream parties to the extent that no party gets more than 40% of the total vote and a party is therefore incentivised to join forces with other parties. ## **Why did the BoJ skip the JGB purchase of the 1y-5y sector?** 

Supply/demand in the 1y–5y sector has been extremely tight, with the BoJ owning some 60-70% of almost all (near-current) off-the-run JS (5y) issues (see **Exhibit 43** ). Moreover, domestic banks remain reluctant to sell older bonds issued with higher coupons due to the carry income. This effectively means that the central bank is running out of short-to-medium-term JGBs to buy. 

The BoJ's previous focus on the short-to-medium-term sector should be attributed to (1) a belief that this sector is of greater relevance to real economic activity than long-term interest rates and (2) concerns that a rise in short-to-medium-term rates could generate significant upward pressure on the entire yield curve. 

20 

**Global Interest Rate Strategist** MORGAN STANLEY RESEARCH 

However, with real economic indicators starting to improve and yields seemingly kept in check by the YCC framework, the BoJ probably now sees somewhat less of an incentive to keep absorbing so much of the shortto-medium-term sector, particularly given that issuance of 2y and 5y JGBs is set to be reduced when FY2017 gets under way in April (see **Exhibit 44** ). We therefore believe that the decision to cut the level of 1y–5y purchases should not necessarily be considered all that surprising in and of itself. 

However, we were taken aback by both the timing and the fact that an entire operation was to be skipped. One possible explanation is that the BoJ has been facing significant supply shortages, with the repo market having tightened dramatically for new 5y JGBs and the central bank making sizeable offers at "Securities lending facility (Sales of JGBs under repurchase agreements)" operations on a daily basis ahead of the 5y auction on January 19. 

It now remains to be seen whether the switch to five operations per month will be "permanent" (at least for the time being) or temporary. If the former, then supply/demand in the 1y–5y sector is liable to slacken quite significantly as a result of the BoJ's purchases being reduced by some ¥10 trillion per year. There is also a risk that market participants might interpret the move as fully fledged tapering and start pushing the YCC framework towards breaking point. The small increase in 5y-10y sector on Friday might demonstrate the BoJ's strong commitment to the long-term yield target. 

Economic conditions have indeed taken a turn for the better of late, but the inflation rate remains in negative territory. We thus find it hard to imagine that the BoJ will continue to reduce its purchases so drastically as to have a major impact on the yield curve. We believe that this month's "skipped" operation may prove to be a one-off (although there is of course little question that the BoJ is looking to scale back its operations in the longer term).

## **Border adjustability and its impact on inflation** 

One key market-impacting news over the week from the new administration was the growing possibility of a border adjustability tax (BAT). The floated possibility of taxing imports from Mexico with a 20% 'import tax' as a way to fund a border wall suggested that a border adjustability tax could be in the pipeline. In this report, we take a quick look how this tax could work and impact breakeven markets. 

**What is the border-adjustability tax ?** The House Republican tax proposal blueprint converts the corporate tax system into a destination-based tax system where cash flows are taxed based on the region of consumptions. This means that export revenues are not taxed while imports are not allowed for cost deductions - effectively levying an import tax while creating an export subsidy. The details of the tax rates are not known yet, but the proposal includes a 20% corporate tax rate that is border adjusted. Our FX and Equity strategists wrote a **detailed piece** on the proposal to "border adjust" the corporate income tax and its impact on FX. 

**What is the theoretical impact of BAT?** Purely from a theoretical perspective, the trade weighted dollar should adjust by 25%, to counter a 20% subsidy for exports and a 20% effective tax for imports. That would leave the overall impact neutral on prices and the flow of goods. However our FX strategists, **do not expect** a full offset because (1) the economic theory is based on purchasing power parity (PPP) and PPP does not always assert itself over a short term, (2) there is import content in exports, (3) there are risks of intervention from EM central banks (China and Mexico in particular) and (4) and there is possibility of a WTO response. 

**How could BAT raise US inflation?** Given the mitigating factors above, our FX strategists suggest that we could see only a 10-15% appreciation of the trade weighted dollar versus a theoretical 25% needed to fully offset the impact of the tax change. That means the resultant tax impact will not be neutral and will favor US exporters over importers. 

Therefore, importers of goods and services will effectively pay a 20% tariff on their imports, which they can either fully pass on to the end users or absorb some of the impact. Net, net, imported goods will be more expensive and will directly impact core goods inflation in the CPI basket significantly. The tariff will also be more punitive on importers because a high fraction of imports are invoiced in dollars and thus importers will not be able to take advantage of the dollar offset to the tax.

## **New Medicare law for physicians?** 

Legislation has been proposed by Republican lawmakers including the nominee for Health and Human Services secretary, Tom Price, on allowing patients to enter into 'private contracts' with doctors as a part of Medicare. The proposed measure is a physicianfriendly measure that would allow physicians to privately charge patients more than the Medicarerecommended prices for their services. 

Such a law could lead to higher out-of-pocket costs for physician visits for Medicare patients, and could also lead to higher costs of end-services provided by these physicians. So far, a lot of the proposals on healthcare reform have been more geared toward higher out-of-pocket costs for patients, which only results in higher CPI weights for such services over time ( see **Exhibit 51** ). However, this proposal could also result in higher endservice costs being measured in CPI and PCE. On the other hand, it can be argued that this would lead to more physicians becoming a part of the Medicare network and it could lower private contracting costs due to competition. 

Recall that physician-cost inflation in Medicare has been dropping since the passage of the "docfix law" in April 2015. However, the 'private contract' proposal, should it become law, could reverse the recent downward pull in PCE inflation for physician services.

## **Increased floater issuance** 

The increase in floating rate debt issuance is driven by two main regulatory changes: 

- Total loss-absorbing capacity (TLAC) which comes into full effect on January 1, 2019 

- Enhanced supplementary leverage ratio (SLR) which comes into full effect on January 1, 2018 

TLAC requires a minimum amount of capital and subordinate long-term debt that can be used to bail-in a failing bank. In particular, it penalizes short-term debt through a 50% haircut on debt with 1 to 2 years of maturity and a 100% haircut on debt maturing in less than 1 year. As a result, banks have an incentive to issue debt callable 1 to 2 years before maturity to reduce short term debt on their books. 

For example, instead of issuing a bullet fixed coupon 10-year bond, a bank would issue a 10-year bond callable in 9 years. The 9 years of fixed rate payment can then be hedged through receiving fixed in the swap market. However, the 9-year swap would not be considered a hedge for accounting purposes under current GAAP. Floating rate notes with an embed call solve that problem, since they eliminate the need to hedge interest rate exposure in the swap market.

## **Negative convexity hedging** 

Prior to the financial crisis of 2008 swap spreads were usually positively correlated with the direction of interest rates. The correlation was largely attributed to MBS portfolios hedging the impact of negative convexity through paying or receiving in swaps as rates sell off or rally, respectively. 

That correlation almost disappeared in the post-crisis world, in part because the Federal Reserve now holds one third of outstanding MBS which removes a large chunk of hedging demand. Additionally, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac reduced the size of their MBS portfolios following the crisis as they went into conservatorship. 

The positive correlation between spreads and rates persists during New York trading hours, however (8:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. EST) (see **Exhibit 71** ). A possible explanation for that is hedging by liability driven investors (LDIs). LDI portfolios exhibit negative convexity due to the optionality embedded in insurance products that they sell as well as the fact that the duration of their liabilities is typically longer than that of their assets.

Our BMIs have turned negative on Bunds, gilts, and JGBs into the 40y JGB and gilt auctions. We suggest investors play for steeper curves, conditionally in the US, but outright in Germany and the UK. Our iBMIs are neutral on global breakevens now, and we favor TIPS 5s30s breakeven curve steepeners. 

## **ECB Watch: Looking Through** 

## _19 Jan 2017_ 

The rise in inflation is a welcome development, but it's mostly due to oil-related base effects. As such, the ECB isn't yet ready to shift stance. To do so, the Bank would have to see a more sustained and sustainable pickup in HICP. We're not there yet. 

## **UK Economics & Strategy: Inflation: Upward Surprise, Exaggerated by Air Fares** 

## _17 Jan 2017_ 

December inflation rose more than expected. Pipeline pressure should help to push CPI inflation above 2.0%Y by February. In breakevens, we still favour steepeners. 

## **Global Interest Rate Strategy: Digging the Data** 

## _13 Jan 2017_ 

Our BMIs are neutral on global duration while our iBMIs lean long TIPS and JGBi breakevens. Economic data and equity strength point to higher rates, but policy questions remain. Instead of outright plays, we focus on yield curves, where we maintain a focus on steeper curves in Germany and in the US. 

## **US Economics & Rates Strategy: How to Get to a March Hike** 

## _7 Jan 2017_ 

We expect the Fed to next hike rates at its September meeting as growth inflects with fiscal policy taking hold and inflation pressures build. A more meaningful drop in the unemployment rate, higher inflation, and a lesstolerant Fed than we expect could push the Fed to hike early.
